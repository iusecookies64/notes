## 2.2 Definition and Intuition

#### Definition of Conditional Probability:

If A and B are events with $P(B) > 0$, then the conditional probability of A given B, denoted by $P(A|B)$, is defined as
$$
P(A|B) = \frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}
$$

#### Common Misconceptions:

1. $A|B$ is not any new kind of event made from combining from A and B, it is simply the event A, but with having the information that B happened. This changes the probability of whether A happened of not, but it's still the even A.

2. $P(A)$ is the *prior* probability of A when we had no evidence. $P(A|B)$ is the *posterior* probability of A i.e. the updated probability of A after having the evidence that B occurred.

3. Often people confuse $P(A|B) = P(B|A)$, but that is rarely the case, this is know as the **Prosecutor's Fallacy** which we will talk about more in section 2.8.

4. $P(A|B)$ or $P(B|A)$ does not imply that events happened in a fixed chronological order, it simply says given information that an event $B$ occurred what is the probability that $A$ occurred or will occur, hence it works irrespective of the chronological order in which the events occur.

#### Intuition 1 (Pebble World)

Consider a sample space $S$ containing finite set of possible outcomes and A and B are event in this subspace. Initially when we have no information, the state is as follows,

1. The sum of mass of each outcome $= 1$.

2. $P(A)$ includes all of the possible outcomes in set $A$.

3. Let $E_{1}, E_{2}, \dots, E_{n}$ be the favorable to the event $B$.
![](Pasted%20image%2020260820101318.png)
After the event B occurred, the set of possible outcomes reduces to the set $B$. In a way it is now our new sample space, and probability of any outcome outside of this is simply 0.

The masses of the outcomes in $B$ are still according to $S$ and hence cannot be interpreted as probabilities because they don' t add up to $1$.

* What we have $P(E_{1}) + P(E_{2}) + \dots + P(E_{n}) = P(B)$.

* What we want $P(E_{1}|B) + P(E_{2}|B) + \dots + P(E_{n}|B) = 1$, while preserving relative masses of $E_{i}$'s.

It is not hard to see that the best way to do this is re-normalize the original probabilities is to simply divide them by $P(B)$, it makes them sum to $1$ and preserves the relative mass of the events. Hence we have
$$P(E_{i}|B) = \frac{P(E)}{P(B)}$$
Now, we need to find the updated probability of event $A$. Let $E_{1}^{'}, E_{2}^{'}, \dots, E_{m}^{'}$ be the events in the set $A\cap B$. We only care about these as after knowing that $B$ occurred, these are the only outcomes favorable to $A$. Hence we have,
$$
\begin{align}
P(A|B)  & = \sum_{i=1}^{m} P(E_{i}^{'}|B) \\
 &  = \sum_{i=1}^{m} \frac{P(E_{i}^{'})}{P(B)} \\
 &  = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m} P(E_{i}^{'})}{P(B)} \\
 &  = \frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}
\end{align}
$$

#### Intuition 2 (Frquentist World)

In a frequentist world, we know that the probability of an outcome is the frequency of that outcome divided by the number of times the experiment is repeated.

* $n$: number of times the experiment was repeated.
* $n_{A}$: number of times $A$ occurred.
* $n_{B}$: number of times B occurred.
* $n_{AB}$: number of times $A$ and $B$ both occurred.

Now we know that $P(A) = \frac{n_{A}}{n}$, $P(B) = \frac{n_{B}}{n}$ and $P(A\cap B) = \frac{n_{AB}}{n}$.

Now, in this world, $P(A|B)$ will be the proportion of times $A$ occurred alongside $B$. Hence we have,
$$P(A|B) = \frac{n_{AB}}{n_{B}} = \frac{\frac{n_{AB}}{n}}{\frac{n_{B}}{n}} = \frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}$$

## 2.3 Baye's Rule and The Law of Total Probability

#### Probability of Intersection of Events

**Theorem:** For any events $A$ and $B$ with positive probabilities, we have
$$
P(A\cap B) = P(A|B) \cdot P(B) = P(B|A) \cdot P(A)
$$
**Proof:** This can be easily obtained from the definition of conditional probability.

**Theorem (Probability of Intersection of $n$ Events):** For any events $A_{1}, A_{2}, \dots, A_{n}$ with $P(A_{i}) > 0 \text{ } \forall \  i = 0, 1, \dots, n$, we have
$$
P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_{i} \right) = P(A_{1}) \cdot P(A_{2}|A_{1}) \cdot P(A_{3}|A_{1}, A_{2}) \dots P(A_{n}|A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{n-1})
$$
**Proof:** Here, we just have to use the previous theorem repeatedly each time isolating one event. First we club events $A_{1}, A_{2}, \dots, A_{n-1}$ as a single event and use above theorem. Then we will club $A_{1}, A_{2}, \dots, A_{n-2}$ and so on.
$$
\begin{align}
P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n}A_{i} \right)  & = P(A_{1},A_{2},\dots, A_{n-1})\cdot P(A_{n}|A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{n-1}) \\
 & = P(A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{n-2}) \cdot P(A_{n-1}|A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{n-2})\cdot P(A_{n-1}|A_{1}, \dots, A_{n-1}) \\
 & \vdots \\
 & = P(A_{1})\cdot P(A_{2}|A_{1})\cdot P(A_{3}|A_{1},A_{2})\dots P(A_{n-1}|A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{n-1})
\end{align}
$$

>[!Note]
>The above theorem can be proved for any order of the $n$ events. Hence we can use any order we want, something that would make the calculations easier and the answer will always be the same.

#### Bayes Rule

**Theorem:** The bayes rule helps us find the conditional probability of $A|B$  in terms of $B|A$.
$$
P(A|B) = \frac{P(B|A)\cdot P(A)}{P(B)}
$$
**Proof:** Now this theorem again can be solved with the theorem about intersection of events,
$$
P(A,B) = P(A|B) \cdot P(B) = P(B|A) \cdot P(A) \implies P(A|B) = \frac{P(B|A)\cdot P(A)}{P(B)}
$$
#### Odds Of An Event

**Definition:** The odds of an event $A$ are defined as,
$$
odds(A) = \frac{P(A)}{P(A^{c})} = \frac{P(A)}{1 - P(A)}
$$
Is say probability of $P(A) = \frac{2}{3}$, then the odds are $odds(A) = \frac{\frac{2}{3}}{\frac{1}{3}} = \frac{2}{1}$. We say that the odds in favor of A are $2:1$. Or we can also say that the odds against $A$ are $1:2$.

* Odds in favor of $A = odds(A)$
* Odds against $A = odds(A^{c})$

**Definition (Odds from Bayes Rule):** For any events $A$ and $B$ with positive probabilities, the odds of $A$ after conditioning on $B$ are,
$$
odds(A|B) = \frac{P(A|B)}{P(A^{c}|B)} = \frac{P(B|A)\cdot P(A)}{P(B|A^{c})\cdot P(A^{c})} = \frac{P(B|A)}{P(B|A^{c})}\cdot \text{Previous Odds}
$$

#### Law Of Total Probability (LOTP)

**Theorem:** Let $A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{n}$ be a partition of the sample space $S$ (i.e. the $A_{i}$'s are disjoint and $\cup A_{i} = S$) with $P(A_{i}) > 0 \ \forall \ i = 1, 2, \dots, n$. Then, for any event $B$ we have
$$
\begin{align}
P(B) & = P(B|A_{1})\cdot P(A_{1}) + P(B|A_{2})\cdot P(A_{2}) + \dots + P(B|A_{n})\cdot P(A_{n}) \\
 &  = \sum_{i=1}^{n}P(B|A_{i})\cdot P(A_{i})
\end{align}
$$
**Proof:**
$$
\begin{align}
P(B) &  = P(B\cap S)  \\
&  = P(B\cap(A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup\dots A_{n})) \\
&  = P((B\cap A_{1})\cup(B\cap A_{2})\dots\cup(B\cap A_{n})) \\
&  = P(B\cap A_{1}) + P(B\cap A_{2}) + \dots + P(B\cap A_{n}) \\
&  = P(B|A_{1})\cdot P(A_{1}) + \dots + P(B|A_{n}) \cdot P(A_{n})
\end{align}
$$
![](Pasted%20image%2020260822093327.png)

>[!Note]
>This law is very powerful, it let's us divide the bigger problem of finding the probability of an event in terms of smaller probabilities.

## 2.4 Conditional Probabilities are Probabilities

When conditioning on an event $E$, we update our beliefs according to this new information. Before knowing $E$ occurred, we were in the universe $S$ where all outcomes were possible. After knowing that $E$ occurred, we are effectively now in a new universe where only the outcomes inside of $E$ can occur. But the laws of probability in this new universe are the same.

**Theorem:** Probabilities laws still work when conditioned on an event $E$,

**(a)** $P(S|E) = 1$ and $P(\phi|E) = 0$

**(b)** If $A_{1}, A_{2}, \dots, A_{n}$ are disjoint events, then
$$
P(A_{1}\cup\dots\cup A_{n}|E) = P(A_{1}|E) + P(A_{2}|E) + \dots + P(A_{n}|E)
$$
**(c)** $P(A^{c}|E) = 1 - P(A|E)$

**(d)** $P(A\cup B|E) = P(A|E) + P(B|E) - P(A,B|E)$

**Proof:**

**(a)** $P(S|E) = \frac{P(S\cap E)}{P(E)} = \frac{P(E)}{P(E)} = 1$. Similarly the other can be proved as well.

**(b)** Here we will use the set property that $(A\cup B)\cap C = (A\cap C)\cup(B\cap C)$.

$$
   \begin{align}
   P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}|E \right)  & = \frac{P((A_{1}\cup A_{2}\dots\cup A_{n})\cap E)}{P(E)} \\
    & = \frac{P((A_{1}\cap E)\cup(A_{2}\cap E)\dots\cup(A_{n}\cap E))}{P(E)} \\
	& = \frac{P(A_{1}\cap E) + P(A_{2}\cap E) + \dots + P(A_{n}\cap E)}{P(E)} \\
	& = P(A_{1}|E) + P(A_{2}|E) + \dots + P(A_{n}|E)
   \end{align}
   $$
**(c)** 
$$
\begin{align}
   P(A\cup A^{c}|E)  & = P(S|E) \\
   P(A|E) + P(A^{c}|E)  & = 1 \\
   P(A^{c}|E) &  = 1 - P(A|E)
\end{align}
   $$
**(d)** 
$$
\begin{align}
   P(A\cup B|E) &  = \frac{P((A\cup B)\cap E)}{P(E)} \\
    & = \frac{P((A\cap E)\cup(B\cap E))}{P(E)} \\
	& = \frac{P(A\cap E) + P(B\cap E) - P((A\cap E)\cap(B\cap E))}{P(E)} \\
	& = P(A|E) + P(B|E) - P(A,B|E)
\end{align}
   $$

>[!Note] All Probabilities are Conditional
>We have seen that conditional probabilities are just probabilities, they behave in exactly the same way. But, all probabilities are conditional, since we always have some base level conditions/assumptions about every experiment.
>
>For example consider the experiment of tossing a coin, the probability of heads we say is $\frac{1}{2}$ but that is only true given the assumption that the coin is fair. We are not writing explicitly that, but we are still assuming that.

>[!Quote]
>Conditional probabilities are probabilities and all probabilities are conditional

#### Intersection of Events With Extra Conditioning

**Theorem:** Let $A,B,E$ be events with positive probabilities and $P(A,E),P(B,E) > 0$, then we have,
$$
P(A,B|E) = P(A|B,E)\cdot P(B|E) = P(B|A,E)\cdot P(A|E)
$$
**Proof:** We will only prove the first one, the other (right most) can be proved in a similar way,
$$
\begin{align}
P(A,B|E) &  = \frac{P(A\cap B\cap E)}{P(E)} \\
 & = \frac{P(A\cap B\cap E)}{P(E)}\cdot \frac{P(B\cap E)}{P(B\cap E)} \\
 & = \frac{P(A,B,E)}{P(B,E)}\cdot \frac{P(B,E)}{P(E)} \\
 & = P(A|B,E)\cdot P(B|E)
\end{align}
$$

#### Bayes Rule With Extra Conditioning

**Theorem:** Provided that $P(A,E), P(B,E) > 0$ we have,
$$
P(A|B,E) = \frac{P(B|A,E)\cdot P(A|E)}{P(B|E)}
$$
**Proof:** Can be proved very easily using the previous theorem.

#### LOTP With Extra Conditioning

**Theorem:** Let $A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{n}$ be a partition of $S$. Provided that $P(A_{i},E) > 0$ for all, we have
$$
P(B|E) = P(B|A_{1},E)\cdot P(A_{1}|E) + P(B|A_{2},E)\cdot P(A_{2}|E) + \dots + P(B|A_{n},E)\cdot P(A_{n}|E)
$$
**Proof:**

$$
\begin{align}
P(B|E) & = \frac{P(B\cap E)}{P(E)} \tag{1} \\
 & = \frac{P(B\cap E\cap S)}{P(E)} \tag{2}\\
 & = \frac{P(B\cap E\cap A_{1}) + P(B\cap E\cap A_{2}) + \dots + P(B\cap E\cap A_{n})}{P(E)} \tag{3}\\
 & = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{P(B\cap E\cap A_{i})}{P(E)}\cdot \frac{P(A_{i}\cap E)}{P(E)} \tag{4}\\
 & = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{P(B,E,A_{i})}{P(A_{i},E)}\cdot \frac{P(A_{i},E)}{P(E)} \tag{5}\\
 & = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(B|A_{i},E)\cdot P(A_{i}|E) \tag{6}
\end{align}
$$

>[!Note] What If $P(A_{i},E) = 0$?
>If the $P(A_{i},E) = 0$ for one or more events in the partition, then we can still use the theorem. In the final sum, only those $A_{i}$'s will remain whose $P(A_{i},E) > 0$, as in the step $3$ all events such that $P(A_{i},E) = 0$ will have $P(B,A_{i},E) = 0$ and hence will simply vanish.

>[!Note]
>The above note allows us to use this theorem with a partition of $E$ instead of a partition of $S$. This is more useful as many time when we are already conditioning on $E$ we want to calculate for sub cases inside of $E$ only.

#### Three Ways Of $P(A|B,C)$

1. $P(A|B,C) = \frac{P(A,B,C)}{P(B,C)}$

2. $P(A|B,C) = \frac{P(B|A,C)\cdot P(A|C)}{P(B|C)}$

3. $P(A|B,C) = \frac{P(C|A,B)\cdot P(A|B)}{P(C|B)}$

It is both challenging and powerful that we have variety of way to approach this kind of conditioning problem.

## 2.5 Independence Of Events

A situation where events provide no information about each other is called Independence.

>[!Quote] Definition
>Events $A$ and $B$ are independent if,
>$$P(A\cap B) = P(A)\cdot P(B)$$
>If $P(A) > 0$ and $P(B) > 0$, then this is equivalent to
>$$P(A|B) = P(A) \text{ and } P(B|A) = P(B)$$

##### Common Pitfalls

1. Independence is different from disjointness, in fact it is the opposite of disjointness as when events $A$ and $B$ are disjoint, then knowing one of them occurred tells us that the other one didn't occur.

2. Independence is a symmetric relation i.e. if $A$ is independent of $B$, then $B$ is also independent of $A$.

3. The independent definition is two ways i.e. if $A$ and $B$ are independent, then $P(A\cap B) = P(A)\cdot P(B)$ and if we have $P(A\cap B) = P(A)\cdot P(B)$ then $A$ and $B$ are independent.

**Proposition:** If $A$ and $B$ are independent, then $(A, B^{c})$, $(A^{c}, B)$, and $(A^{c}, B^{c})$ are also independent.

**Proof:**
$$
\begin{align}
P(A\cap B^{c}) &  = P(A) - P(A\cap B) = P(A) - P(A)\cdot P(B) \\
 & = P(A)\cdot (1-P(B)) = P(A)\cdot P(B^{c})
\end{align}
$$

Similarly, we can prove $P(A^{c}, B)$. For $P(A^{c}, B^{c})$ we have,
$$
\begin{align}
P(A^{c}\cap B^{c}) &  = P((A\cup B)^{c}) = 1 - P(A\cup B) \\
 & = 1 - P(A) - P(B) + P(A\cap B) = (1-P(A)) - P(B)(1-P(A)) \\
 & = (1-P(A)) \cdot (1-P(B)) = P(A^{c})\cdot P(B^{c})
\end{align}
$$

### Independence Of 3 Events

**Definition:** Events $A$, $B$ and $C$ are said to be independent if all of the following equations hold
$$
\begin{align}
 P(A,B)  & = P(A)\cdot P(B) \\
 P(B,C)  & = P(B)\cdot P(C) \\
 P(A,C)  & = P(A)\cdot P(C) \\
 P(A,B,C)  & = P(A)\cdot P(B)\cdot P(C)
\end{align}
$$

**Proof:** Although this is not a theorem, we can still prove this for better intuition. We can prove why we need all of them through counter cases.

* *Pairwise Independent:* Consider the experiment of tossing a fair coin two times, and consider the events,
	
	$A$: getting heads on the first toss ($P(A) = \frac{1}{2}$),
	B: getting heads on the second toss ($P(B) = \frac{1}{2}$),
	C: getting the same output both times ($P(C) = \frac{1}{2}$)
	
	Then the pairwise probabilities are,
	
	$P(A,B) = \frac{1}{4} = P(A)\cdot P(B)$
	$P(A,C) = \frac{1}{4} = P(A)\cdot P(C)$
	$P(B,C) = \frac{1}{4} = P(B)\cdot P(C)$
	
	But, when all of the events are taken together, we get
	
	$P(A,B,C) = P(A,B) = \frac{1}{4} \neq P(A)\cdot P(B)\cdot P(C)$
	
* *Triplet Works:* Consider the events $A$, $B$ and $C$ such that $A = \phi$, then we have $P(A,B,C) = 0$ for any events $B$ and $C$. But this does not mean that we will get $P(B\cap C) = P(B)\cdot P(C)$ for them.

Hence, just having pairwise independence is not enough and so is all three taken at a time. We need both pairwise independence and all three taken at a time.

>[!Note] Independence Of Many Events
If we have $n$ events, then for them to be independent we require all subsets of these events to be independent i.e. all pairs, all triplets, all quadruplets, all quintuplets, and so on.

### Conditional Independence

**Definition:** Events $A$ and $B$ are said to be conditionally independent given event $E$ if,
$$
P(A\cap B|E) = P(A|E)\cdot P(B|E)
$$
Using the conditional bayes theorem, we can get the following formulas from the above definition.
$$
\begin{align}
P(A\cap B|E) &  = P(A|B,E)\cdot P(B|E) \\
P(A|E)\cdot P(B|E)  & = P(A|B,E)\cdot P(B|E) \\
P(A|E)  & = P(A|B,E) \\
\implies P(A|B,E) &  = P(A|E)
\end{align}
$$
Similarly, we can do for $B$ also and get,
$$
P(B|A,E) = P(B|E)
$$

#### Conditional Independence Doesn't Imply Independence

If $A$ and $B$ are conditionally independent, that doesn't mean that are independent in general. The below example proves this by showing the counter case is possible.

**Example:** Consider a coin tossing experiment (Random Coin) where first we choose randomly from two coins one of which is fair and other one is not fair, it lands heads with probability $\frac{3}{4}$. Let $F$ be the event of choosing a fair coin, then given $F$ the probability of heads is $\frac{1}{2}$ in every toss independent of previous tosses. Also given $F^{c}$, each toss still independently has probability of heads as $\frac{3}{4}$. But the general probability of heads on $kth$ toss is dependent on the outcomes of previous tosses, this is because each outcome gives more information about whether the coin chosen is fair or not fair. We will discuss the numbers in examples section later.

#### Independence Doesn't Imply Conditional Independence

If $A$ and $B$ are independent, that doesn't mean that they are conditionally independent too. Again the below example proves this,

**Example:** Again consider a coin tossing experiment with a fair coin. Let $H_{1}$ be the event of getting heads on first toss and $H_{2}$ be for getting heads on second toss. We know that $H_{1}$ and $H_{2}$ are independent. But consider the event $A$ to be getting different results in each of the first two toss, then we have,$P(H_{1}|A) = \frac{1}{2}$ and $P(H_{2}|A) = \frac{1}{2}$. But,
$$P(H_{1},H_{2}|A) = 0 \neq P(H_{1}|A)\cdot P(H_{2}|A) = \frac{1}{2}\cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}$$

#### Conditional Independence Given $E$ vs $E^{c}$

If $A$ and $B$ are conditionally independent given $E$, then it doesn't imply that they are independent given $E^{c}$. We can prove this with a counter example, 

**Example:** Consider two schools $A$ and $B$ with different grading system. School $A$ gives grades based on your performance in the exam and school $B$ gives grades randomly independent of your performance in the exam. Let $G$ be the event that you get good grades, and $W$ be the event that you worked hard (which implies good performance in exam).Let $A$ be the event that the chosen student is from the school $A$ and same for $B$. Here we assume that these are the only schools under consideration, hence $B^{c} = A$, then we have $P(G|W,B) = P(G|B)$ i.e. $G$ and $W$ are independent given student is from school $B$. But $P(G|W,B^{c}) = P(G|W,A)$ in this case $G$ and $W$ are not independent since working hard improves the probability of getting a good grade.

#### Information Specificity (or Symmetry Breaking)

**The Two-Children Problem**

A family has two children. Assume standard simplifying assumptions: each child is equally likely to be a boy or a girl ($P(\text{Boy}) = P(\text{Girl}) = 1/2$), gender is binary, the genders of the children are independent, and seasons are equally likely and independent of gender.

Let the sample space for the children in order of age (Elder, Younger) be:
$$S = \{GG, GB, BG, BB\}$$
where each configuration has a prior probability of $1/4$.

Find the conditional probability that **both children are girls** under each of the following scenarios:

**(a)** You are told the older child is a girl.
   
**(b)** You are told that at least one of the children is a girl.
   
**(c)** You randomly meet one of the two children (equally likely to meet either) and observe that she is a girl.

**(d)** You are told that at least one child is a girl who was born in winter.

**Solution:**

**(a)** Conditioning Event $A = \{GG, GB\} \implies P(A) = 1/2$, Target Event $\{GG\}$.

$$P(GG \mid \text{elder is } G) = \frac{P(GG)}{P(A)} = \frac{1/4}{1/2} = \frac{1}{2}$$

**(b)** Conditioning Event $B = \{GG, GB, BG\} \implies P(B) = 3/4$, Target Event $\{GG\}$.

$$P(GG \mid \text{at least one } G) = \frac{P(GG)}{P(B)} = \frac{1/4}{3/4} = \frac{1}{3}$$

**(c)** Let $M_G$ be the event that you meet a girl

$$
\begin{align}
P(M_G)  & = P(M_G \mid GG)P(GG) + P(M_G \mid GB)P(GB) + P(M_G \mid BG)P(BG) + P(M_G \mid BB)P(BB) \\
& = (1)\left(\frac{1}{4}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{4}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{4}\right) + (0)\left(\frac{1}{4}\right) = \frac{1}{2}
\end{align}
$$

Since meeting a girl given $GG$ is guaranteed, $P(GG \cap M_G) = P(GG) = 1/4$. So the probability of $\{GG\}$ given we meet a girl is,

$$P(GG \mid M_G) = \frac{P(GG \cap M_G)}{P(M_G)} = \frac{1/4}{1/2} = \frac{1}{2}$$

**(d)** Probability a specific child is a girl born in winter: $p = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{8}$.
$$
\begin{align}
P(\text{at least one winter girl}) &  = 1 - P(\text{neither child is a winter girl})  \\
 & = 1 - \left(1 - \frac{1}{8}\right)^2 \\
 & = 1 - \left(\frac{7}{8}\right)^2  \\
 & = \frac{15}{64}
\end{align}
$$
	**Numerator:** $P(GG \cap \text{at least one winter girl}) = P(GG) \times P(\text{at least one winter child among 2 girls})$
$$= \frac{1}{4} \times \left(1 - \left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^2\right) = \frac{1}{4} \times \frac{7}{16} = \frac{7}{64}$$
    **Calculation:**
$$P(GG \mid \text{at least one winter girl}) = \frac{7/64}{15/64} = \frac{7}{15}$$

The phenomenon demonstrated across these variations is known as **information specificity** (or **symmetry breaking**) in conditional probability. It illustrates how conditioning on a specific, seemingly irrelevant attribute shifts the probability from $\frac{1}{3}$ up toward $\frac{1}{2}$.

##### The Mechanism: "Double the Chances"

The probability increases because a two-girl family ($GG$) has **twice as many opportunities** to possess a specific trait as a single-girl family ($GB$ or $BG$).

**Generic Condition ("At least one girl"):** Eliminates only $BB$, leaving $GG$, $GB$, and $BG$ as equally likely. Since there are two ways to have one girl ($GB$ and $BG$) and only one way to have two ($GG$), the conditional probability is $\frac{1}{1 + 2} = \frac{1}{3}$.

**Specific Condition ("Born in winter"):** 
	- In a $GG$ family, **both** children are eligible to be the "winter girl" (two chances).
	- In a $GB$ or $BG$ family, **only one** child is eligible (one chance).

Because $GG$ families have double the chances to satisfy the criteria, conditioning on the rare trait biases the sample space toward $GG$ relative to $GB$ and $BG$.

##### The Probability Spectrum

If an attribute has probability $p$ of occurring for a girl, and $A$ be the event that at least one girl with attribute $p$, the general formula is
$$
\begin{align}
P(GG|A) & = \frac{P(A|GG)\cdot P(GG)}{P(A)}  \\
 & = \frac{(1-P(A^{c}|GG))\cdot P(GG)}{P(A|GG)\cdot P(GG) + P(A|BG)\cdot P(BG) + P(A|GB)\cdot P(GB)} \\
 & = \frac{(1-(1-p)^{2})\cdot \frac{1}{4}}{(1 - (1-p)^{2})\cdot \frac{1}{4} + p\cdot \frac{1}{4} + p\cdot \frac{1}{4}} \\
 & = \frac{1 - 1 - p^{2}+2p}{1-1-p^{2}+2p+2p} \\
 & = \frac{2 - p}{4 - p}
\end{align}
$$
As the attribute becomes more specific ($p$ gets smaller), the probability smoothly interpolates from $\frac{1}{3}$ to $\frac{1}{2}$:

- **Generic girl ($p = 1$):** $P = \frac{2 - 1}{4 - 1} = \frac{1}{3} \approx 0.333$
- **Winter girl ($p = \frac{1}{4}$):** $P = \frac{2 - 0.25}{4 - 0.25} = \frac{7}{15} \approx 0.467$
- **Tuesday girl ($p = \frac{1}{7}$):** $P = \frac{2 - 1/7}{4 - 1/7} = \frac{13}{27} \approx 0.481$
- **Ultra-specific trait ($p \to 0$):** $P \to \frac{2 - 0}{4 - 0} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.500$

##### Implicit Identification

Specificity acts as a proxy for **naming a specific child**. Saying "at least one child is a girl" leaves complete ambiguity about _which_ child is being discussed. However, specifying a rare trait ("born on March 31 at 8:20 PM") makes it virtually impossible for both children to share the trait, effectively pinning the description down to one unique individual. Once a specific child is designated, the status of the _other_ child returns to the baseline independent probability of $50\%$ ($\frac{1}{2}$).

#### Random Coin

You have a fair coin and a biased coin that lands heads with probability $\frac{3}{4}$.

**(a)** You pick a coin at random and toss it $3$ times and all of them lands heads. What is probability that the coin you picked was fair.

**(b)** Given that the chosen coin landed heads $3$ times, what is the probability that it will land heads the $4th$ time.

**(c)** If $H_{1}$ and $H_{2}$ are events of getting heads on the first toss and second toss respectively, then prove that $H_{1}$ and $H_{2}$ are not independent in general, but given $F$ (event of choosing a fair coin) or $F^{c}$ they are independent.

**Solution:**
**(a)** Let $3H$ be the event of getting $3$ heads and $F$ be the event of choosing a fair coin. Then we have
$$
\begin{align}
P(F|3H)  & = \frac{P(3H|F)\cdot P(F)}{P(3H)} = \frac{\frac{1}{2^{3}}\cdot \frac{1}{2}}{P(3H|F)\cdot P(F) + P(3H|F^{c})\cdot P(F^{c})} \\
 & = \frac{\frac{1}{2^{4}}}{\frac{1}{2^{4}} + \left( \frac{3}{4} \right)^{3}\cdot \frac{1}{2}} = \frac{1}{1+\frac{27}{16}} = \frac{16}{53} \approx 0.3
\end{align}
$$

>[!Note] Prior vs Posterior
>Saying that $P(A) = 1$ given that $A$ occurred is wrong, as $P(A)$ is the prior probability i.e. before we have any knowledge of what event occurred. What is true is $P(A|A) = 1$ i.e. probability of $A$ given $A$ is $1$.

#### Testing For A Rare Disease

A patient named Fred is tested for a rare disease called conditionitis that affects $1\%$ of the population. The test result is positive i.e. the test claims that Fred has the disease. Let $T$ be the event that he tests positive and $D$ be the event that he actually has the disease. Suppose that the test is $95\%$ accurate. There are many ways to test accuracy, in this it means that $P(T|D) = 0.95$ and $P(T^{c}|D^{c}) = 0.95$. Find the probability that he has the disease given he tested positive.

**Solution:** We need to find $P(D|T)$ for which we will use the bayes rule.
$$
\begin{align}
P(D|T) &  = \frac{P(T|D)\cdot P(D)}{P(T)} = \frac{P(T|D)\cdot P(D)}{P(T|D)\cdot P(D) + P(T|D^{c})\cdot P(D^{c})} \\
 & = \frac{0.95\cdot 0.01}{0.95\cdot 0.01 + 0.05\cdot 0.99} = \frac{95}{95 + 495} = 0.16
\end{align}
$$

**Intuition:** This result surprises a lot of people, since the test seems so accurate and the we tested positive, still calculations show that there is only $16\%$ chance that we have the disease. The fact that people forget is that the disease is pretty rare and that Fred got tested independently i.e. without any symptom or doctor suggestion.

Imagine a population of $10000$ people, only $100$ of them will actually have the disease. Now let's consider that everyone get's tested for the disease, given the accuracy of the disease, $95$ out of $100$ will be tested positive among the people who actually have the disease. Among the people who don't have the disease $5\%$ will still get tested positive i.e. $0.05\cdot 9900 = 495$. Hence, among the people who tested positive, those who actually have the disease are $\frac{95}{95+495} = 0.16$. Hence we get the same result.

>[!Note] Person With Symptom
>In general when a person get tested for a disease, it almost always because a doctor advised them to do so. Now a doctor only advises for a test of a rare disease when they see some known symptoms in the patient. Hence the chances of actually having the disease given we test positive and we have symptoms will be higher. Later we will see what are the benefits of getting a re-test.

#### Six Fingered Man

A crime has been committed and the perpetrator is only one of the $n$ men in the city. Initially all men are equally likely to be a guilty. An eye witness then reports that the perpetrator had 6 fingers is one of his hands.

Let $p_{0}$ be the probability that an innocent man has 6 fingers and $p_{1}$ be the probability that a perpetrator has 6 fingers ($p_{0}<p_{1}<1$).

Rugen lives in the country and is found to have $6$ fingers on his right hand.

**(a)** Given this info, what is the probability that he is guilt.

**(b)** Now suppose all $n$ men in the country get their hands checked and Rugen is the only one with $6$ fingers, then what is the probability that he is guilty now.

**Solution:**

**(a)** Let $G$ be the event that Rugen is guilty, and $S$ be the event that he has 6 fingers. Given that $P(S|G) = p_{1}$ and $P(S|G^{c}) = p_{0}$, then we have
$$
\begin{align}
P(G|S)  & = \frac{P(S|G)\cdot P(G)}{P(S)} = \frac{P(S|G)\cdot P(G)}{P(S|G)\cdot P(G) + P(S|G^{c})\cdot P(G^{c})} \\
 & = \frac{p_{1}\cdot \frac{1}{n}}{p_{1}\cdot \frac{1}{n} + p_{0}\cdot \frac{n-1}{n}} = \frac{p_{1}}{p_{1}+p_{0}(n-1)}
\end{align}
$$
**(b)** Let $A$ be the even that none of the other men have $6$ fingers. Then we have,
$$
P(G|S,A) = \frac{P(S,A|G)\cdot P(G)}{P(S,A)}
$$
* $P(S,A|G) = P(S|G)\cdot P(A|G) =  p_{1}\cdot (1-p_{0})^{n-1}$ since a person having $6$ fingers is completely independent of others having $6$ fingers.
	
* $P(S,A) = P(S,A|G)\cdot P(G) + P(S,A|G^{c})\cdot P(G^{c})$, here the term to focus on is the $P(S, A|G^{c})$. Since Rugen is not guilty, then some other person is guilty, to find this probability we have the following $2$ ways,
	
	* *Symmetry:* Since all of the other men are equally likely to be the perpetrator, by symmetry it doesn't matter who it is, all that matter is that one person is for him the probability of not having $6$ fingers is $(1-p_{1})$, for Rugen the probability of having $6$ fingers is $p_{0}$ and for other $n-2$ men the probability that they don't have 6 fingers is $(1-p_{0})^{n-2}$. Hence we have 
$$
P(S,A|G^{c}) = p_{0}\cdot (1-p_{0})^{n-2}\cdot p_{1}
$$
	* *LOTP:* Let $G_{i}$ be the even that $ith$ man is guilty $i=1,2,3,\dots,n-1$, then we have using the law of total probability,
$$
\begin{align}
P(S,A|G) &  = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} P(S,A|G^{c},M_i) P(M_{i}|G^{c}) \\
 & = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} p_{0}\cdot (1-p_{0})^{c} (1-p_{1}) \cdot \frac{1}{n-1} \\
 & = (n-1)\cdot p_{0}\cdot (1-p_{0})^{c}\cdot (1-p_{1})\cdot \frac{1}{n-1} \\
 & = p_{0}\cdot (1-p_{0})^{c}\cdot (1-p_{1})
\end{align}
$$
	
	Hence, the final answer as,
$$
\begin{align}
P(G|S,A) & = \frac{P(S,A|G)\cdot P(G)}{P(S,A)} \\
& = \frac{p_{1}\cdot (1-p_{0})^{n-1}\cdot \frac{1}{n}}{p_{1}\cdot (1-p_{0})^{n-1}\cdot \frac{1}{n} + p_{0}\cdot(1-p_{0})^{n-2}(1-p_{1})\cdot \frac{n-1}{n}} \\
& = \frac{p_{1}}{p_{1}+p_{0}\cdot \frac{1-p_{1}}{1-p_{0}}\cdot (n-1)}
\end{align}
$$
