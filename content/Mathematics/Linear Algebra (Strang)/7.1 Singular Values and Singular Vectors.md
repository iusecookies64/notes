# Overview: The Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

When we studied symmetric matrices, we saw something wonderful: a symmetric matrix $S$ always has a full set of *orthonormal* eigenvectors, allowing us to diagonalize it cleanly as $S = Q \Lambda Q^T$. But what if our matrix $A$ is not symmetric? Even worse, what if $A$ isn't even square, say an $m \times n$ rectangular matrix that doesn't have eigenvectors at all?

This chapter develops one central idea that applies to **every** matrix $A$, whether square or rectangular: the **Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)**. It is the natural extension of eigenvectors and diagonalization to all matrices—without giving up orthogonality.

Why does the ordinary eigenvector equation $Ax = \lambda x$ fail for an $m \times n$ matrix? Because an $m \times n$ matrix takes input vectors $x$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and maps them to output vectors $Ax$ in a completely different space, $\mathbb{R}^m$. Expecting the output vector to be a scalar multiple of the input vector makes no sense when they don't even have the same number of components! Even for a square unsymmetric matrix, the eigenvectors are generally not orthogonal, and there might not even be enough of them to form a basis.

The key insight of the SVD is to use **two different sets of orthonormal basis vectors**:
- A set of orthonormal **input vectors** (called **right singular vectors**) $v_1, \dots, v_n$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
- A set of orthonormal **output vectors** (called **left singular vectors**) $u_1, \dots, u_m$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$.

How do these vectors connect to the four fundamental subspaces of $A$? If $\operatorname{rank}(A) = r$:
- The first $r$ input vectors $v_1, \dots, v_r$ form an orthonormal basis for the **row space** $C(A^T)$, while the remaining $v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n$ form an orthonormal basis for the **nullspace** $N(A)$.
- The first $r$ output vectors $u_1, \dots, u_r$ form an orthonormal basis for the **column space** $C(A)$, while the remaining $u_{r+1}, \dots, u_m$ form an orthonormal basis for the **left nullspace** $N(A^T)$.

Instead of $Ax = \lambda x$, the matrix $A$ maps each row-space basis vector $v_i$ directly onto a scalar multiple of the corresponding column-space basis vector $u_i$:
$$
Av_i = \sigma_i u_i \quad \text{for } i = 1, \dots, r
$$
where the scalars $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0$ are positive numbers called the **singular values** of $A$, placed on the main diagonal of the $m \times n$ matrix $\Sigma$. Together, the orthogonal matrices $U = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 & \dots & u_m \end{bmatrix}$ and $V = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 & \dots & v_n \end{bmatrix}$ diagonalize $A$:

> [!abstract] Definition: Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)
> Any $m \times n$ matrix $A$ of rank $r$ can be factored as $AV = U\Sigma$, or equivalently:
> $$
> A = U \Sigma V^T = \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^T + \sigma_2 u_2 v_2^T + \dots + \sigma_r u_r v_r^T
> $$
> where $U$ ($m \times m$) and $V$ ($n \times n$) are orthogonal matrices ($U^T U = I_m$, $V^T V = I_n$), and $\Sigma$ ($m \times n$) is a diagonal matrix containing the positive singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0$ in its first $r$ diagonal entries and zeros elsewhere.

Let's unpack the column-times-row form on the right-hand side of the box above. When we multiply $U\Sigma$, its first $r$ nonzero columns are $\sigma_1 u_1, \dots, \sigma_r u_r$ (and the remaining $m - r$ columns are zero). Multiplying $(U\Sigma)$ by the rows $v_i^T$ of $V^T$ expresses $A$ as a sum of $r$ **rank-one matrices** $\sigma_i u_i v_i^T$—completely parallel to the spectral decomposition $S = Q \Lambda Q^T = \lambda_1 q_1 q_1^T + \dots + \lambda_n q_n q_n^T$ for a symmetric matrix $S$.

In fact, if $A$ happens to be a symmetric positive definite matrix $S$, then each $u_i = v_i = q_i$ (the orthonormal eigenvectors of $S$) and each $\sigma_i = \lambda_i$ (the positive eigenvalues of $S$), so $A = U\Sigma V^T$ reduces directly to $S = Q \Lambda Q^T$. For a general matrix $A$, the right singular vectors $v_i$ are orthonormal eigenvectors of $A^T A$, and the left singular vectors $u_i$ are orthonormal eigenvectors of $A A^T$.

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Why the SVD Matters in Data Science & Applications
> Because the singular values are ordered from largest to smallest ($\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0$), the rank-one pieces $\sigma_i u_i v_i^T$ are ordered by importance:
> - **Principal Component Analysis (PCA) & Statistics:** When $A$ is a matrix of centered data, $\frac{1}{n-1} A A^T$ is the **sample covariance matrix**. Its eigenvalues are $\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_r^2$ (up to the $\frac{1}{n-1}$ scaling) and its eigenvectors are the left singular vectors $u_i$, which point along the principal directions of variance in the data.
> - **Image Compression:** A photograph is a matrix of pixel intensities. By decomposing the matrix into its rank-one pieces $\sigma_i u_i v_i^T$ and keeping only the first $k \ll r$ terms with the largest singular values, we capture the dominant visual structure of the image with a fraction of the storage.

# 7.1 Singular Values and Singular Vectors

## 1. From Two Orthogonal Bases to Matrix Factorization

Let's look closely at how we transition from individual vector equations to the full matrix factorization. For any $m \times n$ matrix $A$, we seek an orthonormal set of inputs $v_1, \dots, v_n \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and an orthonormal set of outputs $u_1, \dots, u_m \in \mathbb{R}^m$ such that $A$ maps each input $v_i$ to a scalar multiple of the corresponding output $u_i$:
$$
Av_i = \sigma_i u_i \quad (1 \le i \le r) \qquad \text{and} \qquad Av_i = 0 \quad (i > r)
$$
Why can we package all $n$ of these vector equations into a single matrix equation? If we place the input vectors $v_1, \dots, v_n$ as the columns of an $n \times n$ matrix $V$, then the $i$-th column of the product $AV$ is simply $Av_i$. On the other hand, if we place the output vectors $u_1, \dots, u_m$ as the columns of an $m \times m$ matrix $U$ and form the $m \times n$ diagonal matrix $\Sigma$ with $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r$ on its main diagonal, then the $i$-th column of $U\Sigma$ is $\sigma_i u_i$ (for $i \le r$) and $0$ (for $i > r$). Equating column by column gives:
$$
AV = U\Sigma
$$
Because the columns of $V$ are orthonormal, $V$ is an orthogonal matrix ($V^T V = I_n$, so $V^{-1} = V^T$). Multiplying both sides of $AV = U\Sigma$ on the right by $V^T$ isolates $A$:
$$
A = U \Sigma V^T = \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^T + \sigma_2 u_2 v_2^T + \dots + \sigma_r u_r v_r^T
$$

> [!summary] Core Properties of the SVD
> 1. **Factorization:** The Singular Value Decomposition of any $m \times n$ matrix $A$ is $A = U\Sigma V^T$, or equivalently $AV = U\Sigma$.
> 2. **Orthonormality:** The right singular vectors $v_i$ (columns of $V$) and left singular vectors $u_i$ (columns of $U$) satisfy $Av_i = \sigma_i u_i$ and are orthonormal: $V^T V = I_n$ and $U^T U = I_m$.
> 3. **Positive Ordered Singular Values:** The diagonal matrix $\Sigma$ contains the positive singular values ordered by magnitude: $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0$.
> 4. **Eigenvalue Connection:** The squares $\sigma_i^2$ of the nonzero singular values are the nonzero eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ of both $A^T A$ and $A A^T$.

Notice the philosophical shift here: **every matrix $A$ is diagonalized by two sets of singular vectors ($U$ and $V$), rather than one set of eigenvectors.** And in the rank-one sum $A = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i u_i v_i^T$, each rank-one matrix $u_i v_i^T$ has the exact same norm (since $\|u_i\| = 1$ and $\|v_i\| = 1$), so the scalar $\sigma_i$ directly measures how much weight or importance the $i$-th piece contributes to $A$.

## 2. The Geometry of the SVD

What does multiplying a vector $x$ by $A = U\Sigma V^T$ actually *look* like geometrically? Because $U$ and $V$ are orthogonal matrices and $\Sigma$ is diagonal, the SVD breaks the linear transformation $x \mapsto Ax$ into three simple geometric steps:
$$
A = U \Sigma V^T = (\text{orthogonal}) \times (\text{diagonal}) \times (\text{orthogonal})
$$
In two (or three) dimensions, an orthogonal matrix is a **rotation** (possibly combined with a reflection if its determinant is $-1$), while a diagonal matrix **stretches** space along the coordinate axes. Let's trace what happens to the unit circle of all unit vectors $x$ ($\|x\| = 1$) when we multiply $x$ by $A = U\Sigma V^T$ from right to left:

1. **First Rotation ($x \mapsto V^T x$):** Since $V^T V = I$, multiplying by $V^T$ rotates the unit circle without changing any lengths or angles. Specifically, since the rows of $V^T$ are $v_1^T, v_2^T, \dots$, multiplying $x = v_1$ by $V^T$ produces the first standard basis vector $e_1 = (1, 0, \dots, 0)^T$, and multiplying $x = v_2$ by $V^T$ produces $e_2 = (0, 1, \dots, 0)^T$. In other words, $V^T$ rotates the orthonormal basis $v_1, \dots, v_n$ so that it lines up with the standard coordinate axes!
2. **Axis-Aligned Stretching ($V^T x \mapsto \Sigma V^T x$):** Next, the diagonal matrix $\Sigma$ stretches the rotated unit circle along each standard coordinate axis by the factor $\sigma_i$. The first axis is stretched by $\sigma_1$, the second by $\sigma_2$, and so on (while any axes beyond $r$ are collapsed to $0$). This turns the unit circle into an axis-aligned **ellipse** (or hyper-ellipsoid) with semi-axis lengths $\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \dots$.
3. **Second Rotation ($\Sigma V^T x \mapsto U \Sigma V^T x = Ax$):** Finally, multiplying by the orthogonal matrix $U$ rotates the axis-aligned ellipse so that the standard coordinate axes $e_1, e_2, \dots$ move to the left singular vectors $u_1, u_2, \dots$. Thus, the input vector $v_1$ on the unit circle ends up at $Av_1 = \sigma_1 u_1$ (the major semi-axis of the ellipse), and $v_2$ ends up at $Av_2 = \sigma_2 u_2$ (the minor semi-axis).

### Parameter Counting & Special Matrix Classes

Let's test this geometric intuition by counting independent parameters (degrees of freedom) in $A = U\Sigma V^T$:

- **General Invertible $2 \times 2$ Matrix (4 Parameters):** Suppose $A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$ has $\sigma_1 > 0$, $\sigma_2 > 0$, and we choose positive determinants for $U$ and $V$ to represent pure rotations by angles $\theta$ and $\phi$ (avoiding reflections). Then:
  $$
  A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \cos\phi & \sin\phi \\ -\sin\phi & \cos\phi \end{bmatrix}
  $$
  Notice how the 4 independent entries $a, b, c, d$ in $A$ correspond to **2 rotation angles** ($\theta$ in $U$ and $\phi$ in $V^T$) plus **2 singular values** ($\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ in $\Sigma$).
- **Symmetric $2 \times 2$ Matrix (3 Parameters):** What happens if $A$ is symmetric, so $b = c$? Now $A$ has only $3$ independent parameters instead of $4$. How do the $4$ SVD numbers ($\theta, \phi, \sigma_1, \sigma_2$) reduce to $3$? For a symmetric matrix, the input and output rotation angles are forced to be equal: $\theta = \phi$ (so $U$ and $V$ share the same eigenvector directions, up to sign), leaving $1$ angle and $2$ singular values ($1 + 2 = 3$ parameters).
- **General $3 \times 3$ Matrix (9 Parameters):** If $A$ is $3 \times 3$ with $9$ independent entries, $\Sigma$ contains $3$ singular values ($\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3$). How many independent parameters do the $3 \times 3$ orthogonal rotation matrices $U$ and $V$ have? Each 3D rotation is determined by $3$ independent angles—familiar to an airplane pilot as **pitch**, **roll**, and **yaw** (Euler angles). That gives $3 + 3 + 3 = 9$ parameters in total!
- **Symmetric Positive Definite Matrix ($S = Q\Lambda Q^T$):** When $S$ is symmetric positive definite, all its eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ are already positive. Its SVD is simply $U\Sigma V^T = Q\Lambda Q^T$, with $U = V = Q$ and $\Sigma = \Lambda$.
- **Symmetric Matrix with Negative Eigenvalues:** What if $S = Q\Lambda Q^T$ is symmetric, but has a negative eigenvalue, say $Sx = -\alpha x$ with $\alpha > 0$? Can we set $\sigma = -\alpha$? **No!** Singular values in $\Sigma$ must always be positive ($\sigma = +\alpha > 0$). To make $Sv = \sigma u$ match $Sx = -\alpha x$, we keep one singular vector as $x$ and reverse the sign of the other singular vector to $-x$ (for instance, $v = x$ and $u = -x$). Then $Sv = -\alpha x = \alpha(-x) = \sigma u$; the sign change in $\sigma$ and the sign change in $u$ cancel out in the product $U\Sigma V^T$.

## 3. The Full Size Form vs. The Reduced Form of the SVD

When $A$ is an $m \times n$ matrix of rank $r$, there are two standard ways to write the SVD depending on whether we keep or discard the vectors from the nullspaces $N(A)$ and $N(A^T)$.

### The Full Size SVD

In the **Full Size SVD**, we complete $v_1, \dots, v_r$ (the row space basis) with an orthonormal basis $v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n$ for the nullspace $N(A)$ to form a **square** $n \times n$ orthogonal matrix $V$. Similarly, we complete $u_1, \dots, u_r$ (the column space basis) with an orthonormal basis $u_{r+1}, \dots, u_m$ for the left nullspace $N(A^T)$ to form a **square** $m \times m$ orthogonal matrix $U$.

> [!abstract] Definition: Full Size SVD
> For an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ of rank $r$, the full size SVD writes $AV = U\Sigma$ (or $A = U\Sigma V^T$) with square orthogonal matrices $U$ ($m \times m$, $U^T = U^{-1}$) and $V$ ($n \times n$, $V^T = V^{-1}$), and an $m \times n$ matrix $\Sigma$:
> $$
> A \begin{bmatrix} v_1 & \dots & v_r & v_{r+1} & \dots & v_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 & \dots & u_r & u_{r+1} & \dots & u_m \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1 & & & 0 \\ & \ddots & & \vdots \\ & & \sigma_r & 0 \\ 0 & \dots & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> The matrix dimensions in $AV = U\Sigma$ are $(m \times n)(n \times n) = (m \times m)(m \times n)$.

### The Reduced Form of the SVD

Think about what happens in the full size SVD when the rank $r$ of $A$ is much smaller than $m$ and $n$ (meaning the nullspaces $N(A)$ and $N(A^T)$ are large). The $m \times n$ matrix $\Sigma$ is mostly filled with zeros! When we multiply $U \Sigma V^T$, all those zeros multiply the nullspace vectors $v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n$ and $u_{r+1}, \dots, u_m$, contributing nothing at all to $A$.

The true heart of the SVD lies entirely in the first $r$ right singular vectors, the first $r$ left singular vectors, and the $r$ positive singular values. By stripping away the zero rows and columns of $\Sigma$ along with the nullspace columns of $U$ and $V$, we obtain the **Reduced SVD** (often called the thin or economy SVD):

> [!abstract] Definition: Reduced SVD
> Let $V_r = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 & \dots & v_r \end{bmatrix}$ be the $n \times r$ matrix whose columns are the orthonormal basis for the row space of $A$, $U_r = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 & \dots & u_r \end{bmatrix}$ be the $m \times r$ matrix whose columns are the orthonormal basis for the column space of $A$, and $\Sigma_r = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r)$ be the **square** $r \times r$ diagonal matrix of positive singular values. Then:
> $$
> A V_r = U_r \Sigma_r \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad A = U_r \Sigma_r V_r^T = \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^T + \dots + \sigma_r u_r v_r^T
> $$
> where the dimensions in $AV_r = U_r \Sigma_r$ are $(m \times n)(n \times r) = (m \times r)(r \times r)$.

> [!warning] Caution: Rectangular $U_r$ and $V_r$ Have Only One-Sided Inverses
> Because the $r$ columns of $V_r$ are orthonormal and the $r$ columns of $U_r$ are orthonormal, we still have:
> $$
> V_r^T V_r = I_r \qquad \text{and} \qquad U_r^T U_r = I_r
> $$
> However, whenever $r < n$ or $r < m$, $V_r$ and $U_r$ are rectangular matrices, so they do **not** have full two-sided inverses:
> $$
> V_r V_r^T \neq I_n \qquad \text{and} \qquad U_r U_r^T \neq I_m
> $$
> (In fact, $V_r V_r^T$ is the orthogonal projection matrix onto the row space of $A$, and $U_r U_r^T$ is the orthogonal projection matrix onto the column space of $A$!). Even though $V_r V_r^T \neq I_n$, the equation $A = U_r \Sigma_r V_r^T$ is still **exact** because the omitted columns $v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n$ sit in the nullspace of $A$ ($Av_i = 0$) and were multiplied by zeros in the full size $\Sigma$.

## 4. Proof of the SVD

How do we actually prove that *every* $m \times n$ matrix $A$ has a Singular Value Decomposition $A = U\Sigma V^T$, and how do we systematically construct $V$, $\Sigma$, and $U$?

Let's start by asking: if $A = U\Sigma V^T$ exists, how are $U$, $\Sigma$, and $V$ related to symmetric matrices that we already know how to diagonalize? If we form the two symmetric matrices $A^T A$ ($n \times n$) and $A A^T$ ($m \times m$) and use $U^T U = I_m$ and $V^T V = I_n$, the cross-terms collapse:
$$
\begin{aligned}
A^T A &= (V \Sigma^T U^T)(U \Sigma V^T) = V (\Sigma^T \Sigma) V^T \\
A A^T &= (U \Sigma V^T)(V \Sigma^T U^T) = U (\Sigma \Sigma^T) U^T
\end{aligned}
$$
Look at the right-hand sides of these two equations: both have the exact symmetric spectral form $Q \Lambda Q^T$!
- $V$ must contain orthonormal eigenvectors of the symmetric matrix $A^T A$.
- $U$ must contain orthonormal eigenvectors of the symmetric matrix $A A^T$.
- The diagonal entries $\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_r^2$ of $\Sigma^T \Sigma$ and $\Sigma \Sigma^T$ must be the nonzero eigenvalues of both $A^T A$ and $A A^T$.

> [!warning] Caution: Why Can't We Choose Eigenvectors of $A^T A$ and $A A^T$ Independently?
> It is tempting to think we are already done: just find any orthonormal eigenvectors of $A^T A$ to put in $V$, and independently find any orthonormal eigenvectors of $A A^T$ to put in $U$. **Watch out—that will fail!**
>
> The SVD requires the coupling equation $Av_k = \sigma_k u_k$ for $k = 1, \dots, r$. If $u_k$ is a unit eigenvector of $S = A A^T$ ($Su_k = \lambda_k u_k$), then $-u_k$ is *also* a unit eigenvector ($S(-u_k) = \lambda_k(-u_k)$). Once we pick a sign for $v_k$, the sign of $u_k$ is completely locked in by $Av_k = \sigma_k u_k$. Even more importantly, if $\lambda_k = \sigma_k^2$ is a repeated eigenvalue of $A^T A$, there is an entire plane (or subspace) of eigenvectors! If we pick two orthogonal vectors $v_1, v_2$ in the eigenspace of $A^T A$, we cannot pick arbitrary orthogonal eigenvectors in the eigenspace of $A A^T$—we *must* let $Av_k = \sigma_k u_k$ determine $u_k$ directly from $v_k$.

This observation gives us the exact blueprint to prove the SVD rigorously.

> [!theorem] Theorem: Existence and Construction of the SVD
> Every $m \times n$ matrix $A$ of rank $r$ can be factored as $A = U\Sigma V^T$, where $V$ ($n \times n$) and $U$ ($m \times m$) are orthogonal matrices and $\Sigma$ ($m \times n$) contains the positive singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0$ on its main diagonal and zeros elsewhere. Specifically, if $v_1, \dots, v_r$ are orthonormal eigenvectors of $A^T A$ with positive eigenvalues $\lambda_k = \sigma_k^2 > 0$, then the vectors:
> $$
> u_k = \frac{Av_k}{\sigma_k} \quad \text{for } k = 1, \dots, r
> $$
> are orthonormal eigenvectors of $A A^T$ with the same eigenvalues $\sigma_k^2$.

**Proof:**
We construct $V$, $\Sigma$, and $U$ step by step:

1. **Choose the Row-Space Basis $v_1, \dots, v_r$ and Singular Values $\sigma_k$:**
   Because $A^T A$ is a symmetric positive semidefinite $n \times n$ matrix of rank $r$, the Spectral Theorem guarantees that it has $r$ positive eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_r > 0$ (and $n - r$ zero eigenvalues) along with an orthonormal set of eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_r$ satisfying:
   $$
   A^T A v_k = \sigma_k^2 v_k \quad \text{where } \sigma_k = \sqrt{\lambda_k} > 0 \text{ for } k = 1, \dots, r
   $$

2. **Define $u_1, \dots, u_r$ via $Av_k = \sigma_k u_k$:**
   To enforce the required relation $Av_k = \sigma_k u_k$, we explicitly define:
   $$
   u_k = \frac{Av_k}{\sigma_k} \quad \text{for } k = 1, \dots, r
   $$

3. **Verify that $u_1, \dots, u_r$ are Eigenvectors of $A A^T$:**
   Let's multiply $u_k$ by $A A^T$ and use the **associative law of matrix multiplication** $(A A^T) A = A (A^T A)$ to shift the parentheses:
   $$
   A A^T u_k = A A^T \left( \frac{Av_k}{\sigma_k} \right) = A \left( \frac{A^T A v_k}{\sigma_k} \right) = A \left( \frac{\sigma_k^2 v_k}{\sigma_k} \right) = \sigma_k^2 \left( \frac{Av_k}{\sigma_k} \right) = \sigma_k^2 u_k
   $$
   Thus, each $u_k$ is indeed an eigenvector of $A A^T$ with eigenvalue $\sigma_k^2$.

4. **Verify that $u_1, \dots, u_r$ are Orthonormal:**
   We must check the inner product $u_j^T u_k$ for all $1 \le j, k \le r$. Using $(Av_j)^T = v_j^T A^T$ and $A^T A v_k = \sigma_k^2 v_k$, along with the fact that $v_1, \dots, v_r$ were chosen to be orthonormal:
   $$
   u_j^T u_k = \left( \frac{Av_j}{\sigma_j} \right)^T \left( \frac{Av_k}{\sigma_k} \right) = \frac{v_j^T (A^T A v_k)}{\sigma_j \sigma_k} = \frac{\sigma_k^2}{\sigma_j \sigma_k} v_j^T v_k = \frac{\sigma_k}{\sigma_j} v_j^T v_k = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } j = k \\ 0 & \text{if } j \neq k \end{cases}
   $$
   This confirms that $u_1, \dots, u_r$ are mutually orthogonal unit vectors!

5. **Complete the Bases Using the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra:**
   Finally, we need to choose the last $n - r$ vectors $v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n$ and the last $m - r$ vectors $u_{r+1}, \dots, u_m$. These vectors live in the nullspace $N(A)$ and the left nullspace $N(A^T)$, respectively. We simply choose *any* orthonormal basis $v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n$ for $N(A)$ (so $Av_i = 0$ for $i > r$) and *any* orthonormal basis $u_{r+1}, \dots, u_m$ for $N(A^T)$.
   Why are these new vectors automatically orthogonal to the first $r$ vectors? By the **Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra**, the entire subspaces are orthogonal:
   $$
   N(A) \perp C(A^T) \qquad \text{and} \qquad N(A^T) \perp C(A)
   $$
   Since $v_1, \dots, v_r \in C(A^T)$ and $u_1, \dots, u_r \in C(A)$, our completed matrices $V = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 & \dots & v_n \end{bmatrix}$ and $U = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 & \dots & u_m \end{bmatrix}$ are fully orthogonal ($V^T V = I_n$, $U^T U = I_m$), and $AV = U\Sigma$, which gives $A = U\Sigma V^T$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Trace and Determinant Relationships
> When $A$ is a square $n \times n$ matrix, $A^T A$ and $A A^T$ have the same trace $\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = \sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i^2$, and the product of the singular values $\sigma_1 \sigma_2 \cdots \sigma_n$ equals $|\det(A)|$ because $\det(A^T A) = (\det A)^2 = \sigma_1^2 \sigma_2^2 \cdots \sigma_n^2$.

## 5. $AB$ and $BA$: Equal Nonzero Eigenvalues

In the proof of the SVD, we saw from $\Sigma^T \Sigma$ and $\Sigma \Sigma^T$ that the $n \times n$ matrix $A^T A$ and the $m \times m$ matrix $A A^T$ share the exact same nonzero eigenvalues $\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_r^2$. That is actually a special case ($B = A^T$) of a remarkable general theorem about the products $AB$ and $BA$ of any two compatible matrices!

> [!theorem] Theorem: $AB$ and $BA$ Have the Same Nonzero Eigenvalues
> If $A$ is an $m \times n$ matrix and $B$ is an $n \times m$ matrix, then the $m \times m$ matrix $AB$ and the $n \times n$ matrix $BA$ have the exact same nonzero eigenvalues.

**Proof:**
Suppose $\lambda \neq 0$ is a nonzero eigenvalue of $AB$ with nonzero eigenvector $x \in \mathbb{R}^m$:
$$
ABx = \lambda x \quad (\text{with } \lambda \neq 0 \text{ and } x \neq 0)
$$
Multiply both sides of this equation on the left by $B$:
$$
B(ABx) = B(\lambda x) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad (BA)(Bx) = \lambda (Bx)
$$
Look at what this equation says: the vector $Bx \in \mathbb{R}^n$ satisfies $(BA)(Bx) = \lambda(Bx)$! To conclude that $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $BA$ with eigenvector $Bx$, we only need to make sure that $Bx$ is not the zero vector. Why did we require $\lambda \neq 0$? Because if $Bx = 0$, then multiplying by $A$ would give $ABx = A(0) = 0$, contradicting $ABx = \lambda x \neq 0$. Therefore, $Bx \neq 0$ is a genuine eigenvector of $BA$ with the same nonzero eigenvalue $\lambda$. By symmetry (swapping the roles of $A$ and $B$), every nonzero eigenvalue of $BA$ is also a nonzero eigenvalue of $AB$. $\blacksquare$

Let's note two important insights about this result:
1. **Why Not Just Use Matrix Similarity?** If $A$ and $B$ are square and $B$ is invertible, then $B^{-1}(BA)B = AB$, which says $BA$ and $AB$ are *similar* matrices and therefore share all eigenvalues. However, our proof above is far more general: it does not require $B$ to be invertible, nor does it require $A$ and $B$ to be square! That is why it applies to rectangular matrices in the SVD when $B = A^T$.
2. **What About the Zero Eigenvalues?** If $m > n$, the $m \times m$ matrix $AB$ is larger than the $n \times n$ matrix $BA$. Since they share the exact same nonzero eigenvalues, $AB$ simply has $m - n$ extra zero eigenvalues compared to $BA$.

## 6. Singular Vectors for $2 \times 2$ Matrices: A Picture Proof

Beyond the algebraic proof using $A^T A$, we can also understand *why* orthogonal singular vectors must exist for an invertible $2 \times 2$ matrix $A$ through a purely geometric continuity argument (a "picture proof").

Our goal is to find a pair of perpendicular unit vectors $v$ and $w$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ ($v \perp w$) such that their output vectors $Av$ and $Aw$ are **also perpendicular** ($Av \perp Aw$).

1. **First Guess:** Start with the standard basis vectors $V = (1, 0)$ and $W = (0, 1)$, which are perpendicular ($90^\circ$ apart on the unit circle). Multiply both by $A$ to get the output vectors $AV$ and $AW$. If the angle $\theta$ from $AV$ to $AW$ happens to be $90^\circ$, we are already done!
2. **Rotating the Input Frame by $90^\circ$:** Suppose instead that the angle $\theta$ from $AV$ to $AW$ is too small (strictly below $90^\circ$). Now imagine rotating our perpendicular input pair $(v, w)$ continuously counterclockwise around the unit circle by $90^\circ$, so that $v$ moves from $V$ to $W$, and $w$ moves from $W$ to $-V$.
3. **Continuous Crossing of $90^\circ$:** At the end of this $90^\circ$ rotation, the output pair $(Av, Aw)$ has moved from $(AV, AW)$ to $(AW, A(-V)) = (AW, -AV)$. Since the angle from $AV$ to $AW$ was $\theta < 90^\circ$, the supplementary angle from $AW$ to $-AV$ is $180^\circ - \theta > 90^\circ$ (too large!).
4. **Conclusion by Continuity:** As $v$ rotates continuously from $V$ toward $W$, the angle between $Av$ and $Aw$ changes continuously from $\theta < 90^\circ$ to $180^\circ - \theta > 90^\circ$. Therefore, at some intermediate position of $v$ between $V$ and $W$, the angle between $Av$ and $Aw$ must pass through **exactly $90^\circ$**. At that point, we have orthogonal inputs $v \perp w$ and orthogonal outputs $Av \perp Aw$, proving the $2 \times 2$ SVD!

## 7. The First Singular Vector $v_1$ and the Variational View

So far, we found all the right singular vectors $v_1, \dots, v_n$ at once as eigenvectors of $A^T A$. However, there is a second, extraordinarily powerful way to understand and find singular vectors **one at a time**: through **optimization** (maximizing the stretching ratio $\frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|}$).

Look back at the SVD ellipse in Figure 7.1. When $x$ ranges over the unit circle ($\|x\| = 1$), $Ax$ traces out an ellipse whose longest semi-axis has length $\|Av_1\| = \|\sigma_1 u_1\| = \sigma_1$, achieved when $x = v_1$. In other words, the first singular vector $v_1$ is the direction in which the matrix $A$ stretches vectors the most!

> [!theorem] Theorem: Variational Characterization of Singular Values and Vectors
> 1. **First Singular Value $\sigma_1$ and Vector $v_1$:** Over all nonzero vectors $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the maximum stretching ratio of $A$ is $\sigma_1$, achieved at the first right singular vector $x = v_1$:
>    $$
>    \max_{x \neq 0} \frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|} = \sigma_1 \quad \text{at } x = v_1
>    $$
> 2. **Second Singular Value $\sigma_2$ and Vector $v_2$:** If we restrict $x$ to be orthogonal to $v_1$ ($v_1^T x = 0$), the maximum stretching ratio is $\sigma_2$, achieved at $x = v_2$:
>    $$
>    \max_{v_1^T x = 0,\, x \neq 0} \frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|} = \sigma_2 \quad \text{at } x = v_2
>    $$
> 3. **General $k$-th Step:** Every subsequent singular vector $v_{k+1}$ maximizes the ratio $\frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|}$ (achieving maximum $\sigma_{k+1}$) over all nonzero vectors $x$ orthogonal to the previous singular vectors $v_1, \dots, v_k$ ($v_1^T x = \dots = v_k^T x = 0$).

**Proof:**
How can we prove that maximizing $\frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|}$ produces the eigenvectors of $S = A^T A$ **independently**, without assuming we already know the SVD $A = U\Sigma V^T$?

1. **Square the Ratio to Form the Rayleigh Quotient:**
   Maximizing $\frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|}$ is equivalent to maximizing its square. Writing $\|Ax\|^2 = (Ax)^T(Ax) = x^T A^T A x$ and setting $S = A^T A$, we want to maximize the **Rayleigh quotient** of the symmetric matrix $S$:
   $$
   \frac{\|Ax\|^2}{\|x\|^2} = \frac{x^T A^T A x}{x^T x} = \frac{x^T S x}{x^T x}
   $$
2. **Expand $x$ in the Orthonormal Eigenvector Basis of $S$:**
   Since $S = A^T A$ is symmetric, it has a complete orthonormal basis of eigenvectors $x_1, \dots, x_n$ with real nonnegative eigenvalues ordered as $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n \ge 0$ ($Sx_i = \lambda_i x_i$). Write any nonzero vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ as a linear combination of these orthonormal eigenvectors:
   $$
   x = c_1 x_1 + c_2 x_2 + \dots + c_n x_n
   $$
   Then $Sx = c_1 \lambda_1 x_1 + c_2 \lambda_2 x_2 + \dots + c_n \lambda_n x_n$. Because the eigenvectors $x_i$ are orthonormal ($x_i^T x_j = 0$ for $i \neq j$ and $x_i^T x_i = 1$), all cross-terms vanish in the dot products $x^T S x$ and $x^T x$:
   $$
   \frac{x^T S x}{x^T x} = \frac{c_1^2 \lambda_1 + c_2^2 \lambda_2 + \dots + c_n^2 \lambda_n}{c_1^2 + c_2^2 + \dots + c_n^2} \le \frac{(c_1^2 + c_2^2 + \dots + c_n^2)\lambda_1}{c_1^2 + c_2^2 + \dots + c_n^2} = \lambda_1
   $$
3. **Identify the Maximizer:**
   This upper bound $\lambda_1$ (the largest eigenvalue of $S = A^T A$) is achieved when we set $c_2 = \dots = c_n = 0$, meaning $x = x_1 = v_1$ is the dominant eigenvector of $S$! Thus:
   $$
   \max_{x \neq 0} \frac{x^T S x}{x^T x} = \max_{x \neq 0} \frac{\|Ax\|^2}{\|x\|^2} = \lambda_1(S) = \sigma_1^2(A) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \max_{x \neq 0} \frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|} = \sigma_1
   $$
4. **Constrained Maximization for $v_2, \dots, v_n$:**
   When we impose the orthogonality constraint $v_1^T x = x_1^T x = 0$ (which can also be formulated via Lagrange multipliers), the coefficient $c_1 = x_1^T x$ is forced to be $0$. The Rayleigh quotient then becomes:
   $$
   \frac{c_2^2 \lambda_2 + \dots + c_n^2 \lambda_n}{c_2^2 + \dots + c_n^2} \le \lambda_2 = \sigma_2^2
   $$
   which is maximized at $x = x_2 = v_2$ with maximum value $\sigma_2$. Repeating this argument by requiring $x$ to be orthogonal to $v_1, \dots, v_k$ sets $c_1 = \dots = c_k = 0$, yielding maximum $\sigma_{k+1}$ at $x = v_{k+1}$. $\blacksquare$

### Eigenvalues Bounded by the Largest Singular Value ($|\lambda| \le \sigma_1$)

How do the eigenvalues $\lambda$ of a square matrix $A$ compare in magnitude to its largest singular value $\sigma_1$?

> [!theorem] Theorem: Maximum Singular Value Bounds All Eigenvalues
> For any square matrix $A$, every eigenvalue $\lambda$ satisfies:
> $$
> |\lambda| \le \sigma_1
> $$

**Proof:**
Multiplying any vector by an orthogonal matrix ($U$ or $V^T$) preserves its Euclidean length. Furthermore, the diagonal matrix $\Sigma$ stretches each component of $V^T x$ by at most $\sigma_1$. Therefore, for every vector $x$:
$$
\|Ax\| = \|U \Sigma V^T x\| = \|\Sigma V^T x\| \le \sigma_1 \|V^T x\| = \sigma_1 \|x\|
$$
Now suppose $x \neq 0$ is an eigenvector of $A$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$, so $Ax = \lambda x$. Taking norms gives $\|Ax\| = \|\lambda x\| = |\lambda| \|x\|$. Substituting this into the inequality above yields:
$$
|\lambda| \|x\| \le \sigma_1 \|x\| \quad \Longrightarrow \quad |\lambda| \le \sigma_1
$$
since $\|x\| > 0$. $\blacksquare$

### SVD of a Rank-1 Matrix and the Cauchy–Schwarz Inequality

Let's apply our tools to a general rank-1 matrix $A = xy^T$, where $x \in \mathbb{R}^m$ and $y \in \mathbb{R}^n$ are nonzero vectors. Since $A$ already has the outer-product form of a single column times a single row, how do we write its Reduced SVD $A = u_1 \sigma_1 v_1^T$?

We simply normalize $x$ and $y$ so that the singular vectors have unit length! Setting:
$$
u_1 = \frac{x}{\|x\|}, \qquad v_1 = \frac{y}{\|y\|}, \qquad \sigma_1 = \|x\| \|y\|
$$
gives the exact rank-1 SVD:
$$
A = xy^T = \frac{x}{\|x\|} \left( \|x\| \|y\| \right) \frac{y^T}{\|y\|} = u_1 \sigma_1 v_1^T
$$
Here, $\sigma_1 = \|x\| \|y\|$ is the only nonzero singular value in $\Sigma$.

Now, what if $A = xy^T$ is a **square** $n \times n$ rank-1 matrix ($x, y \in \mathbb{R}^n$)? What is its only nonzero eigenvalue $\lambda_1$, and what happens when we check $|\lambda_1| \le \sigma_1$?
Multiplying $A = xy^T$ by the vector $x$ and using associativity gives:
$$
Ax = (xy^T)x = x(y^T x) = \lambda_1 x \quad \text{with } \lambda_1 = y^T x
$$
So $x$ is the eigenvector and $\lambda_1 = y^T x$ is the eigenvalue! Plugging $\lambda_1 = y^T x$ and $\sigma_1 = \|x\| \|y\|$ into our theorem $|\lambda_1| \le \sigma_1$ immediately produces:
$$
|y^T x| \le \|x\| \|y\|
$$
which is none other than the famous **Cauchy–Schwarz inequality**!

## 8. Computing Eigenvalues and Singular Values

In practice, finding eigenvalues or singular values of a matrix of size $n \ge 5$ requires an iterative numerical algorithm (like the shifted $QR$ algorithm). Before running those iterations, however, we want to simplify the matrix by introducing as many zeros as possible using fast, exact orthogonal transformations (like Householder reflections or Givens rotations).

Let's ask a fundamental question: **What is the main difference between simplifying a symmetric matrix $S$ (for $Sx = \lambda x$) and simplifying a general matrix $A$ (for $Av = \sigma u$)?**

1. **Symmetric Eigenvalue Problem ($S \mapsto Q^{-1} S Q = Q^T S Q$):**
   Because eigenvalues are preserved under *similarity* transformations $Q^{-1} S Q$, we must multiply $S$ on the left by $Q^T$ and on the right by the **same** orthogonal matrix $Q$. That restriction limits how many zeros we can create! If we try to use $Q^T$ on the left to zero out the entire lower triangle of $Q^T S$, then multiplying on the right by $Q$ will destroy those newly created zeros. Because $Q^T S Q$ remains symmetric, the best we can do in a direct finite reduction is **tridiagonal** form (three nonzero diagonals):
   $$
   Q^{-1} S Q = \begin{bmatrix}
   a_1 & b_1 & & \\
   b_1 & a_2 & b_2 & \\
   & b_2 & \ddots & \ddots \\
   & & \ddots & a_n
   \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{tridiagonal for }\lambda\text{'s})
   $$

2. **Singular Value Problem ($A \mapsto Q_1^{-1} A Q_2 = Q_1^T A Q_2$):**
   For singular values, if $A = U\Sigma V^T$, then multiplying on the left by $Q_1^T$ and on the right by $Q_2$ gives $Q_1^T A Q_2 = (Q_1^T U) \Sigma (V^T Q_2)$. Since $Q_1^T U$ and $Q_2^T V$ are still orthogonal matrices, **singular values are completely unchanged by multiplying $A$ on the left and right by two different orthogonal matrices $Q_1^T$ and $Q_2$!**
   Having two independent orthogonal matrices $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ gives us extra freedom to create zeros without destroying previous ones: we use $Q_1^T$ to zero out entries below the main diagonal (column by column) and $Q_2$ to zero out entries to the right of the superdiagonal (row by row). This reduces $A$ directly to upper **bidiagonal** form (only two nonzero diagonals):
   $$
   Q_1^{-1} A Q_2 = \begin{bmatrix}
   c_1 & d_1 & & \\
   0 & c_2 & d_2 & \\
   & 0 & \ddots & \ddots \\
   & & 0 & c_n
   \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{bidiagonal for }\sigma\text{'s})
   $$

Notice how neatly these two forms connect: since the singular values of $A$ (and of $A^* = Q_1^{-1} A Q_2$) are the square roots of the eigenvalues of $S = A^T A$, multiplying $(\text{bidiagonal})^T (\text{bidiagonal})$ produces a symmetric **tridiagonal** matrix!

> [!warning] Caution: Never Compute the SVD by Explicitly Multiplying $A^T A$!
> Knowing that $(A^*)^T A^*$ is tridiagonal—or that $v_i$ are eigenvectors of $A^T A$—offers a tempting shortcut that **we should never take in numerical code**: do *not* multiply $A^T A$ explicitly and find its eigenvalues!
> 1. Forming $A^T A$ is unnecessary extra work.
> 2. Much worse, multiplying $A^T A$ **squares the condition number** of the problem ($\kappa(A^T A) = (\sigma_1 / \sigma_n)^2 = \kappa(A)^2$), which catastrophically destroys numerical accuracy for small singular values in floating-point arithmetic.

### The Golub–Kahan Algorithm and Randomized SVD

Instead of forming $A^T A$, the standard **Golub–Kahan algorithm** computes the SVD by working directly on $A$ in two phases:
1. **Bidiagonal Reduction ($O(mn^2)$ multiplications):** Find orthogonal matrices $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ such that $A^* = Q_1^{-1} A Q_2$ is upper **bidiagonal**, preserving all singular values of $A$.
2. **Adapted Shifted $QR$ Iteration ($O(n^2)$ multiplications):** Apply an adapted version of the shifted $QR$ algorithm directly to the bidiagonal matrix $A^*$ that preserves both its bidiagonal structure and its singular values. Because it operates only on bidiagonal matrices, each step is extremely fast, requiring only $O(n^2)$ multiplications in total to converge to all singular values to nearly machine precision.

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Randomized Linear Algebra for Massive Matrices
> What if $A$ is so enormous (as in modern big-data applications) that even $O(mn^2)$ operations for bidiagonalization are too expensive? In that regime, we turn to **random sampling** (*randomized linear algebra*). By projecting $A$ onto a smaller random subspace, randomized algorithms compute highly accurate approximations to the dominant singular values and singular vectors with overwhelmingly high probability at a fraction of the computational cost.
