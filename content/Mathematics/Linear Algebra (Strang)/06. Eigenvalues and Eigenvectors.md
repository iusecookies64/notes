# 6.1 Introduction to Eigenvalues: $Ax = \lambda x$

Linear algebra enters a dynamic realm in the study of **eigenvalues** ($\lambda$) and **eigenvectors** ($x$). While earlier chapters focus on static equilibrium systems ($Ax = b$), eigenvalues and eigenvectors govern systems of change: discrete-time difference equations ($u_{k+1} = A u_k$) driven by matrix powers $A^k$, and continuous-time differential equations ($du/dt = Au$) driven by matrix exponentials $e^{At}$. These dynamic equations cannot be solved by Gaussian elimination; instead, eigenvectors reveal the intrinsic coordinate axes along which the matrix acts purely by scalar stretching or shrinking.

## Fundamental Definitions and Geometric Meaning

> [!abstract] Definition: Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors
> Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ square matrix. A non-zero vector $x \in \mathbb{C}^n$ (or $\mathbb{R}^n$) is called an **eigenvector** of $A$ if the transformation $A x$ produces a vector collinear with $x$:
> $$
> Ax = \lambda x \quad (x \ne 0)
> $$
> The scalar factor $\lambda$ is the **eigenvalue** associated with the eigenvector $x$.

### Geometric Interpretation and Eigenspaces
1. **Direction Invariance:**
   For a typical vector $v$, the vector $Av$ points in a completely different direction. An eigenvector $x$ is exceptional: multiplication by $A$ preserves its line of action, merely scaling it by $\lambda$ (or reversing its direction if $\lambda < 0$).
2. **Eigenvector Lines and Eigenspaces:**
   If $x$ is an eigenvector of $A$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$, then any non-zero scalar multiple $cx$ ($c \ne 0$) is also an eigenvector:
   $$
   A(cx) = c(Ax) = c(\lambda x) = \lambda(cx)
   $$
   The set containing all eigenvectors corresponding to $\lambda$, together with the zero vector $0$, forms a vector space called the **eigenspace** of $\lambda$. This is the nullspace of the shifted matrix:
   $$
   E_\lambda = N(A - \lambda I)
   $$

### Eigenvector Decomposition and Decoupled Evolution
When an arbitrary vector $v$ can be represented as a linear combination of eigenvectors $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_k$ with associated eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_k$:
$$
v = c_1 x_1 + c_2 x_2 + \dots + c_k x_k
$$
applying the linear transformation $A$ decouples the motion along each independent eigenvector direction:
$$
Av = c_1 Ax_1 + c_2 Ax_2 + \dots + c_k Ax_k = c_1 \lambda_1 x_1 + c_2 \lambda_2 x_2 + \dots + c_k \lambda_k x_k
$$
Repeated application of $A$ yields powers of eigenvalues:
$$
A^n v = c_1 \lambda_1^n x_1 + c_2 \lambda_2^n x_2 + \dots + c_k \lambda_k^n x_k
$$

> [!note] Dynamic Modes and Asymptotic Growth
> The magnitudes of the eigenvalues $|\lambda_i|$ determine the long-term behavior of discrete dynamical systems:
> - **Decaying Modes ($|\lambda_i| < 1$):** As $n \to \infty$, $\lambda_i^n \to 0$. The component along $x_i$ vanishes asymptotically.
> - **Steady State ($\lambda_i = 1$):** As $n \to \infty$, $\lambda_i^n = 1$. The component along $x_i$ remains invariant.
> - **Unbounded Growth ($|\lambda_i| > 1$):** As $n \to \infty$, $|\lambda_i|^n \to \infty$. The system experiences exponential divergence along $x_i$.

## Special Geometric Matrices

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors correspond directly to the geometric actions of fundamental transformations:

### Identity Matrix
For the identity matrix $I$, every non-zero vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ satisfies:
$$
Ix = 1 \cdot x
$$
Every vector is an eigenvector, and all eigenvalues are $\lambda = 1$. The entire space $\mathbb{R}^n$ is the eigenspace.

### Projection Matrices
A projection matrix $P$ satisfies the idempotent property $P^2 = P$.
- Vectors already residing in the column space $C(P)$ are unchanged:
  $$
  Px = x = 1 \cdot x \implies \lambda = 1
  $$
  The column space $C(P)$ is the eigenspace for $\lambda = 1$.
- Vectors in the nullspace $N(P)$ are mapped to zero:
  $$
  Px = 0 = 0 \cdot x \implies \lambda = 0
  $$
  The nullspace $N(P)$ is the eigenspace for $\lambda = 0$.

> [!theorem] Spectrum of a Projection Matrix
> The only possible eigenvalues of a projection matrix $P$ are $\lambda = 1$ and $\lambda = 0$.
> 
> **Proof:**
> Let $Px = \lambda x$ with $x \ne 0$. Multiplying by $P$:
> $$
> P^2 x = P(Px) = P(\lambda x) = \lambda (Px) = \lambda^2 x
> $$
> Since $P^2 = P$:
> $$
> \lambda^2 x = \lambda x \implies (\lambda^2 - \lambda)x = 0
> $$
> Because $x \ne 0$, $\lambda^2 - \lambda = 0$, yielding $\lambda(\lambda - 1) = 0$, so $\lambda = 0$ or $\lambda = 1$.

### Reflection Matrices
A reflection matrix $R$ across a subspace can be written in terms of the projection matrix $P$ onto that subspace as $R = 2P - I$:
- For vectors $x \in C(P)$ lying on the mirror hyperplane:
  $$
  Rx = (2P - I)x = 2Px - x = 2x - x = 1 \cdot x \implies \lambda = 1
  $$
- For vectors $x \in N(P)$ perpendicular to the mirror hyperplane:
  $$
  Rx = (2P - I)x = 2(0) - x = -1 \cdot x \implies \lambda = -1
  $$
The eigenvectors of $R = 2P - I$ are identical to the eigenvectors of $P$, with eigenvalues shifted to $1$ and $-1$.

### Rotation Matrices and Complex Spectrum
A plane rotation by an angle $\theta \ne k\pi$:
$$
Q = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}
$$
rotates every non-zero vector in $\mathbb{R}^2$, meaning no real line can be invariant ($Qx \ne \lambda x$ for real $\lambda$ and $x \in \mathbb{R}^2$).
- Characteristic equation:
  $$
  \det(Q - \lambda I) = (\cos\theta - \lambda)^2 + \sin^2\theta = \lambda^2 - 2\lambda\cos\theta + 1 = 0
  $$
- The roots are complex conjugate pairs of unit magnitude on the unit circle:
  $$
  \lambda = \cos\theta \pm i\sin\theta = e^{\pm i\theta}
  $$
Even when a matrix has purely real entries, its eigenvalues and eigenvectors can be intrinsically complex.

## The Characteristic Equation: $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$

To determine eigenvalues and eigenvectors systematically without prior geometric knowledge, rewrite the defining relation:
$$
Ax = \lambda x \iff (A - \lambda I)x = 0
$$

> [!theorem] The Eigenvalue Condition
> A scalar $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ if and only if the shifted matrix $(A - \lambda I)$ is singular:
> $$
> \det(A - \lambda I) = 0
> $$

**Proof:**
By definition, an eigenvector $x$ must be non-zero ($x \ne 0$). The homogeneous system $(A - \lambda I)x = 0$ admits a non-trivial solution if and only if the nullspace $N(A - \lambda I)$ contains non-zero vectors. By the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra and invertibility criteria, a square matrix possesses a non-trivial nullspace if and only if it is non-invertible (singular), which is equivalent to $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$.

> [!abstract] Definition: Characteristic Polynomial
> The determinant:
> $$
> p(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I)
> $$
> is a polynomial in $\lambda$ of degree $n$, known as the **characteristic polynomial** of $A$. The equation $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ is the **characteristic equation**.

By the **Fundamental Theorem of Algebra**, any polynomial of degree $n$ with complex coefficients factors completely into $n$ linear roots over the complex field $\mathbb{C}$:
$$
\det(A - \lambda I) = (-1)^n (\lambda - \lambda_1)(\lambda - \lambda_2)\cdots(\lambda - \lambda_n) = 0
$$
where $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_n$ are the eigenvalues, counted with their **algebraic multiplicities**.

### The Three-Step Algorithm for Finding Eigensystems
1. **Characteristic Polynomial:** Subtract $\lambda$ along the main diagonal of $A$ and compute $\det(A - \lambda I)$.
2. **Eigenvalues:** Solve the characteristic equation $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ for the $n$ roots $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$.
3. **Eigenvectors:** For each eigenvalue $\lambda_i$, find non-trivial solutions to $(A - \lambda_i I)x = 0$ by finding a basis for $N(A - \lambda_i I)$ using Gaussian elimination.

> [!note] Eigenspace Computation for $2 \times 2$ Singular Matrices
> When $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of a $2 \times 2$ matrix, the matrix $(A - \lambda I)$ is singular, so its rows are linearly dependent. If a non-zero row is $(a, b)$, the orthogonal vector in its nullspace is:
> $$
> x = \begin{bmatrix} -b \\ a \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{or} \quad \begin{bmatrix} b \\ -a \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> which automatically satisfies $\begin{bmatrix} a & b \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -b \\ a \end{bmatrix} = -ab + ba = 0$.

> [!warning] Multiplicities and Defective Matrices
> An eigenvalue may occur as a repeated root of the characteristic polynomial:
> - **Algebraic Multiplicity ($AM$):** The multiplicity of $\lambda$ as a root of $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$.
> - **Geometric Multiplicity ($GM$):** The number of linearly independent eigenvectors associated with $\lambda$, given by the dimension of the nullspace $\dim N(A - \lambda I)$.
> 
> The inequality $1 \le GM \le AM$ always holds. If $GM < AM$ for any eigenvalue, the matrix is **defective** (it lacks a full set of $n$ independent eigenvectors and cannot be diagonalized).

## Core Algebraic Properties of the Eigensystem

### Property 1: Powers of a Matrix
If $x$ is an eigenvector of $A$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$, then $x$ is an eigenvector of $A^n$ with eigenvalue $\lambda^n$ for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$:
$$
A^n x = \lambda^n x
$$

**Proof:**
We prove by induction. The base case $n = 1$ is $Ax = \lambda x$. Assuming $A^k x = \lambda^k x$, multiplying by $A$:
$$
A^{k+1} x = A(A^k x) = A(\lambda^k x) = \lambda^k (Ax) = \lambda^k (\lambda x) = \lambda^{k+1} x
$$
Hence the statement holds for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

### Property 2: Inverses of Matrices
If $A$ is invertible, then $\lambda \ne 0$, and $x$ is an eigenvector of $A^{-1}$ with eigenvalue $\lambda^{-1}$:
$$
A^{-1} x = \frac{1}{\lambda} x
$$

**Proof:**
Multiply $Ax = \lambda x$ by $A^{-1}$:
$$
A^{-1}(Ax) = A^{-1}(\lambda x) \implies x = \lambda (A^{-1}x)
$$
Since $A$ is invertible, $\det(A) \ne 0 \implies \lambda \ne 0$. Dividing by $\lambda$:
$$
A^{-1} x = \frac{1}{\lambda} x
$$

### Property 3: Diagonal Shift
Adding or subtracting a scalar multiple of the identity matrix shifts every eigenvalue by that scalar without altering the eigenvectors.

> [!theorem] Diagonal Shift Theorem
> If $A$ has eigenvalue $\lambda$ corresponding to eigenvector $x$, then $(A + cI)$ has eigenvalue $\lambda + c$ corresponding to the exact same eigenvector $x$:
> $$
> (A + cI)x = (\lambda + c)x
> $$

**Proof:**
Expanding by linearity:
$$
(A + cI)x = Ax + cIx = \lambda x + cx = (\lambda + c)x
$$

### Property 4: Singular Matrices and Zero Eigenvalues
A matrix $A$ is singular if and only if $\lambda = 0$ is an eigenvalue.

**Proof:**
$A$ is singular $\iff \det(A) = 0 \iff \det(A - 0 \cdot I) = 0$. Thus $\lambda = 0$ satisfies the characteristic equation. Furthermore:
$$
Ax = 0 \cdot x = 0 \iff x \in N(A)
$$
The eigenspace for $\lambda = 0$ is precisely the nullspace $N(A)$. The geometric multiplicity of $\lambda = 0$ is the nullity $n - \operatorname{rank}(A)$.

### Property 5: Triangular Matrices
For an upper triangular or lower triangular matrix $U$, the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries:
$$
\lambda_i = u_{ii}
$$

**Proof:**
The matrix $(U - \lambda I)$ is also triangular, with diagonal elements $(u_{11} - \lambda), (u_{22} - \lambda), \dots, (u_{nn} - \lambda)$. The determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries:
$$
\det(U - \lambda I) = (u_{11} - \lambda)(u_{22} - \lambda)\cdots(u_{nn} - \lambda) = 0
$$
The roots are precisely the diagonal entries $\lambda = u_{ii}$.

> [!warning] Row Operations Do NOT Preserve Eigenvalues
> While Gaussian elimination preserves the determinant and row space, it **does not preserve eigenvalues**:
> $$
> A \xrightarrow{\text{elimination}} U \centernot\implies \operatorname{eig}(A) = \operatorname{eig}(U)
> $$
> The pivots of $A$ (the diagonal elements of $U$) are generally completely different from the eigenvalues of $A$. Row exchanges and row additions disrupt the eigensystem.

## Trace and Determinant of Matrices

Two essential quantities provide direct global constraints on the spectrum of any square matrix:

> [!abstract] Definition: Trace of a Matrix
> The **trace** of an $n \times n$ matrix $A$, denoted $\operatorname{tr}(A)$, is the sum of the entries on its main diagonal:
> $$
> \operatorname{tr}(A) = \sum_{i=1}^n a_{ii} = a_{11} + a_{22} + \dots + a_{nn}
> $$

### Cyclic Property of the Trace

> [!theorem] Cyclic Invariance: $\operatorname{tr}(AB) = \operatorname{tr}(BA)$
> For any matrices $A \in \mathbb{C}^{m \times n}$ and $B \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times m}$, the product matrices $AB$ and $BA$ satisfy:
> $$
> \operatorname{tr}(AB) = \operatorname{tr}(BA)
> $$

**Proof:**
By matrix multiplication, the $i^{\text{th}}$ diagonal entry of the $m \times m$ matrix $AB$ is $(AB)_{ii} = \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} b_{ji}$. Summing over all $i = 1, \dots, m$:
$$
\operatorname{tr}(AB) = \sum_{i=1}^m (AB)_{ii} = \sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} b_{ji}
$$
Similarly, the $j^{\text{th}}$ diagonal entry of the $n \times n$ matrix $BA$ is $(BA)_{jj} = \sum_{i=1}^m b_{ji} a_{ij}$. Summing over all $j = 1, \dots, n$:
$$
\operatorname{tr}(BA) = \sum_{j=1}^n (BA)_{jj} = \sum_{j=1}^n \sum_{i=1}^m b_{ji} a_{ij}
$$
Because scalar multiplication commutes ($a_{ij} b_{ji} = b_{ji} a_{ij}$) and finite double summations can be interchanged:
$$
\operatorname{tr}(AB) = \sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} b_{ji} = \sum_{j=1}^n \sum_{i=1}^m b_{ji} a_{ij} = \operatorname{tr}(BA)
$$

### Product of Eigenvalues
> [!theorem] Determinant as the Product of Eigenvalues
> For any $n \times n$ matrix $A$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_n$:
> $$
> \det(A) = \prod_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = \lambda_1 \lambda_2 \cdots \lambda_n
> $$

**Proof:**
The characteristic polynomial factors as:
$$
\det(A - \lambda I) = (-1)^n (\lambda - \lambda_1)(\lambda - \lambda_2)\cdots(\lambda - \lambda_n)
$$
Evaluating this identity at $\lambda = 0$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\det(A - 0 \cdot I) &= (-1)^n (0 - \lambda_1)(0 - \lambda_2)\cdots(0 - \lambda_n) \\
\det(A) &= (-1)^n (-\lambda_1)(-\lambda_2)\cdots(-\lambda_n) \\
&= (-1)^n (-1)^n (\lambda_1 \lambda_2 \cdots \lambda_n) \\
&= (-1)^{2n} \prod_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = \prod_{i=1}^n \lambda_i
\end{aligned}
$$

### Sum of Eigenvalues
> [!theorem] Trace as the Sum of Eigenvalues
> For any $n \times n$ matrix $A$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_n$:
> $$
> \operatorname{tr}(A) = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \dots + \lambda_n
> $$

**Proof:**
Consider the coefficient of $(-\lambda)^{n-1}$ in the characteristic polynomial expanded from two perspectives:
1. **Product Factorization:**
   $$
   \det(A - \lambda I) = \prod_{i=1}^n (\lambda_i - \lambda) = (-\lambda)^n + \left(\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i\right)(-\lambda)^{n-1} + \dots + \prod_{i=1}^n \lambda_i
   $$
2. **Leibniz Determinant Expansion:**
   In the determinant of $(A - \lambda I)$, the only term containing $(-\lambda)^{n-1}$ or $(-\lambda)^n$ arises from the main diagonal product:
   $$
   (a_{11} - \lambda)(a_{22} - \lambda)\cdots(a_{nn} - \lambda) = (-\lambda)^n + \left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_{ii}\right)(-\lambda)^{n-1} + \mathcal{O}(\lambda^{n-2})
   $$
   All other permutation products contain at most $n-2$ diagonal factors and contribute terms of degree at most $n-2$ in $\lambda$.

Equating the coefficients of $(-\lambda)^{n-1}$ from both expressions:
$$
\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = \sum_{i=1}^n a_{ii} = \operatorname{tr}(A)
$$

### Characteristic Equation for $2 \times 2$ Matrices
For any $2 \times 2$ matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$, the characteristic polynomial is determined entirely by the trace and determinant:
$$
\det(A - \lambda I) = (a - \lambda)(d - \lambda) - bc = \lambda^2 - (a + d)\lambda + (ad - bc)
$$
$$
\lambda^2 - \operatorname{tr}(A)\lambda + \det(A) = 0
$$
The eigenvalues are given by the quadratic formula:
$$
\lambda = \frac{\operatorname{tr}(A) \pm \sqrt{(\operatorname{tr}(A))^2 - 4\det(A)}}{2}
$$

## Spectral Properties of Special Matrix Classes

The algebraic structure of a matrix imposes strict geometric constraints on its eigenvalues:

| Matrix Family | Algebraic Definition | Nature of Eigenvalues $\lambda$ | Nature of Eigenvectors $x$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Symmetric Matrix** | $S^T = S$ | Purely real: $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ | Mutually orthogonal |
| **Skew-Symmetric Matrix** | $A^T = -A$ | Purely imaginary: $\lambda = ib$ ($b \in \mathbb{R}$) | Mutually orthogonal |
| **Orthogonal Matrix** | $Q^T Q = I$ | Unit magnitude: $\lvert\lambda\rvert = 1$ ($e^{i\theta}$) | Mutually orthogonal |
| **Markov Matrix** | $M_{ij} \ge 0, \sum_{i} M_{ij} = 1$ | $\lambda_{\max} = 1$, all $\lvert\lambda_i\rvert \le 1$ | Steady-state eigenvector $x_1 \ge 0$ |
| **Projection Matrix** | $P^2 = P, P^T = P$ | $\lambda \in \{0, 1\}$ | Orthogonal subspaces ($C(P) \perp N(P)$) |

> [!note] Analogy with the Complex Plane
> The eigenvalue behavior of these fundamental matrix classes mirrors the complex plane:
> - **Symmetric matrices** correspond to the **real line** ($\operatorname{Re}(z)$).
> - **Skew-symmetric matrices** correspond to the **imaginary axis** ($\operatorname{Im}(z)$).
> - **Orthogonal matrices** correspond to the **unit circle** ($|z| = 1$).

## Commuting Matrices and Simultaneous Eigenvectors

### The Fallacy of Naive Addition and Multiplication
For general matrices $A$ and $B$:
- The eigenvalues of $A + B$ are **not** $\lambda_A + \lambda_B$.
- The eigenvalues of $AB$ are **not** $\lambda_A \lambda_B$.

The naive assertion $ABx = A(\beta x) = \beta (Ax) = \beta \lambda x$ is false because it relies on the unfounded premise that $A$ and $B$ share the exact same eigenvector $x$. In general, the eigenvectors of $A$ and $B$ are completely distinct.

### Simultaneous Diagonalizability Theorem

> [!theorem] Commutativity and Shared Eigenvectors
> Two matrices $A$ and $B$ share a complete set of $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors if and only if they commute:
> $$
> AB = BA
> $$

**Proof:**
1. **Shared Eigenvectors $\implies AB = BA$:**
   Suppose $A$ and $B$ share $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors $x_1, \dots, x_n$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ and $\beta_i$ respectively:
   $$
   Ax_i = \lambda_i x_i \quad \text{and} \quad Bx_i = \beta_i x_i
   $$
   Evaluating the composite transformations on each eigenvector:
   $$
   ABx_i = A(\beta_i x_i) = \beta_i Ax_i = \beta_i \lambda_i x_i = \lambda_i \beta_i x_i
   $$
   $$
   BAx_i = B(\lambda_i x_i) = \lambda_i Bx_i = \lambda_i \beta_i x_i
   $$
   Thus $ABx_i = BAx_i$ for every basis vector $x_i$. Because $AB$ and $BA$ agree on an entire basis of $\mathbb{C}^n$, they are identical operators: $AB = BA$.

2. **$AB = BA \implies$ Shared Eigenvectors (Distinct Eigenvalues Case):**
   Assume $A$ has $n$ distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ with corresponding eigenvectors $x_1, \dots, x_n$. For any eigenvector $x_i$ of $A$:
   $$
   Ax_i = \lambda_i x_i
   $$
   Multiply by $B$ and use commutativity $BA = AB$:
   $$
   A(Bx_i) = (AB)x_i = (BA)x_i = B(Ax_i) = B(\lambda_i x_i) = \lambda_i (Bx_i)
   $$
   This proves that $Bx_i$ is also an eigenvector of $A$ corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda_i$. Because $\lambda_i$ is distinct, its eigenspace is one-dimensional ($E_{\lambda_i} = \operatorname{span}\{x_i\}$). Therefore, $Bx_i$ must be a scalar multiple of $x_i$:
   $$
   Bx_i = \beta_i x_i
   $$
   Hence, $x_i$ is simultaneously an eigenvector of $B$.

> [!note] Connection to Quantum Mechanics: Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle
> In quantum mechanics, observable physical quantities are represented by self-adjoint operators. If two operators commute ($AB = BA$), they possess simultaneous eigenvectors, meaning both physical observables can be determined simultaneously with arbitrary precision.
> 
> When two operators do not commute, such as the position operator $P$ and momentum operator $Q$:
> $$
> QP - PQ = I
> $$
> they cannot share a common basis of eigenvectors. No non-trivial state vector can satisfy $Px = 0$ and $Qx = 0$ simultaneously (as that would require $(QP - PQ)x = 0 = Ix \implies x = 0$). This non-commutativity establishes the **Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle**:
> $$
> \|Px\| \|Qx\| \ge \frac{1}{2} \|x\|^2
> $$

## Estimating Eigenvalues: The Gershgorin Circle Theorem

Calculating the exact roots of $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ for high-dimensional matrices is computationally expensive. The Gershgorin Circle Theorem provides a rigorous geometric bound on where eigenvalues reside in the complex plane directly from matrix entries.

> [!theorem] Gershgorin Circle Theorem
> Every eigenvalue of an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ lies within at least one of the closed disks $D_i$ in the complex plane centered at the diagonal entry $a_{ii}$ with radius equal to the sum of the absolute values of the remaining row entries:
> $$
> \lambda \in \bigcup_{i=1}^n D_i \quad \text{where} \quad D_i = \left\{ z \in \mathbb{C} : |z - a_{ii}| \le R_i \right\}
> $$
> with row radius:
> $$
> R_i = \sum_{\substack{j=1 \\ j \ne i}}^n |a_{ij}|
> $$

**Proof:**
Let $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue of $A$ with eigenvector $x \ne 0$:
$$
Ax = \lambda x \iff \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} x_j = \lambda x_i \quad \text{for all } i = 1, \dots, n
$$
Choose index $k$ such that $|x_k|$ is the coordinate of maximal magnitude:
$$
|x_k| = \max_{1 \le j \le n} |x_j| > 0
$$
Extract the $k$-th equation:
$$
a_{kk} x_k + \sum_{j \ne k} a_{kj} x_j = \lambda x_k \implies (\lambda - a_{kk}) x_k = \sum_{j \ne k} a_{kj} x_j
$$
Taking the absolute value of both sides and applying the triangle inequality:
$$
|\lambda - a_{kk}| |x_k| = \left| \sum_{j \ne k} a_{kj} x_j \right| \le \sum_{j \ne k} |a_{kj}| |x_j|
$$
Since $|x_j| \le |x_k|$ for all $j$:
$$
|\lambda - a_{kk}| |x_k| \le \left( \sum_{j \ne k} |a_{kj}| \right) |x_k| = R_k |x_k|
$$
Dividing by $|x_k| > 0$:
$$
|\lambda - a_{kk}| \le R_k
$$
Thus $\lambda$ lies within the Gershgorin disk $D_k$. Since every eigenvalue must satisfy this condition for some coordinate index $k$, every eigenvalue lies in the union $\bigcup_{i=1}^n D_i$.
