# Solving Linear Equations

$$Ax = b$$

- In this chapter we primarily focus on $n \times n$ matrix $A$ and the $n$ equations that form in $Ax = b$.

## 2.1 Elimination and Back Substitution

- It is a systematic way to solve equation $Ax = b$ by simplifying it to the form $Ux = c$ and then using back substitution.
- $U$: upper triangular matrix
- To simplify $A$ we use row operations (on both sides of equation) to convert $A$ to $U$ & $b$ to $c$.
- **Note:** row operations simply follow the rules of equation solving i.e. we can add/subtract equations & we can multiply both sides of an equation with a non-zero value.

  These steps manipulate the equations giving us new equations, but they don't change the final solution i.e. these don't add or remove solutions.

### Example

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 4 & 11 & 14 \\ 2 & 8 & 17 \end{bmatrix}, \quad b = \begin{bmatrix} 19 \\ 55 \\ 50 \end{bmatrix}$$

- The row operations we represent as matrix $E_{ij}$ which tells us that this matrix eliminates $A_{ij}$ to 0.

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 4 & 11 & 14 \\ 2 & 8 & 17 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{E_{21}} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 5 & 6 \\ 2 & 8 & 17 \end{bmatrix}$$

- In words we say that we subtracted 2 times row 1 from row 2.
- As a matrix operation we can write

$$E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$E_{21} A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 4 & 11 & 14 \\ 2 & 8 & 17 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 5 & 6 \\ 2 & 8 & 17 \end{bmatrix}$$

- Continuing on the process we have.

$$E_{21} A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 5 & 6 \\ 2 & 8 & 17 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{E_{31}} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 5 & 6 \\ 0 & 5 & 13 \end{bmatrix}$$

where

$$E_{31} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$E_{31} E_{21} A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 5 & 6 \\ 0 & 5 & 13 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{E_{32}} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 5 & 6 \\ 0 & 0 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$$

where

$$E_{32} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\Rightarrow\; ==U = E_{32} E_{31} E_{21} A== = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 5 & 6 \\ 0 & 0 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$$

- Now we need to apply the same operations to $b$

$$\Rightarrow\; ==c = E_{32} E_{31} E_{21} b== = \begin{bmatrix} 19 \\ 17 \\ 14 \end{bmatrix}$$

- Solving $Ux = c$ using back substitution.

$$\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 5 & 6 \\ 0 & 0 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 19 \\ 17 \\ 14 \end{bmatrix}$$

- $7z = 14 \Rightarrow z = 2$
- $5y + 6z = 17 \Rightarrow y = 1$
- $2x + 3y + 4z = 19 \Rightarrow x = 4$

$$\boxed{x = (4, 1, 2)} \text{ is a solution to } Ax = b.$$

### Points to Note

**(i)** If all the $n$ diagonal values (called pivots) are non zero, then we can say that a solution will always exist

- The matrix $A$ is invertible
- The rank of $A$ is full rank ($r = n$)
- The columns (& rows) of $A$ are independent.

**(ii)** If any of the pivot values is 0, then there may be no solution (or) there will be infinitely many.

- Columns of $A$ are dependent $\Rightarrow Ax = 0$ where $x$ is not the $0$ vector.
  - $A(\alpha x) = 0$ (for some constant $\alpha$)
- There are infinitely many solutions to $Ax = 0$, usually written as $AX = 0$
- Now consider $Ax = b$ to have a solution i.e. $x$ is a solution to $Ax = b$
  - $\Rightarrow x + \alpha X$ is also a solution
  - $\Rightarrow A(x + \alpha X) = Ax + A(\alpha X) = Ax + 0 = Ax = b$
- Hence ==$A(x + \alpha X)$== gives infinitely many solutions.

**(iii)** If any pivot is 0, then rank of $A$ is $< n$. The columns & rows are dependent.

**(iv)** From the example, the matrices $E_{21}, E_{31}, E_{32}$ can be combined into a single matrix $E$ called the **elimination matrix**.

$$\Rightarrow\; ==E = E_{32} E_{31} E_{21}==$$

$$\Rightarrow\; E_{32} E_{31} E_{21} A = EA = U$$

- Note the order of operations must be the same, otherwise we will get wrong result.

### Role of Permutation Matrix

- Sometimes we run into a temporary failure where we get 0 at a pivot position, but there is a non-zero value in that column below that row.
- In this case we need to apply a permutation matrix operation to solve the issue.

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{E_{21}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & 5 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \xleftarrow{P_{23}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$$

where

$$P_{23} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\boxed{U = P_{23} E_{21} A}$$

### Augmented Matrix

- To make it easy to apply row operations to $A$ & $b$ we create a single matrix $[A \;\; b]$ called the ==augmented matrix==.
- Using this we can perform elimination of both $A$ & $b$ at the same time & place.

### $PA = LU$

- The final equation that describes the elimination process is $PA = LU$, where $P$ is the permutation matrix in case there were any row exchanges during the elimination process.
- $L = E^{-1}$, where $E$ is the elimination matrix.
- If $E = E_{32} E_{31} E_{21}$, then we have $L = (E_{32} E_{31} E_{21})^{-1}$

$$\boxed{==L = E_{21}^{-1} E_{31}^{-1} E_{32}^{-1}==}$$

- The inverse reverses the order.

**Note:** One thing that we can note is that $E_{21}, E_{31}, E_{32}$ are all lower triangular matrices since all operations are with previous rows.

- Their product $E$ is also lower triangular.
- $E^{-1} = L$ is also lower triangular provided that ==$E$ is invertible which it is==.

### Block Multiplication

- This is yet another way of thinking about matrix multiplication.
- We can divide the matrix into blocks, for example consider 2 $n \times n$ matrices $A$ & $B$ ($n$ is even)
- We divide $A$ & $B$ into 4 $\frac{n}{2} \times \frac{n}{2}$ matrices.

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} A_1 & A_2 \\ A_3 & A_4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} B_1 & B_2 \\ B_3 & B_4 \end{bmatrix}$$

- Let $AB = C$, then $C$ is also $n \times n$

$$C = \begin{bmatrix} C_1 & C_2 \\ C_3 & C_4 \end{bmatrix}$$

- Here $==C_1 = A_1 B_1 + A_2 B_3==$
- We can look at this as row blocks of $A$ multiplied with column blocks of $B$.

## 2.2 Elimination Matrices and Inverse Matrices

### Inverse of Matrix

- Inverse of a square matrix $A$ is written as $A^{-1}$.
- Mathematically, whatever $A$ does, $A^{-1}$ undoes.
- Ex: $AA^{-1} = I$
- ==Not all matrices have inverses, for inverse to exist the columns (hence rows) of $A$ must be independent.==

**Notes about Inverses:**

**(i)** The inverse exists if and only if elimination produces $n$ pivots.

**(ii)** ==The matrix $A$ cannot have two different inverses i.e. if $BA = I$ & $AC = I$, then $B = C$==

*proof:* $(BA)C = C \Rightarrow B(AC) = BI \Rightarrow \boxed{B = C}$

**(iii)** If $A$ is invertible then the one and only solution to $\boxed{Ax = b}$ is $x = A^{-1}b$

**(iv)** ==Suppose there is a non-zero vector $x$ such that $Ax = 0$, then $A$ has dependent columns.== It cannot have an inverse. No matrix can bring back $0$ to $x$.

$$Ax = 0 \Rightarrow A^{-1}Ax = A^{-1}0$$

$$Ix = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0$$

$\Rightarrow$ But we assumed that $x \neq 0$, hence $A^{-1}$ does not exist.

**(v)** A 2 by 2 matrix is invertible if and only if $ad - bc \neq 0$

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc} \begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}$$

- The number $ad - bc$ is the **determinant** of $A$.

**(vi)** A triangular matrix has an inverse provided no diagonal entries $d_i$ are zero.

**(vii)** For square matrices, an inverse on one side is automatically an inverse on the other side

$$\Rightarrow\; ==\text{If } A^{-1}A = I \Rightarrow AA^{-1} = I.==$$

### The Inverse of Product $AB$

If $A$ and $B$ are both invertible then ==inverse of the product $AB$== is $B^{-1}A^{-1}$

$$(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AIA^{-1}$$

$$\Rightarrow AA^{-1} = I.$$

- This result is very intuitive: to undo what $AB$ does, first we need to undo what $B$ did and then what $A$ did.

### Elimination Matrix $E$ & Its Inverse

- In each step of elimination we multiply $l_{ij}$ to row $j$ & subtract from row $i$, the matrix that does this transformation is given by $E_{ij}$

$$E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -l_{21} & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad E_{31} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -l_{31} & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad E_{32} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -l_{32} & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\Rightarrow E_{32} E_{31} E_{21} A = U$$

- Assuming no row exchange required

$$\Rightarrow E = E_{32} E_{31} E_{21}$$

- After multiplying we get

$$E = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -l_{21} & 1 & 0 \\ (l_{32}l_{21} - l_{31}) & -l_{32} & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

- The inverse of $E$ undoes what $E$ does in reverse order

$$\Rightarrow E^{-1} = E_{21}^{-1} E_{31}^{-1} E_{32}^{-1}$$

- Multiplying this we get

$$E^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ l_{21} & 1 & 0 \\ l_{31} & l_{32} & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

- This is much more neat than $E$, this matrix has a special name: it is called $L$.

$$\Rightarrow EA = U \quad \& \quad A = LU$$

### Geometric Interpretation of Row Operations

- Row operations create a new system of equations with the same solution.
- When done in a way that aligns the new extra equations with the axis we solve the equation and obtain the solution to the new (& original) system.

**Example:** Consider the following system.

$$x + y = 3 \quad \to \text{(i)}$$
$$x + 2y = 5 \quad \to \text{(ii)}$$
$$\text{subtract (ii) from (i):}\quad -y = -2 \;\;(\text{or})\;\; y = 2 \quad \to \text{(iii)}$$

> [!todo] Figure placeholder
> 2D axes showing lines (i), (ii) and (iii) all passing through one common point. Line (iii) is horizontal ($\parallel$ to $x$ axis).

- If we plot (i), (ii) & (iii) on the graph, we will see that eliminating $x$ from the system gave a new line which is $\parallel$ to $x$ axis (more aligned).

**Example:** Consider the following system obtained after elimination

$$3x + 4y + 5z = 4 \quad \to \text{(i)}$$
$$7y + 3z = 1 \quad \to \text{(ii)}$$
$$z = 6 \quad \to \text{(iii)}$$

- After the first step of elimination we obtain equation (ii), we can see it is more aligned as this plane is $\parallel$ to $x$ axis, but passes through the original solution.
- The equation (iii) is even more aligned: it is $\parallel$ to both $x$ & $y$ axis but still passes through the solution.
- The modified (iii) equation having only one component gives us one part of the solution directly, which we put in equation (ii) to get $y$ & finally get $x$.

**(39)** If $A = I - uv^T$, then $A^{-1} = I + uv^T(1 - v^Tu)^{-1}$. Show that $AA^{-1} = I$ except $Au = 0$ when $v^Tu = 1$.

## How To Compute $A^{-1}$

- One of the ways to compute $A^{-1}$ is using **Gauss-Jordan Elimination** on augmented $A$.
- Consider $AA^{-1} = I$, let

$$A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & x_2 & \cdots & x_n \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\Rightarrow AA^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} Ax_1 & Ax_2 & \cdots & Ax_n \end{bmatrix} = I$$

$$\Rightarrow Ax_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad Ax_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \;\; \& \text{ so on}$$

- Hence to obtain columns $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ for $A^{-1}$ we need to solve each of these systems of equations.
- But since each of these has the same coefficient matrix, we can do this by creating a single augmented matrix $[A \;\; I]$ & applying elimination on it.

### Gauss-Jordan Elimination

This doesn't stop after converting $A$ to $U$, it moves further & converts $U$ to $I$, so essentially we do $A \to I$.

- When $A$ is converted to $I$, then the right side directly gives the solution.

$$Ax = b \Rightarrow A^{-1}Ax = A^{-1}b$$

$$Ix = A^{-1}b \Rightarrow x = A^{-1}b$$

$$\Rightarrow \text{Hence } \boxed{Ix = c \text{ gives } x = c}$$

- The augmented matrix $[A \;\; I]$: this contains all equations $Ax_i = I_i$ as seen earlier.
- Hence when we convert $A$ to $I$ we get $A^{-1}$ on the other block.

$$[A \;\; I] \Rightarrow [I \;\; A^{-1}]$$

- Let $E$ be the elimination matrix that takes $A \to I$, then

$$E[A \;\; I] = [EA \;\; EI] = [I \;\; E], \text{ but since } EA = I$$

$$\Rightarrow E = A^{-1}.$$

### Matrix Computation Cost

- Cost of taking $A$ to $U$ is $\frac{n^3}{3}$ multiplications & $\frac{n^3}{3}$ subtractions for large $n$.
- On the right hand side we have $b \to c$ that takes about $n^2$ operations & then back substitution again takes $n^2$ computations.
- For large $n$ it can be extremely expensive to achieve this in reasonable time. It might not even be possible without the use of a super computer.

### $A = LU$ General Proof

We have already seen this for a $3 \times 3$ matrix, but now let's see this for a general case.

- The primary insight is that $l_{ij}$ is multiplied to row $j$ and subtracted from row $i$.
- But these rows are not the original rows of $A$, they may be modified. ==(They are all rows of $U$)==
- The thing we are sure of is that pivot rows are not modified further & $l_{ij}$ always multiplies with a pivot row (i.e. row $j$).
- Consider the $j$th row of $U$, how is it made: it starts as the $j$th row of $A$, then in first part of elimination gets modified by $l_{j1}$ times row 1 of $A$.
- This is the only time we see a row of $A$ as pivot. From now on all rows are modified rows of $A$, but ==since pivot rows don't change they are rows of $U$==. Even row 1 of $A$ is row 1 of $U$.

$$\text{row } j \text{ of } U = (\text{row } j \text{ of } A) - l_{j1}(\text{row 1 of } U) - l_{j2}(\text{row 2 of } U) - l_{j3}(\text{row 3 of } U) - \cdots - l_{j,j-1}(\text{row } j{-}1 \text{ of } U)$$

$$\Rightarrow \text{row } j \text{ of } A = (\text{row } j \text{ of } U) + l_{j1}(\text{row 1 of } U) + l_{j2}(\text{row 2 of } U) + \cdots + l_{j,j-1}(\text{row } j{-}1 \text{ of } U)$$

$$\Rightarrow \text{row } j \text{ of } A = \begin{bmatrix} l_{j1} & l_{j2} & \cdots & l_{j,j-1} & 1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \end{bmatrix} U$$

- If we write this for each $j$ we get

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ l_{21} & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ l_{31} & l_{32} & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & & & \ddots & \\ l_{n1} & l_{n2} & l_{n3} & \cdots & 1 \end{bmatrix} U$$

$$\boxed{A = LU}$$

### Second Proof of $A = LU$

- In the first step of elimination, we choose $l_{11} = 1,\; l_{21} = \frac{a_{21}}{a_{11}},\; l_{31} = \frac{a_{31}}{a_{11}},\; \ldots,\; l_{n1} = \frac{a_{n1}}{a_{11}}$

  then we create a matrix in which rows are multiples of (row 1 of $A$) i.e.

$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot (\text{row } 1) \\ l_{21} (\text{row } 1) \\ l_{31} (\text{row } 1) \\ \vdots \\ l_{n1} (\text{row } 1) \end{bmatrix}$$

- If we subtract the above matrix from $A$, we get $A_2$.

$$A_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & x & \cdots & x \\ \vdots & x & \cdots & x \\ 0 & x & \cdots & x \end{bmatrix}$$

- For the next step we construct a new matrix with $l_{12} = 0,\; l_{22} = 1,\; l_{32} = \frac{a'_{32}}{a'_{22}},\; l_{42} = \frac{a'_{42}}{a'_{22}},\; \ldots,\; l_{n2} = \frac{a'_{n2}}{a'_{22}}$.
- Here $a'$ is the element of $A_2$, the matrix now is

$$\begin{bmatrix} 0 \cdot (\text{row } 2 \text{ of } A_2) \\ 1 \cdot (\text{row } 2 \text{ of } A_2) \\ l_{32} (\text{row } 2 \text{ of } A_2) \\ \vdots \\ l_{n2} (\text{row } 2 \text{ of } A_2) \end{bmatrix}$$

- If we subtract this from $A_2$, we will get $A_3$ & so on.
- If we continue the process we will finally get 0.
- The matrix in each step can be written as the product of a column & row. i.e.

$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ l_{21} \\ l_{31} \\ \vdots \\ l_{n1} \end{bmatrix} [\text{row } 1 \text{ of } A] \quad \text{and,} \quad \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ l_{32} \\ \vdots \\ l_{n2} \end{bmatrix} [\text{row } 2 \text{ of } A_2]$$

  & so on.

- The columns are columns of $L$ & the rows are rows of $U$.

$$A - l_1u_1 - l_2u_2 - \cdots - l_nu_n = 0$$

$$\Rightarrow A = l_1u_1 + l_2u_2 + \cdots + l_nu_n$$

$$\boxed{A = LU}$$

**Q)** When is $A = LU$ possible without any row exchanges.

**A)** When all upper left $k$ by $k$ matrices of $A$ are invertible.

- For $L_1^{-1}LD$ to be diagonal, $L_1^{-1}L$ must be diagonal & since $L_1^{-1}$, $L$ are also unit lower triangular, hence $L_1^{-1}L$ must be unit diagonal

$$\Rightarrow L_1^{-1}L = I \Rightarrow \boxed{L = L_1}$$

- With similar reasoning we have

$$U_1U^{-1} = I \Rightarrow \boxed{U = U_1}$$

$$\Rightarrow L_1^{-1}LD = D_1U_1U^{-1} \Rightarrow \boxed{D = D_1}$$

## Permutations and Transposes

Permutation matrices have 1 in every row and 1 in every column, they are also square matrices.

- Some facts about permutation matrices
  - **(i)** There are $n!$ $n \times n$ permutation matrices
  - **(ii)** Half of them are even & other half are odd.
  - **(iii)** The columns of $P$ are orthogonal to each other (as their dot product is 0).
  - **(iv)** The product $P_1P_2$ is a permutation, so is $P^{-1}$.
  - **(v)** If $A$ is invertible, there is a perm $P$ to order its rows in advance, so that elimination on $PA$ meets no zero in pivot position. $\boxed{\text{Then } PA = LU}$
  - **(vi)** If we want to permute the columns then we need to multiply $P$ on the right.
    - $\Rightarrow PAQ$ has row & column permutations

**Note:** If we think about all possible permutations of $A$ we can create using $PAQ$, the answer is $n! \times n! = (n!)^2$. For a $3 \times 3$ matrix $A$ this is 36. If we think about all possible arrangements of elements of $A$, then that would be $9!$.

- Clearly $9!$ is way larger than 36, this is because using $PAQ$ we can't break a row or column.
- **Indices:** If we label each row/col with indices, we see this more clearly.

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & i \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{(rows and columns labelled 1, 2, 3)}$$

consider $P = (2\;1\;3)$ & $Q = (3\;1\;2)$

$$PAQ = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & i \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$= \begin{bmatrix} d & e & f \\ a & b & c \\ g & h & i \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{(row labels } 2, 1, 3\text{)}$$

$$= \begin{bmatrix} e & f & d \\ b & c & a \\ h & i & g \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{(column labels } 2, 3, 1\text{; row labels } 2, 1, 3\text{)}$$

- The index down the row/column remains the same after permutations.

### The Transpose of $A$

The transpose of $A$ denoted by $A^T$ is the matrix made by interchanging the rows of $A$ to columns (or vice-versa).

Ex: If

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix}, \quad A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 & 7 \\ 2 & 5 & 8 \\ 3 & 6 & 9 \end{bmatrix}$$

- The flip happens across the main diagonal of the matrix.
- $(A^T)_{ij} = A_{ji}$
- $(A + B)^T = A^T + B^T$
- $(AB)^T = B^TA^T$
- $(A^{-1})^T = (A^T)^{-1}$

### Proof of $(AB)^T = B^TA^T$

The proof is much easier to see for vectors.

$$(Ax)^T = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 \cdot x \\ a_2 \cdot x \\ a_3 \cdot x \end{bmatrix}^T = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 \cdot x & a_2 \cdot x & a_3 \cdot x \end{bmatrix}$$

where $a_i$ is row $i$ of $A$ and $a_i \cdot x$ is the dot product of $a_i$ & $x$.

Now,

$$x^TA^T = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & x_2 & x_3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} | & | & | \\ a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ | & | & | \end{bmatrix}$$

$$x^TA^T = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 \cdot x & a_2 \cdot x & a_3 \cdot x \end{bmatrix} = (Ax)^T$$

- In other words in $Ax$, $x$ combines the columns of $A$ & in $x^TA^T$, $x^T$ combines the rows of $A^T$.
- Moving to $AB$ using the same idea.

Let $B = \begin{bmatrix} | & | & | \\ x_1 & x_2 & x_3 \\ | & | & | \end{bmatrix}$

$$AB = \begin{bmatrix} Ax_1 & Ax_2 & Ax_3 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\Rightarrow (AB)^T = \begin{bmatrix} (Ax_1)^T \\ (Ax_2)^T \\ (Ax_3)^T \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1^TA^T \\ x_2^TA^T \\ x_3^TA^T \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\Rightarrow B^TA^T = \begin{bmatrix} - x_1^T - \\ - x_2^T - \\ - x_3^T - \end{bmatrix} A^T = \begin{bmatrix} x_1^TA^T \\ x_2^TA^T \\ x_3^TA^T \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\boxed{\Rightarrow (AB)^T = B^TA^T}$$

### Proof of $(A^{-1})^T = (A^T)^{-1}$

This is a simple proof. We start with $A^{-1}A = I$

$$\Rightarrow (A^{-1}A)^T = I^T = I$$

$$\Rightarrow A^T(A^{-1})^T = I$$

$$\boxed{\Rightarrow (A^{-1})^T = (A^T)^{-1}}$$

- This also proves that $A^T$ is invertible when $A$ is invertible.

### Inner Product and Outer Product

- A better way to write dot product without using the unprofessional dot.

$$\boxed{x \cdot y = x^Ty}$$

- This is called the **Inner Product**.
- The **outer product** is given by $xy^T$ and is a rank 1 matrix.

### $A^T$ using Inner Product

There is a better, more mathematical way to define $A^T$. It is the matrix that makes these inner products equal

$$\boxed{(Ax)^Ty = x^T(A^Ty)}$$

- Inner product of $Ax$ with $y$ = Inner product of $x$ with $A^Ty$.

### Symmetric Matrices

- A symmetric matrix has $\boxed{S^T = S}$.
- This means that every $s_{ij} = s_{ji}$
- The inverse of a symmetric matrix is symmetric. $\Rightarrow (S^{-1})^T = (S^T)^{-1} = S^{-1}$

### Symmetric Products $A^TA$, $AA^T$, $LDL^T$

- The product $A^TA$ produces a symmetric matrix.

$$\Rightarrow (A^TA)^T = A^T(A^T)^T = A^TA$$

- Similarly $AA^T$ is also symmetric.

$$(AA^T)^T = (A^T)^TA^T = AA^T$$

- Many scientific problems that start with a matrix $A$ which is rectangular end up with $A^TA$ (or) $AA^T$ which are both symmetric.

### Symmetric Matrices in Elimination

Consider the factorization of a symmetric matrix

$$S = LDU \Rightarrow S^T = (LDU)^T$$

$$\Rightarrow S^T = U^TD^TL^T$$

$$\Rightarrow \text{But } S^T = S \Rightarrow LDU = U^TD^TL^T$$

$$\boxed{\Rightarrow L = U^T \;\;(\text{or})\;\; U = L^T}$$

- There is symmetry in the triple product.

$$\boxed{S = LDL^T}$$

**Example:** For a rectangular matrix this saddle-point matrix $S$ is symmetric and important.

$$S = \begin{bmatrix} I & A \\ A^T & 0 \end{bmatrix} = S^T \quad \text{has size } m + n$$

Apply block factorization $S = LDL^T$.

**sol:**

$$S = \begin{bmatrix} I & A \\ A^T & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} I & 0 \\ -A^T & I \end{bmatrix}$$

$$E_{21}S = \begin{bmatrix} I & 0 \\ -A^T & I \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} I & A \\ A^T & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} I & A \\ 0 & -A^TA \end{bmatrix} = U$$

$$DU = \begin{bmatrix} I & 0 \\ 0 & -A^TA \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} I & A \\ 0 & I \end{bmatrix}$$

$$L = \begin{bmatrix} I & 0 \\ A^T & I \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\Rightarrow S = \begin{bmatrix} I & 0 \\ A^T & I \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} I & 0 \\ 0 & -A^TA \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} I & A \\ 0 & I \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\boxed{\Rightarrow S = LDL^T}$$

### Transpose of Permutation

- Let $P$ be a permutation matrix.
- The transpose of $P$, $P^T$, is special because

$$\boxed{P^TP = I}$$

- The proof of this is simple: the rows of $P^T$ are columns of $P$ & row $i$ of $P^T$ is equal to column $i$ of $P$.
- Hence the dot product at the diagonal is 1.

$$\Rightarrow (P^TP)_{ii} = (\text{row } i \text{ of } P^T)(\text{col } i \text{ of } P) = 1$$

- All other dot products result in zero since they are orthogonal.
- This also means that

$$\boxed{P^T = P^{-1}}$$

  since the inverse is unique.

### Even And Odd Permutations

- An even permutation has an even number of inversions & odd has odd.
- Even perm takes an even count of steps to return back to original perm & odd perm needs odd.
- **Inversion:** It is the number of reverse ordered pairs in the perm.
- Consider the permutation $3\,1\,2$, the 3 pairs are $(3,1)$, $(3,2)$, $(1,2)$
- The original perm is $1\,2\,3$, and a wrong order (reverse order) is simply when the pair are not in the same order as in the original perm w.r.t. each other.
- Hence in $3\,1\,2$ there are 2 reverse ordered pairs or 2 inversions & they are $(3,1)$ & $(3,2)$.
- A swap between elements of a perm increases the inversion count by an odd number (or decreases by an odd number).

**Proof:** Consider a perm $P$, if we are swapping elements at positions $i$ & $j$ then if $P_i > P_j$ then after swap inversion count decreases by 1

- If $P_i < P_j$, then after swap inversion count increases by 1
- If there are $k$ elements between $P_i$ & $P_j$, then interaction of $P_i$ & $P_k$ will add $\pm 1$ inversion each; in total, from $P_k$ between $P_i$ & $P_j$ we get $\{-2, 0, 2\}$ added to the total number of inversions.
- Hence the in-between elements don't change the parity of total inversions.
- Hence after swap the parity of inversion count changes.
- Hence the parity of inversion count and number of swaps is same.

**(27)** In algebra a group of matrices includes $AB$ and $A^{-1}$ if it includes $A$ & $B$. Which of these sets are groups?

**(a)** Lower triangular matrices $L$, 1's diagonal

**sol:** Consider two lower triangular matrices $L_1$ & $L_2$, then we need to prove that $L_1L_2$ is lower triangular & $L_1^{-1}$ is also lower triangular.

$(L_1)_{ij} = 0$ if $j > i$, same for $L_2$

$$\Rightarrow (L_1L_2)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{m} (L_1)_{ik}(L_2)_{kj}$$

If $j > i$, then we have

$$(L_1L_2)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{j-1} (L_1)_{ik}(L_2)_{kj} + \sum_{k=j}^{m} (L_1)_{ik}(L_2)_{kj}$$

The left sum is 0 as all of $(L_2)_{kj}$ have $k < j$.

$$\Rightarrow (L_1L_2)_{ij} = \sum_{k=j}^{m} (L_1)_{ik}(L_2)_{kj}$$

This sum is also 0 as $(L_1)_{ik}$ are all zero because $k \geq j$ & $j > i \Rightarrow k > i$.

$$\Rightarrow (L_1L_2)_{ij} = 0, \text{ when } i < j$$

$\Rightarrow$ Hence $L_1L_2$ is lower triangular.

- Now we need to prove that the inverse of $L_1$ is also lower triangular.

Let $x$ be the $i$th column of $L^{-1}$, then we have,

$$\Rightarrow Lx = e_i \quad (i\text{th column of } I)$$

$$\Rightarrow L_{11}x_1 = 0, \text{ but since } L \text{ is lower triangular with 1's in diagonal} \Rightarrow L_{11} = 1$$

$$\Rightarrow x_1 = 0.$$

$$\Rightarrow L_{21}x_1 + L_{22}x_2 = 0$$

$$\Rightarrow L_{22}x_2 = 0 \Rightarrow x_2 = 0$$

- This will keep going until row $i$ where the sum needs to be 1.

$$\Rightarrow L_{i1}x_1 + L_{i2}x_2 + \cdots + L_{ii}x_i = 1$$

$$\Rightarrow L_{ii}x_i = 1 \Rightarrow x_i = 1$$

- After row $i$, we will have

$$L_{(i+1)i}\,x_i + L_{(i+1)(i+1)}\,x_{i+1} = 0$$

$$\Rightarrow x_{i+1} = -\frac{L_{(i+1)i}\,x_i}{L_{(i+1)(i+1)}}$$

- Hence $x_j$ for $j > i$ may (or) may not be 0.
- Hence if we do this for every column of $L^{-1}$ we will get that $L^{-1}$ is also lower triangular with 1's in the diagonal.
