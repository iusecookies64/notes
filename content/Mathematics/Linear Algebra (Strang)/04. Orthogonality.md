# 4.1 Orthogonality of Vectors and Subspaces

## Orthogonal Vectors

We have spent the last few chapters studying the four fundamental subspaces of a matrix purely in terms of dimension and span. Now we want to see how those subspaces sit *relative to each other* inside $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $\mathbb{R}^m$. The right language for that is perpendicularity. So let's start with the simplest question: when are two vectors perpendicular?

> [!abstract] Definition: Orthogonal Vectors
> Two vectors $v$ and $w$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ are **orthogonal** (denoted $v \perp w$) if and only if their inner product (dot product) is zero:
> $$
> v^T w = 0 \iff v \cdot w = 0
> $$

This is just "perpendicular" restated in algebraic form, so it's worth checking that the two notions agree.

### Geometric Interpretation and the Pythagorean Theorem

In Euclidean geometry, two vectors are perpendicular when the angle between them is $\theta = 90^\circ$. The geometric form of the dot product tells us

$$
v^T w = \|v\| \|w\| \cos\theta
$$

and when $\theta = 90^\circ$ we have $\cos(90^\circ) = 0$, so $v^T w = 0$. The algebraic definition therefore matches the picture we already have in mind.

There is a second way to see the same thing, using only lengths, and it is the **Pythagorean Theorem**. For any two vectors $v$ and $w$, let's expand the squared length of their sum:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|v + w\|^2 &= (v + w)^T (v + w) \\
&= v^T v + v^T w + w^T v + w^T w \\
&= \|v\|^2 + \|w\|^2 + 2 v^T w
\end{aligned}
$$

Look at the last line. The familiar "sum of squares" is exactly what we get when the cross term $2v^Tw$ disappears, which means

$$
\|v + w\|^2 = \|v\|^2 + \|w\|^2 \iff v^T w = 0
$$

So orthogonality is precisely the condition under which the Pythagorean relation holds.

One edge case deserves a mention, since it will matter repeatedly later.

> [!note] The Zero Vector
> The zero vector $\mathbf{0}$ is orthogonal to every vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$, because $\mathbf{0}^T v = 0$ for all $v \in \mathbb{R}^n$. It is also the only vector orthogonal to itself, since $v^T v = \|v\|^2 = 0 \implies v = \mathbf{0}$.

## Orthogonal Subspaces

Now let's upgrade from single vectors to whole subspaces. Two subspaces are "perpendicular" if there is no way to pick a vector from one and a vector from the other that fails to be perpendicular.

> [!abstract] Definition: Orthogonal Subspaces
> Two subspaces $V$ and $W$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$ are **orthogonal** (denoted $V \perp W$) if every vector in $V$ is orthogonal to every vector in $W$:
> $$
> v^T w = 0 \quad \text{for all } v \in V \text{ and all } w \in W
> $$

Notice how strong this condition is: it is a requirement on *every pair* $(v, w)$, not just on some chosen pair. A natural consequence is that two orthogonal subspaces can't share anything except the zero vector.

### Intersection of Orthogonal Subspaces

> [!theorem] Intersection of Orthogonal Subspaces
> If $V \perp W$, the only vector common to both subspaces is the zero vector:
> $$
> V \cap W = \{\mathbf{0}\}
> $$

**Proof:** Suppose $x \in V \cap W$. Since $x \in V$ and $x \in W$, and every vector in $V$ is orthogonal to every vector in $W$, the vector $x$ must be orthogonal to itself:

$$
x^T x = 0 \implies \|x\|^2 = 0 \implies x = \mathbf{0}
$$

So the intersection contains only the zero vector, and since the zero subspace has dimension zero,

$$
\dim(V \cap W) = 0
$$

$\blacksquare$

### Dimensions of Orthogonal Subspaces

This tiny intersection puts a hard cap on how large two orthogonal subspaces can be. Let's start from the dimension formula for the sum of two subspaces:

$$
\dim(V + W) = \dim V + \dim W - \dim(V \cap W)
$$

Substituting $\dim(V \cap W) = 0$ removes the correction term:

$$
\dim(V + W) = \dim V + \dim W
$$

The combined subspace $V + W$ lives inside $\mathbb{R}^n$, so its dimension cannot exceed $n$. Therefore

$$
\dim V + \dim W \le n
$$

The natural follow-up question is: what happens when we hit this bound with equality? That is exactly where orthogonal complements come in.

## Orthogonal Complements

Given a subspace $V$, we can ask for *all* vectors that are perpendicular to it. The collection of all of them is itself a subspace, and it gets its own name.

> [!abstract] Definition: Orthogonal Complement
> Given a subspace $V$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$, its **orthogonal complement** (denoted $V^\perp$ and pronounced "$V$ perp") is the set of all vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ that are orthogonal to every vector in $V$:
> $$
> V^\perp = \{w \in \mathbb{R}^n \mid v^T w = 0 \text{ for all } v \in V\}
> $$

Now we can return to the equality case from the previous section. When two orthogonal subspaces $V$ and $W$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ satisfy

$$
\dim V + \dim W = n
$$

the two subspaces fill up the available room as much as orthogonality allows, and each subspace turns out to be the orthogonal complement of the other: $W = V^\perp$ and $V = W^\perp$.

At this point you might doubt why the orthogonal complement of $V$ should have dimension exactly $n - \dim V$. It is easy to prove with a matrix. Put a basis of $V$ as the rows of a matrix $A$. Then $A$ has size $\dim(V) \times n$ and its rank is $\dim(V)$, since the rows are independent. The row space of $A$ is $C(A^T) = V$, and the null space $N(A)$ consists of exactly the vectors orthogonal to every row, so $N(A) = V^\perp$. By the fundamental theorem, $\dim N(A) = n - r$, and hence $\dim(V^\perp) = n - \dim(V)$. Rearranged:

$$
\dim(V) + \dim(V^{\perp}) = n
$$

With dimensions matching up this neatly, we get something even stronger than the dimension count: every vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$ splits cleanly into a piece from $V$ and a piece from $V^\perp$.

> [!theorem] Direct Sum Decomposition
> Any vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ can be decomposed uniquely into the sum of a vector in $V$ and a vector in $V^\perp$:
> $$
> x = x_V + x_{V^\perp} \quad \text{where } x_V \in V \text{ and } x_{V^\perp} \in V^\perp
> $$
> This establishes that $\mathbb{R}^n$ is the direct sum of $V$ and $V^\perp$:
> $$
> \mathbb{R}^n = V \oplus V^\perp
> $$

**Proof:** Let $\{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_s\}$ be a basis for $V$ (so $\dim V = s$), and let $\{w_1, w_2, \dots, w_t\}$ be a basis for $W = V^\perp$ (so $\dim W = t = n - s$). The plan is to show that putting these two bases together gives a basis for all of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

1. Since $V \cap W = \{\mathbf{0}\}$, the combined set of $s + t = n$ vectors $\{v_1, \dots, v_s, w_1, \dots, w_t\}$ is linearly independent. To see why, suppose a linear combination of them is zero, so that
   $$
   (c_1 v_1 + \dots + c_s v_s) + (d_1 w_1 + \dots + d_t w_t) = \mathbf{0}
   $$
   Then the vector $u = c_1 v_1 + \dots + c_s v_s = -(d_1 w_1 + \dots + d_t w_t)$ lies in both $V$ and $W$. A vector in both subspaces is orthogonal to itself, so $u^T u = 0$ and hence $u = \mathbf{0}$. Each basis is independent on its own, so $u = \mathbf{0}$ forces every $c_i = 0$ and every $d_j = 0$. If some vector of the combined set were a combination of the others, this would give a nonzero vector, contradicting what we just showed. So the combined set is independent.
2. In $\mathbb{R}^n$, any set of $n$ linearly independent vectors forms a basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$. Our combined set has exactly $n$ vectors, so it is a basis.
3. Therefore, every vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ can be uniquely written as a linear combination:
   $$
   x = (c_1 v_1 + \dots + c_s v_s) + (d_1 w_1 + \dots + d_t w_t)
   $$
4. Defining $x_V = \sum_{i=1}^s c_i v_i \in V$ and $x_W = \sum_{j=1}^t d_j w_j \in W$, we obtain:
   $$
   x = x_V + x_W \quad \text{with } x_V \in V, \; x_W \in W = V^\perp
   $$
   The uniqueness of basis expansion coefficients guarantees that this decomposition is unique.

$\blacksquare$

## Orthogonal Subspaces of a Matrix

Now we can finally connect all of this back to the four fundamental subspaces. The punchline is that they come in two orthogonal pairs, one pair living in the input space $\mathbb{R}^n$ and one in the output space $\mathbb{R}^m$.

> [!theorem] The Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra, Part II
> For any $m \times n$ matrix $A$ of rank $r$:
> 1. The row space $C(A^T)$ and the nullspace $N(A)$ are orthogonal complements in $\mathbb{R}^n$:
>    $$
>    N(A) = (C(A^T))^\perp \quad \text{and} \quad C(A^T) = (N(A))^\perp
>    $$
> 2. The column space $C(A)$ and the left nullspace $N(A^T)$ are orthogonal complements in $\mathbb{R}^m$:
>    $$
>    N(A^T) = (C(A))^\perp \quad \text{and} \quad C(A) = (N(A^T))^\perp
>    $$

Each statement has two ingredients: the subspaces are orthogonal, and their dimensions add up to the dimension of the ambient space. We handle the pairs one at a time.

### Proof 1: $C(A^T) \perp N(A)$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$

**Proof:** Let $x \in N(A)$. By definition of the nullspace, writing the rows of $A$ as $a_i^T$:

$$
Ax = \mathbf{0} \implies \begin{bmatrix} a_1^T \\ a_2^T \\ \vdots \\ a_m^T \end{bmatrix} x = \begin{bmatrix} a_1^T x \\ a_2^T x \\ \vdots \\ a_m^T x \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

This says $x$ is orthogonal to every row $a_i^T$ of $A$. But the row space contains all combinations of rows, not just the rows themselves, so let's check an arbitrary vector in it. Any vector in $C(A^T)$ can be expressed as $A^T y$ for some $y \in \mathbb{R}^m$, and the inner product is

$$
x^T (A^T y) = (Ax)^T y = \mathbf{0}^T y = 0
$$

Since $x^T (A^T y) = 0$ for all $x \in N(A)$ and all $A^T y \in C(A^T)$, the two subspaces are orthogonal: $C(A^T) \perp N(A)$.

Orthogonality alone isn't enough for complements, so we check dimensions. By the Rank-Nullity Theorem,

$$
\dim C(A^T) + \dim N(A) = r + (n - r) = n
$$

Because their dimensions sum to $n$, they are orthogonal complements in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

$\blacksquare$

### Proof 2: $C(A) \perp N(A^T)$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$

**Proof:** The argument is the same with $A$ replaced by $A^T$. Let $y \in N(A^T)$, so $A^T y = \mathbf{0}$. Any vector in the column space $C(A)$ can be expressed as $Ax$ for some $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, and their inner product is

$$
y^T (Ax) = (A^T y)^T x = \mathbf{0}^T x = 0
$$

Thus every vector in $N(A^T)$ is orthogonal to every vector in $C(A)$. Checking dimensions:

$$
\dim C(A) + \dim N(A^T) = r + (m - r) = m
$$

Because their dimensions sum to $m$, $C(A)$ and $N(A^T)$ are orthogonal complements in $\mathbb{R}^m$.

$\blacksquare$

## Structural Insights for Solutions to $Ax = b$

This orthogonal split has a very practical payoff. Every vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ decomposes into orthogonal components coming from the row space and the nullspace, and this structure governs the solvability and geometry of linear systems $Ax = b$. Let's walk through the consequences one by one.

1. **Unique Subspace Decomposition:**
   Since $\mathbb{R}^n = C(A^T) \oplus N(A)$, any solution $x$ can be uniquely written as:
   $$
   x = x_r + x_n \quad \text{where } x_r \in C(A^T) \text{ and } x_n \in N(A)
   $$

2. **Uniqueness of the Row Space Component:**
   If $Ax = b$ is solvable, there exists exactly **one** solution $x_r$ belonging strictly to the row space $C(A^T)$.

   **Proof:** Suppose $x_1, x_2 \in C(A^T)$ are both solutions to $Ax = b$. Then:
   $$
   A x_1 = b \quad \text{and} \quad A x_2 = b \implies A(x_1 - x_2) = \mathbf{0}
   $$
   Hence $(x_1 - x_2) \in N(A)$. However, because $C(A^T)$ is a subspace, $(x_1 - x_2) \in C(A^T)$ as well. Since $C(A^T) \cap N(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$:
   $$
   x_1 - x_2 = \mathbf{0} \implies x_1 = x_2
   $$
   $\blacksquare$

3. **Action on the General Solution:**
   Applying the matrix $A$ to the decomposed vector:
   $$
   A x = A(x_r + x_n) = A x_r + A x_n = A x_r + \mathbf{0} = A x_r = b
   $$
   The nullspace component $x_n$ contributes nothing to the right-hand side $b$, so all the "work" of producing $b$ is done by $x_r$.

4. **Minimum-Norm Solution:**
   Because $x_r \perp x_n$, the Pythagorean Theorem states:
   $$
   \|x\|^2 = \|x_r + x_n\|^2 = \|x_r\|^2 + \|x_n\|^2
   $$
   Since $\|x_n\|^2 \ge 0$, the smallest possible length for any solution occurs when $x_n = \mathbf{0}$:
   $$
   \|x\| \ge \|x_r\|
   $$
   The unique shortest solution to $Ax = b$ is the pure row space solution $x = x_r$.

5. **Linear Independence of Combined Bases:**
   Let $\{v_1, \dots, v_r\}$ be a basis for $C(A^T)$ and $\{w_1, \dots, w_{n-r}\}$ be a basis for $N(A)$. Suppose a combination of all these vectors vanishes:
   $$
   \sum_{i=1}^r c_i v_i + \sum_{j=1}^{n-r} d_j w_j = \mathbf{0} \implies x_r + x_n = \mathbf{0} \implies x_r = -x_n
   $$
   Since $x_r \in C(A^T)$ and $-x_n \in N(A)$, both vectors lie in $C(A^T) \cap N(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$. Hence $x_r = \mathbf{0}$ and $x_n = \mathbf{0}$. By independence of the individual basis sets, all $c_i = 0$ and all $d_j = 0$, so the combined set is independent.

6. **Equivalence of Basis Properties:**
   When the dimension count is known ($n$ vectors in an $n$-dimensional space), either property alone is enough to get a basis:
   - Any $n$ linearly independent vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ automatically span $\mathbb{R}^n$, forming a basis.
   - Any $n$ vectors that span $\mathbb{R}^n$ are automatically linearly independent, forming a basis.

## Problem Set 4.1 Solutions

Let's put these ideas to work on a few curated exercises from the problem set.

### Exercise 7: Fredholm's Alternative

**Problem:** If $b$ is not in $C(A)$, show that part of $b$ lies in $N(A^T)$. Prove that exactly one of the following systems has a solution:

$$
Ax = b \quad \text{or} \quad A^T y = \mathbf{0} \text{ with } y^T b = 1
$$

**Solution:** The key is the decomposition of the output space. Since $\mathbb{R}^m = C(A) \oplus N(A^T)$, any vector $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$ can be uniquely written as

$$
b = p + e \quad \text{where } p \in C(A) \text{ and } e \in N(A^T)
$$

This already answers the first part: when $b \notin C(A)$, the piece $e$ is the part of $b$ that lies in $N(A^T)$. Now we split into two cases depending on whether $b$ is in the column space.

1. **Case 1 ($b \in C(A)$):**
   Here $e = \mathbf{0}$, so $Ax = b$ is solvable. For any $y \in N(A^T)$:
   $$
   y^T b = y^T (Ax) = (A^T y)^T x = \mathbf{0}^T x = 0 \ne 1
   $$
   Therefore, no vector $y$ can satisfy both $A^T y = \mathbf{0}$ and $y^T b = 1$.
2. **Case 2 ($b \notin C(A)$):**
   Here $e \ne \mathbf{0}$, meaning $Ax = b$ has no solution. Since $e \in N(A^T)$, we have $A^T e = \mathbf{0}$. We construct $y$ by normalizing with respect to $e$:
   $$
   y = \frac{e}{\|e\|^2}
   $$
   Then $A^T y = \frac{1}{\|e\|^2} A^T e = \mathbf{0}$, and taking the inner product with $b$:
   $$
   y^T b = y^T (p + e) = y^T p + y^T e = 0 + \frac{e^T e}{\|e\|^2} = \frac{\|e\|^2}{\|e\|^2} = 1
   $$
   Thus $A^T y = \mathbf{0}$ with $y^T b = 1$ is solvable.

In each case exactly one of the two systems has a solution.

### Exercise 9: Nullspace of $A^T A$

**Problem:** Prove that if $A^T A x = \mathbf{0}$, then $Ax = \mathbf{0}$.

**Solution:** Assume $A^T (Ax) = \mathbf{0}$. By definition of the left nullspace, the vector $Ax$ lies in $N(A^T)$. At the same time, $Ax$ is a combination of the columns of $A$, so it lies in the column space $C(A)$. Because $C(A)$ and $N(A^T)$ are orthogonal complements, their intersection is trivial:

$$
C(A) \cap N(A^T) = \{\mathbf{0}\}
$$

Since $Ax$ belongs to both subspaces, it must be the zero vector:

$$
Ax = \mathbf{0}
$$

There is also a purely algebraic route to the same conclusion: multiply $A^T A x = \mathbf{0}$ on the left by $x^T$ to get

$$
x^T (A^T A x) = (Ax)^T (Ax) = \|Ax\|^2 = 0 \implies Ax = \mathbf{0}
$$

An important consequence is that $A^T A$ and $A$ have the same nullspace, so the rank of $A^{T}A$ is the same as the rank of $A$. In particular, if $A$ has full column rank $n$, then $A^{T}A$ is an $n \times n$ invertible matrix.

### Exercise 13: Matrix Criterion for Orthogonal Subspaces

**Problem:** How can we check if two subspaces $V$ and $W$ are orthogonal?

**Solution:** Let the columns of matrix $V$ form a basis for subspace $V$, and the columns of matrix $W$ form a basis for subspace $W$. The subspaces are orthogonal if and only if every basis vector of $V$ is perpendicular to every basis vector of $W$, and the matrix product $V^T W$ collects exactly all of these dot products as its entries. So the test is

$$
V^T W = O \quad \text{(the zero matrix)}
$$

### Exercise 15: Intersecting Subspaces

**Problem:** Show that subspaces $V$ and $W$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ must intersect non-trivially if $\dim V + \dim W > n$.

**Solution:** Start from the dimension addition theorem:

$$
\dim(V \cap W) = \dim V + \dim W - \dim(V + W)
$$

Because $V + W \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, its dimension satisfies $\dim(V + W) \le n$, which gives

$$
\dim(V \cap W) \ge \dim V + \dim W - n
$$

If $\dim V + \dim W > n$, then $\dim(V \cap W) > 0$, guaranteeing the existence of non-zero vectors in $V \cap W$. Consequently, such subspaces cannot be orthogonal.

### Exercise 20: The Double Orthogonal Complement

**Problem:** Show that for any subspace $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$:

$$
(S^\perp)^\perp = S
$$

**Solution:** We show one inclusion directly and then use dimensions to upgrade it to equality.

1. Every vector $s \in S$ is orthogonal to every vector in $S^\perp$. By definition of orthogonal complement, this implies $s \in (S^\perp)^\perp$, so $S \subseteq (S^\perp)^\perp$.
2. Comparing dimensions:
   $$
   \dim(S^\perp) = n - \dim S \implies \dim\big((S^\perp)^\perp\big) = n - \dim(S^\perp) = n - (n - \dim S) = \dim S
   $$
3. Since $S \subseteq (S^\perp)^\perp$ and their dimensions are identical, $(S^\perp)^\perp = S$.

### Exercise 23: Reversal of Subspace Inclusion

**Problem:** If subspace $S$ is contained in subspace $V$ ($S \subseteq V$), prove that $V^\perp \subseteq S^\perp$.

**Solution:** Let $y \in V^\perp$. By definition:

$$
y^T v = 0 \quad \text{for all } v \in V
$$

Because $S \subseteq V$, every vector $s \in S$ is also in $V$. Therefore:

$$
y^T s = 0 \quad \text{for all } s \in S
$$

This satisfies the definition of $S^\perp$, proving $y \in S^\perp$. Hence:

$$
S \subseteq V \implies V^\perp \subseteq S^\perp
$$

# 4.2 Projections onto Lines and Subspaces

Orthogonality is about to earn its keep. Whenever we have a vector $b$ that does not live in the place we would like it to live, we can ask a very natural question: *what is the closest point to $b$ in that place?* The answer is the **projection** of $b$, and the machinery for computing it will turn out to be one of the most useful tools in all of linear algebra. We start with the simplest possible "place" (a line) and then work our way up to arbitrary subspaces.

## Projection onto a Line

Take a non-zero vector $a \in \mathbb{R}^m$ and look at the line passing through it. The projection of a vector $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$ onto this line is the point $p$ on the line that is closest to $b$. Picture dropping a perpendicular from the tip of $b$ down to the line; the foot of that perpendicular is $p$.

![[04. Orthogonality-1791474300360.webp]]

### Derivation of the Scalar Multiplier $\hat{x}$

Let's turn this picture into algebra. Since $p$ lies on the one-dimensional line through $a$, it must be some scalar multiple of $a$, and our whole job is to find that scalar:

$$
p = a \hat{x}
$$

The gap between $b$ and its projection is the **error vector**:

$$
e = b - p = b - a \hat{x}
$$

Now comes the key geometric insight. The point $p$ is the closest point to $b$ exactly when the error vector $e$ meets the line at a right angle, that is, when $e$ is perpendicular to $a$:

$$
a \perp e \implies a^T e = 0
$$

Substituting $e = b - a \hat{x}$ turns this geometric condition into an equation for $\hat{x}$:

$$
a^T (b - a \hat{x}) = 0 \implies a^T b - a^T a \hat{x} = 0
$$

Since $a^T a$ is just a (non-zero) number, we can solve for the scalar directly:

$$
\hat{x} = \frac{a^T b}{a^T a}
$$

### The Projection Vector and Projection Matrix

With $\hat{x}$ in hand, plug it back into $p = a \hat{x}$:

$$
p = a \frac{a^T b}{a^T a}
$$

Here is a small but powerful trick. Because matrix multiplication is associative, we can regroup $a(a^T b)$ as $(a a^T) b$. The vector $b$ now sits at the far right, so everything in front of it is a matrix acting on $b$:

$$
p = \left(\frac{a a^T}{a^T a}\right) b = P b
$$

That matrix is the **Projection Matrix** onto the line spanned by $a$:

$$
P = \frac{a a^T}{a^T a}
$$

Notice that $a a^T$ is an $m \times m$ matrix (a column times a row), while $a^T a$ is a plain number, so $P$ is an $m \times m$ matrix that takes *any* $b$ and hands back its shadow on the line. Two properties of $P$ are worth singling out.

> [!theorem] Fundamental Properties of the Projection Matrix $P$
> The projection matrix $P = \dfrac{a a^T}{a^T a}$ satisfies:
> 1. **Symmetry:** $P^T = P$.
> 2. **Idempotence:** $P^2 = P$.

**Proof:**

For symmetry, transpose $P$ and use the fact that $(a a^T)^T = a a^T$:

$$
P^T = \left(\frac{a a^T}{a^T a}\right)^T = \frac{(a a^T)^T}{a^T a} = \frac{a a^T}{a^T a} = P
$$

For idempotence, multiply $P$ by itself. The middle factor $a^T a$ is a scalar, so it can be pulled out and cancelled against one copy of the denominator:

$$
P^2 = \left(\frac{a a^T}{a^T a}\right) \left(\frac{a a^T}{a^T a}\right) = \frac{a (a^T a) a^T}{(a^T a)^2} = \frac{a a^T}{a^T a} = P
$$

$\blacksquare$

The idempotence has a clean meaning: projecting a vector that already lies on the line leaves it unchanged, so projecting twice is the same as projecting once.

### The Error Vector and Complementary Projection

The error vector can also be produced by a matrix. Since $p = Pb$,

$$
e = b - p = b - P b = (I - P) b
$$

So $(I - P)$ is itself a projection matrix: it projects onto the $(m-1)$-dimensional subspace perpendicular to $a$, and it picks up exactly the part of $b$ that $P$ threw away.

### Length of the Projection Vector

How long is the shadow? Using the dot product formula $a^T b = \|a\| \|b\| \cos\theta$, we get

$$
\|p\| = \|a\| |\hat{x}| = \|a\| \frac{|a^T b|}{\|a\|^2} = \frac{\|a\| \|b\| |\cos\theta|}{\|a\|} = \|b\| |\cos\theta|
$$

This is precisely what elementary trigonometry says about the adjacent side of a right-angled triangle with hypotenuse $\|b\|$, a nice sanity check that the algebra and the geometry agree.

### Scaling Properties

It is instructive to ask what happens when we rescale the ingredients.

1. **Scaling $a$:** Replacing $a$ with $c a$ (for $c \ne 0$) leaves the projection matrix $P$ unchanged:
   $$
   P' = \frac{(c a)(c a)^T}{(c a)^T (c a)} = \frac{c^2 a a^T}{c^2 a^T a} = \frac{a a^T}{a^T a} = P
   $$
   This makes sense: the line is the same no matter which non-zero vector we use to describe it, so the projection onto it cannot depend on that choice.
2. **Scaling $b$:** Replacing $b$ with $c b$ scales the projection vector by the same factor, which is just linearity of $P$:
   $$
   p' = P (c b) = c P b = c p
   $$

## Rank Equivalence: $\operatorname{rank}(A^T A) = \operatorname{rank}(A)$

For a line we only needed to divide by the number $a^T a$. When we move to higher-dimensional subspaces, that number becomes the matrix $A^T A$, and we will need to *invert* it. So before generalizing, we should settle when that is allowed. The answer comes from showing that $A^T A$ and $A$ have exactly the same nullspace, and therefore the same rank.

> [!theorem] Nullspace and Rank Equality
> For any real $m \times n$ matrix $A$:
> $$
> N(A^T A) = N(A) \quad \text{and} \quad \operatorname{rank}(A^T A) = \operatorname{rank}(A)
> $$

**Proof:**

We show the two nullspaces contain each other. First, suppose $x \in N(A)$, so $Ax = \mathbf{0}$. Multiplying both sides by $A^T$ gives

$$
A^T A x = A^T \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0} \implies x \in N(A^T A)
$$

so $N(A) \subseteq N(A^T A)$.

Conversely, suppose $x \in N(A^T A)$, so $A^T A x = \mathbf{0}$. The trick is to premultiply by $x^T$, which converts the equation into a statement about a length:

$$
x^T (A^T A x) = 0 \implies (Ax)^T (Ax) = 0 \implies \|Ax\|^2 = 0 \implies Ax = \mathbf{0}
$$

Hence $x \in N(A)$, and $N(A^T A) \subseteq N(A)$. Combining both directions,

$$
N(A^T A) = N(A)
$$

For the ranks, note that both $A$ and $A^T A$ have $n$ columns, so the Rank-Nullity Theorem gives

$$
\dim N(A^T A) = n - \operatorname{rank}(A^T A) \quad \text{and} \quad \dim N(A) = n - \operatorname{rank}(A)
$$

Since the nullspaces are identical, their dimensions are equal:

$$
n - \operatorname{rank}(A^T A) = n - \operatorname{rank}(A) \implies \operatorname{rank}(A^T A) = \operatorname{rank}(A)
$$

$\blacksquare$

The practical payoff is the following.

> [!note] Invertibility of $A^T A$ and the Left Inverse
> If an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ has full column rank ($\operatorname{rank}(A) = n$, with $m \ge n$), then:
> - $A^T A$ is an $n \times n$ symmetric, positive definite, and **invertible** matrix.
> - The **left inverse** $A^{-1}_{\text{left}}$ exists and satisfies:
>   $$
>   \big((A^T A)^{-1} A^T\big) A = (A^T A)^{-1} (A^T A) = I_n \implies A^{-1}_{\text{left}} = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T
>   $$

## Projection onto Subspaces

Now let's project onto a whole subspace, not just a line. Let $S$ be an $n$-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$ spanned by linearly independent basis vectors $\{a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n\}$. We pack these vectors into the columns of an $m \times n$ matrix $A$, so that the subspace is simply the column space:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & \dots & a_n \end{bmatrix}, \quad S = C(A)
$$

Because the columns are independent, $\operatorname{rank}(A) = n$, and by the result we just proved, $A^T A$ is invertible.

The projection $p$ of a vector $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$ onto $C(A)$ has to live in $C(A)$, which means it is a linear combination of the columns of $A$. Our goal is to find the right coefficients:

$$
p = A \hat{x} = \hat{x}_1 a_1 + \hat{x}_2 a_2 + \dots + \hat{x}_n a_n
$$

The error vector is, as before,

$$
e = b - p = b - A \hat{x}
$$

Exactly as with the line, $p$ is the closest point in $C(A)$ to $b$ when the error vector is perpendicular to the *entire* subspace. It is enough to demand that $e$ be orthogonal to each basis column, since then it is orthogonal to every combination of them:

$$
a_1^T e = 0, \quad a_2^T e = 0, \quad \dots, \quad a_n^T e = 0
$$

Stacking these $n$ equations as the rows of a single matrix equation gives

$$
\begin{bmatrix} a_1^T \\ a_2^T \\ \vdots \\ a_n^T \end{bmatrix} e = \mathbf{0} \implies A^T e = \mathbf{0}
$$

Now substitute $e = b - A \hat{x}$:

$$
A^T (b - A \hat{x}) = \mathbf{0} \implies A^T A \hat{x} = A^T b
$$

These are the celebrated **Normal Equations**. Since $A^T A$ is invertible, we can solve them:

$$
\hat{x} = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T b
$$

### The Subspace Projection Matrix

To get the projection itself, multiply $\hat{x}$ by $A$:

$$
p = A \hat{x} = A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T b
$$

Factoring out $b$ on the right reveals the **Projection Matrix** $P$ onto the column space $C(A)$:

$$
P = A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T
$$

Compare this with the line case: $\dfrac{a a^T}{a^T a}$ is just the special case $A = a$, where the matrix inverse collapses to division by a number. As before, $P$ should be symmetric and idempotent.

> [!theorem] Properties of the General Projection Matrix
> The projection matrix $P = A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T$ is:
> 1. **Symmetric:** $P^T = P$.
> 2. **Idempotent:** $P^2 = P$.

**Proof:**

For symmetry, transpose each factor and reverse their order. The matrix $A^T A$ is symmetric, so its inverse is too, and everything returns to where it started:

$$
P^T = \big(A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T\big)^T = (A^T)^T \big((A^T A)^{-1}\big)^T A^T = A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T = P
$$

For idempotence, multiply $P$ by itself. The two inner factors $A^T A$ and $(A^T A)^{-1}$ meet in the middle and cancel to the identity:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P^2 &= \big(A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T\big) \big(A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T\big) \\
&= A (A^T A)^{-1} \big(A^T A\big) (A^T A)^{-1} A^T \\
&= A I (A^T A)^{-1} A^T \\
&= A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T = P
\end{aligned}
$$

$\blacksquare$

It is worth seeing what happens at the extreme end, when the subspace is everything.

> [!note] The Square Invertible Case
> If $A$ is an $n \times n$ square invertible matrix, its columns span the entire space $\mathbb{R}^n$. In this case, $(A^T A)^{-1} = A^{-1} (A^T)^{-1}$, and:
> $$
> P = A A^{-1} (A^T)^{-1} A^T = I \cdot I = I
> $$
> Any vector $b \in \mathbb{R}^n$ already lies in $C(A)$, so its projection onto $C(A)$ is $b$ itself: $P b = I b = b$.

### Orthogonal Decomposition of $b$

Putting the projection and the error together, every vector $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$ splits into two perpendicular parts:

$$
b = p + e = P b + (I - P) b
$$

where:
- $p = P b \in C(A)$ is the component along the subspace.
- $e = (I - P) b \in N(A^T)$ is the component perpendicular to the subspace (indeed, we arranged $A^T e = \mathbf{0}$).

## Oblique Projectors and Matrix Construction

So far, we have projected onto $V = C(A)$ by dropping a *perpendicular*. In other words, we decomposed $b = p + e$ by projecting onto $V$ along its orthogonal complement $V^\perp = N(A^T)$. But nothing forces us to move along the perpendicular direction. More generally, we can project onto a target subspace $V$ along *any* complementary subspace $W$ satisfying $\mathbb{R}^m = V \oplus W$, even when $W$ is not perpendicular to $V$. Such a projection is called an **oblique projection**.

To understand it geometrically, we first need the notion of a subspace that has been shifted away from the origin.

> [!abstract] Definition: Affine Subspace (Flat)
> Given a vector space $\mathbb{R}^m$, a subset $\mathcal{A} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ is an **affine subspace** (also called an **affine flat** or **linear manifold**) if it is formed by translating a linear subspace $W$ by a fixed displacement vector $x_0$:
> $$
> \mathcal{A} = x_0 + W = \{x_0 + w \mid w \in W\}
> $$
> The dimension of the affine subspace is defined as the dimension of the underlying parallel subspace: $\dim(\mathcal{A}) = \dim W$.

![[04. Orthogonality-1792216624646.webp]]

Let's unpack how an affine subspace differs from the linear subspaces we have been using all along.

> [!note] Geometric Distinction: Linear vs. Affine Subspaces
> - **Anchor at the Origin:** A linear subspace must contain the origin $\mathbf{0}$. An affine subspace is a linear subspace translated away from the origin; it retains the exact same "flatness" and directional structure, but does not pass through $\mathbf{0}$ unless the shift vector $x_0$ already belongs to $W$ (in which case $x_0 + W = W$).
>   - In $\mathbb{R}^2$: the line $y = 2x$ passes through $(0, 0)$ and is a linear subspace; the line $y = 2x + 3$ is an affine line parallel to $y = 2x$ shifted by $\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$.
>   - In $\mathbb{R}^3$: the $xy$-plane ($z = 0$) is a 2D linear subspace; the horizontal plane $z = 5$ passing through $b = (3, 4, 5)$ is a 2D affine plane parallel to the $xy$-plane.
> - **Connection to Inhomogeneous Systems $Ax = b$:**
>   - The solutions to the homogeneous equation $Ax = \mathbf{0}$ form the nullspace $N(A)$, which is a **linear subspace** passing through $\mathbf{0}$.
>   - The complete solution to an inhomogeneous equation $Ax = b$ ($b \ne \mathbf{0}$) is expressed as $x = x_p + x_n$ with $x_n \in N(A)$. The set of all solutions is the translation $x_p + N(A)$, which does not pass through $\mathbf{0}$ ($A\mathbf{0} \ne b$) and is therefore an **affine subspace** parallel to $N(A)$.
> - **Closure Property:** While linear subspaces are closed under arbitrary linear combinations $\sum c_i v_i$, affine subspaces are closed under **affine combinations**, that is, linear combinations where the coefficients sum to one:
>   $$
>   \sum_{i=1}^k c_i x_i \in \mathcal{A} \quad \text{for any } x_1, \dots, x_k \in \mathcal{A} \text{ and } \sum_{i=1}^k c_i = 1
>   $$

Why is the coefficient-sum condition the right one? Every vector in the affine subspace $\mathcal{A}$ has the form $x_0 + w$ with $w \in W$. So take vectors $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n \in \mathcal{A}$, each of the form $a_i = x_0 + w_i$. For their linear combination to land back in $\mathcal{A}$, it must again be expressible as $x_0 + w$ for some $w \in W$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_1 a_1 + c_2 a_2 + \dots + c_n a_n &= x_0 + w \\
\left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i \right) x_0 + (c_1 w_1 + \dots + c_n w_n) &= x_0 + w
\end{aligned}
$$

Matching the $x_0$ part and the $W$ part, this forces

$$
\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i\right) x_0 = x_0 \quad \text{and} \quad c_1 w_1 + \dots + c_n w_n = w
$$

The second condition is automatic, because $W$ is a linear subspace. The first one holds (for a general $x_0$) only when $c_1 + c_2 + \dots + c_n = 1$, which is exactly the affine-combination condition.

### Uniqueness of the Projection Path: Why Moving Along $W$ Does Not Yield Multiple Choices

Suppose the projection direction subspace $W$ has dimension greater than 1 (for instance, in $\mathbb{R}^4$ with $\dim V = 2$ and $\dim W = 2$, or in $\mathbb{R}^3$ with $\dim V = 1$ and $\dim W = 2$). Then $W$ contains uncountably many direction vectors, and a natural worry arises: *if we stand at the tip of $b$ and may move in any direction within $W$ to reach the target subspace $V$, don't we have infinitely many choices of direction, producing infinitely many different projections?*

The answer is **no**. Out of the infinitely many direction vectors inside $W$, **almost all of them completely miss $V$**. There is **exactly one unique vector** $w \in W$ that successfully connects $b$ to $V$.

### Geometric Explanation via Affine Intersections

The cleanest way to see this is through dimension counting. Starting from the tip of $b$, the set of all points in $\mathbb{R}^m$ reachable by subtracting vectors in the projection direction subspace $W$ is an **affine subspace**, a flat plane shifted away from the origin by $b$:

$$
\mathcal{A} = b - W = \{b - w \mid w \in W\}
$$

Its dimension is $\dim(\mathcal{A}) = \dim W$.

The target subspace $V$ is a linear subspace of dimension $\dim V$ passing through the origin. A valid projection corresponds to a point in the intersection $\mathcal{A} \cap V$:

$$
x \in \mathcal{A} \cap V \iff x \in V \quad \text{and} \quad x = b - w \text{ for some } w \in W
$$

Because $\mathbb{R}^m = V \oplus W$, the dimensions of the two flats add up to the ambient dimension:

$$
\dim V + \dim(\mathcal{A}) = \dim V + \dim W = m
$$

In an $m$-dimensional space, two transversal flats whose dimensions sum to $m$ intersect at **precisely a single point**:
- In $\mathbb{R}^3$: a 1D line ($V$) and a 2D plane ($\mathcal{A}$) intersect at exactly 1 point.
- In $\mathbb{R}^4$: two 2D planes ($V$ and $\mathcal{A}$) intersect at exactly 1 point.

Contrast this with choosing an arbitrary single direction $u \in W$ and travelling along the 1D line $b - t u$. When $m \ge 3$, that line and the subspace $V$ will almost never meet (they are skew lines/flats). It is only the *whole* flat $b - W$ that is guaranteed to hit $V$, and it hits it exactly once.

Geometry is persuasive, but let's also nail the uniqueness down rigorously.

> [!theorem] Uniqueness of the Decomposition
> Let $\mathbb{R}^m = V \oplus W$. Then for every $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$ there is exactly one point $p \in V$ and exactly one vector $w \in W$ such that $b - w = p$, that is, $b = p + w$.

**Proof:**

Suppose two vectors $w_1, w_2 \in W$ reach points $v_1, v_2 \in V$:

$$
b - w_1 = v_1 \implies b = v_1 + w_1
$$

$$
b - w_2 = v_2 \implies b = v_2 + w_2
$$

Subtracting the two equations and rearranging:

$$
(v_1 - v_2) + (w_1 - w_2) = \mathbf{0} \implies \underbrace{v_1 - v_2}_{\in V} = \underbrace{w_2 - w_1}_{\in W}
$$

So the vector $v_1 - v_2$ belongs to $V$ but is also equal to a vector $w_2 - w_1 \in W$. Therefore it must lie in their intersection:

$$
(v_1 - v_2) \in V \cap W
$$

Since $V \cap W = \{\mathbf{0}\}$, we must have

$$
v_1 - v_2 = \mathbf{0} \implies v_1 = v_2
$$

$$
w_2 - w_1 = \mathbf{0} \implies w_1 = w_2
$$

$\blacksquare$

Thus both the projection point $p \in V$ and the connecting error vector $w \in W$ are unique. The requirement that one must *actually land* on $V$ consumes all the degrees of freedom provided by the dimension of $W$.

What if $V$ and $W$ are *not* complementary? Then the direct-sum condition fails, and it is illuminating to see exactly how.

> [!note] What Happens If $V$ and $W$ Are Not Complementary?
> The direct sum condition $\mathbb{R}^m = V \oplus W$ consists of two independent requirements, spanning the ambient space ($V + W = \mathbb{R}^m$) and trivial intersection ($V \cap W = \{\mathbf{0}\}$), which separately govern the **existence** and **uniqueness** of a projection:
> 1. **Failure of Spanning ($V + W \subsetneq \mathbb{R}^m \implies$ Non-Existence):**
>    - **Algebraically:** Any vector $b \notin V + W$ cannot be written in the form $b = v + w$ with $v \in V$ and $w \in W$. Thus, no projection of $b$ onto $V$ along $W$ exists.
>    - **Geometrically:** The reachable affine flat $\mathcal{A} = b - W$ and the target subspace $V$ are skew flats that completely miss each other: $(b - W) \cap V = \emptyset$.
> 2. **Non-Trivial Intersection ($V \cap W \ne \{\mathbf{0}\} \implies$ Non-Uniqueness):**
>    - **Algebraically:** If there exists a non-zero vector $z \in V \cap W$ and $b = v + w$ is a valid decomposition ($v \in V$, $w \in W$), then for every scalar $c \in \mathbb{R}$:
>      $$
>      b = \underbrace{(v + c z)}_{\in V} + \underbrace{(w - c z)}_{\in W}
>      $$
>      Every choice of $c$ yields a distinct projection point $p(c) = v + c z \in V$, producing infinitely many valid projections (the entire affine flat $v + (V \cap W)$). Because a linear operator $P$ must assign a single unique output $P b$ to each input $b$, no projection matrix can be defined.
>    - **Geometrically:** The affine flat $\mathcal{A} = b - W$ intersects $V$ not at a single point, but along a higher-dimensional affine subspace of dimension $\dim(V \cap W) \ge 1$.

### Mathematical Formulation of Oblique Projectors

With the geometry settled, let's build the matrix. Let $V$ and $W$ be complementary subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^m$ such that $\dim V = k$, $\dim W = m - k$, and $\mathbb{R}^m = V \oplus W$. We need a handle on each of them in terms of matrices.

For the target subspace, let the columns of an $m \times k$ matrix $A$ form a basis for $V$:

$$
V = C(A), \quad \operatorname{rank}(A) = k
$$

For the projection direction, it is convenient to describe $W$ as a nullspace. Let $W^\perp$ denote the orthogonal complement of $W$. Since $\dim(W^\perp) = m - (m - k) = k$, we can choose an $m \times k$ matrix $B$ of rank $k$ whose columns form a basis for $W^\perp$. By the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra,

$$
W = (C(B))^\perp = N(B^T)
$$

The direct sum condition $V \cap W = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ says that no non-zero vector of $C(A)$ lies in $N(B^T)$:

$$
A x \in N(B^T) \implies B^T A x = \mathbf{0} \implies x = \mathbf{0}
$$

In other words, $B^T A x = \mathbf{0}$ forces $x = \mathbf{0}$, which guarantees that the $k \times k$ matrix $B^T A$ is **invertible**. That is the matrix we will be inverting, in the same way $A^T A$ was inverted in the orthogonal case.

### Derivation of the Oblique Projection Matrix

For any vector $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$, the projection $p$ lies in $V = C(A)$, so it is a combination of the columns of $A$:

$$
p = A \hat{x}
$$

The only change from before is the condition on the error. Instead of being orthogonal to $V$, the error vector $e = b - p = b - A \hat{x}$ must lie in the projection direction $W = N(B^T)$, which means

$$
B^T e = \mathbf{0} \implies B^T (b - A \hat{x}) = \mathbf{0}
$$

Expanding gives the oblique analogue of the normal equations:

$$
B^T A \hat{x} = B^T b
$$

Since $B^T A$ is invertible, we can solve for the coefficient vector:

$$
\hat{x} = (B^T A)^{-1} B^T b
$$

Substituting into $p = A \hat{x}$:

$$
p = A (B^T A)^{-1} B^T b
$$

Factoring out $b$ defines the **Oblique Projection Matrix** $P$:

$$
P = A (B^T A)^{-1} B^T
$$

![[04. Orthogonality-1792774929826.webp]]

### Verification of Properties

Let's check that this matrix really behaves like a projector onto $V$ along $W$.

First, **idempotence**. The middle factors $B^T A$ and $(B^T A)^{-1}$ cancel just as before:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P^2 &= \big(A (B^T A)^{-1} B^T\big) \big(A (B^T A)^{-1} B^T\big) \\
&= A (B^T A)^{-1} \big(B^T A\big) (B^T A)^{-1} B^T \\
&= A I_k (B^T A)^{-1} B^T \\
&= A (B^T A)^{-1} B^T = P
\end{aligned}
$$

Second, the **subspaces** of $P$ are the intended ones:
- **Range:** Since $B^T A$ is invertible, $C(P) = C(A) = V$.
- **Nullspace:** Because $A$ has full column rank and $(B^T A)^{-1}$ is invertible, $P x = \mathbf{0} \iff B^T x = \mathbf{0}$, which proves $N(P) = N(B^T) = W$.

Third, we can **specialize to the orthogonal case**. When the projection direction is orthogonal to the target subspace, $W = V^\perp = (C(A))^\perp = N(A^T)$. Setting $B = A$ recovers the orthogonal projection matrix:

$$
P = A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T
$$

When $B \ne A$, $P$ is generally not symmetric ($P^T \ne P$), which confirms that the projection is oblique.

## Algebraic Characterization of Projectors: Idempotence ($P^2 = P$)

Both the orthogonal projector $P = A(A^T A)^{-1}A^T$ and the oblique projector $P = A(B^T A)^{-1}B^T$ satisfy $P^2 = P$. This is no coincidence. Idempotence alone is the universal algebraic condition that characterizes *every* subspace projector, so we can promote it from a property to a definition.

> [!abstract] Definition: Projector (Projection Matrix)
> A linear operator or square matrix $P \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ is called a **projector** (or **projection matrix**) if and only if it is **idempotent**:
> $$
> P^2 = P
> $$

### Sufficiency of Idempotence for Subspace Projection

A word on terminology first. In introductory texts (including Strang), "projection matrix" is often used as shorthand for an *orthogonal* projection matrix, which requires both idempotence ($P^2 = P$) and symmetry ($P^T = P$). In general linear algebra, however, idempotence alone is the necessary and sufficient algebraic condition for a matrix to be a projection matrix onto a subspace.

Why is that enough? Let's see how $P^2 = P$ alone produces all the geometry we have been discussing, in three steps.

**1. Invariance on the Target Subspace.** If a vector $x$ lies in the column space $C(P)$, it can be written as $x = P v$ for some $v \in \mathbb{R}^m$. Applying $P$ again yields

$$
P x = P(P v) = P^2 v = P v = x
$$

Thus $P$ acts as the identity operator on its range $C(P)$. Once a vector is already within the target subspace, projecting it a second time leaves it unchanged.

**2. Direct Sum Decomposition of the Ambient Space.** For any vector $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$, consider the algebraic identity

$$
b = P b + (I - P) b
$$

The vector $P b$ lies in the column space $C(P)$. The vector $(I - P) b$ lies in the nullspace $N(P)$, because

$$
P(I - P) b = (P - P^2) b = (P - P) b = \mathbf{0}
$$

Furthermore, the intersection of $C(P)$ and $N(P)$ is strictly trivial. If $x$ lies in both, then by step 1 $x = P x$, while membership in the nullspace gives $P x = \mathbf{0}$:

$$
x \in C(P) \cap N(P) \implies x = P x \text{ and } P x = \mathbf{0} \implies x = \mathbf{0}
$$

Therefore, any idempotent matrix $P$ decomposes the entire space into a direct sum:

$$
\mathbb{R}^m = C(P) \oplus N(P)
$$

Every vector $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$ is decomposed uniquely into $b = p + e$, where $p \in C(P)$ and $e \in N(P)$. Consequently, $P$ represents the unique projector onto $C(P)$ along $N(P)$.

**3. The Complementary Projector.** The complementary matrix $P_c = I - P$ is also idempotent:

$$
(I - P)^2 = I - 2P + P^2 = I - 2P + P = I - P
$$

It projects onto the nullspace $N(P)$ along the column space $C(P)$, so the two projectors $P$ and $I - P$ split the space between them.

## Orthogonal vs. Oblique Projectors

We've just seen that any idempotent matrix $P$ splits the whole space into $C(P) \oplus N(P)$. But notice that nothing in that statement says how these two subspaces sit relative to each other. They could meet at a right angle, or they could lean toward each other at some slant. That geometric relationship is exactly what separates the projectors we actually care about (the "closest point" kind) from their more awkward cousins. Projectors are classified by how their range $C(P)$ (the subspace we project onto) relates to their nullspace $N(P)$ (the direction along which we project).

> [!abstract] Classification of Projectors
> Let $P \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ be a projector ($P^2 = P$) projecting onto $V = C(P)$ along $W = N(P)$, where $\mathbb{R}^m = V \oplus W$.
> - **Orthogonal Projector:** $V \perp W$, which means $N(P) = (C(P))^\perp$. The projection direction is perpendicular to the target subspace.
> - **Oblique Projector:** $V \not\perp W$, which means $N(P) \ne (C(P))^\perp$. The projection direction is transversal to $V$, but at an oblique (non-perpendicular) angle.

In words: both kinds of projector drop a vector $b$ onto $V$, but an orthogonal projector drops it straight down, while an oblique projector drops it at a slant.

![[04. Orthogonality-1792215492425.webp]]

### Geometric Comparison

The easiest way to feel the difference is with a shadow. To project a vector $b$ onto a subspace $V$ "along" a direction $W$ means casting a shadow of $b$ onto $V$ using parallel light rays that travel in the direction of $W$. If the sun is directly overhead at midday, the rays hit the ground at $90^\circ$, and that is an **orthogonal projection**: the error vector $e = b - P b$ lies in $N(P) = (C(P))^\perp$, so it is perpendicular to the projection $p \in V$. If instead it is the late afternoon sun, the rays strike $V$ at a slant, and we get an **oblique projection**. The error vector $e = b - P b$ still belongs to $N(P)$, but because $N(P)$ is not orthogonal to $C(P)$, the error is **not** perpendicular to the projection $p = P b$.

This difference shows up very concretely in lengths. For an orthogonal projector, $p$ and $e$ are perpendicular, so the Pythagorean Theorem applies to the decomposition $b = p + e$:

$$
\|b\|^2 = \|p + e\|^2 = \|p\|^2 + \|e\|^2 \ge \|p\|^2 \implies \|P b\| \le \|b\|
$$

So an orthogonal projector can never make a vector longer. It is a non-expansive contraction, and its induced matrix 2-norm satisfies $\|P\|_2 = 1$ (for $P \ne O$).

For an oblique projector, $p$ and $e$ are not perpendicular, so we have to fall back on the general vector norm formula, which carries an extra cross term:

$$
\|b\|^2 = \|p + e\|^2 = \|p\|^2 + \|e\|^2 + 2 p^T e
$$

Whenever $p^T e < 0$, that cross term eats into $\|b\|^2$, and the projected vector can be strictly longer than the original: $\|P b\| > \|b\|$. Consequently the induced matrix 2-norm of an oblique projector satisfies $\|P\|_2 > 1$, and as the angle between $V$ and $W$ becomes arbitrarily small (the slant gets flatter and flatter), $\|P\|_2 \to \infty$.

### Symmetry and the Characterization of Orthogonal Projectors

So how can we tell, just by looking at the matrix, whether a projector is orthogonal or oblique? We don't want to compute $C(P)$ and $N(P)$ and check angles every time. It turns out the answer is purely algebraic, and it comes down to one familiar property: symmetry.

> [!theorem] Orthogonal Projection Characterization Theorem
> Let $P \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ be a projector ($P^2 = P$). Then $P$ is an **orthogonal projector** if and only if $P$ is **symmetric**:
> $$
> P \text{ is an orthogonal projector} \iff P^T = P
> $$

Since this is an "if and only if", we have to prove both directions.

**Proof:**

**Direction 1 ($\Leftarrow$): If $P^2 = P$ and $P = P^T$, then $P$ is an orthogonal projector.**

To show that $P$ is an orthogonal projector, we need to show that for every vector $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$, the projection $p = P b$ and the error vector $e = (I - P) b$ are orthogonal. So let's compute their inner product:

$$
\langle P b, (I - P) b \rangle = (P b)^T (I - P) b = b^T P^T (I - P) b
$$

Now we use the symmetry condition $P^T = P$ to replace the transpose:

$$
P^T (I - P) = P(I - P) = P - P^2
$$

Then idempotence $P^2 = P$ kills the whole expression:

$$
P - P^2 = P - P = O
$$

Substituting this back into the inner product gives

$$
\langle P b, (I - P) b \rangle = b^T O b = 0 \quad \text{for all } b \in \mathbb{R}^m
$$

Because the error vector is orthogonal to the projection vector for every $b$, we get $C(P) \perp N(P)$. Hence $P$ is an orthogonal projector.

**Direction 2 ($\Rightarrow$): If $P$ is an orthogonal projector, then $P = P^T$.**

If $P$ is an orthogonal projector, its column space and nullspace are orthogonal complements: $C(P) \perp N(P)$. Thus, for any $u \in C(P)$ and $v \in N(P)$, we have $u^T v = 0$.

The idea is to compare $\langle P x, y \rangle$ with $\langle x, P y \rangle$ for arbitrary vectors. Take arbitrary $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^m$ and decompose each of them via $P$:

$$
x = \underbrace{P x}_{\in C(P)} + \underbrace{(I - P) x}_{\in N(P)}, \qquad y = \underbrace{P y}_{\in C(P)} + \underbrace{(I - P) y}_{\in N(P)}
$$

First, compute $\langle P x, y \rangle$ by expanding $y$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle P x, y \rangle &= \langle P x, P y + (I - P) y \rangle \\
&= \langle P x, P y \rangle + \underbrace{\langle P x, (I - P) y \rangle}_{= 0 \text{ since } C(P) \perp N(P)} \\
&= \langle P x, P y \rangle
\end{aligned}
$$

Next, compute $\langle x, P y \rangle$ by expanding $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle x, P y \rangle &= \langle P x + (I - P) x, P y \rangle \\
&= \langle P x, P y \rangle + \underbrace{\langle (I - P) x, P y \rangle}_{= 0 \text{ since } N(P) \perp C(P)} \\
&= \langle P x, P y \rangle
\end{aligned}
$$

Both expressions equal $\langle P x, P y \rangle$, so $\langle P x, y \rangle = \langle x, P y \rangle$. Writing this out in matrix form:

$$
x^T P^T y = x^T P y \implies x^T (P^T - P) y = 0 \quad \text{for all } x, y \in \mathbb{R}^m
$$

A matrix that gives zero when sandwiched between every pair of vectors must itself be zero:

$$
P^T - P = O \implies P^T = P
$$

Thus, every orthogonal projector is symmetric. $\blacksquare$

### Why Row Space Equaling Column Space Does Not Imply Symmetry in General

There is a tempting geometric shortcut for Direction 2 that looks convincing but is actually flawed, so it's worth seeing exactly where it breaks down.

> [!warning] Common Pitfall: $C(A) = C(A^T) \centernot\implies A = A^T$
> It is tempting to attempt a geometric shortcut:
> 1. In an orthogonal projector, $C(P) \perp N(P)$.
> 2. By the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra, the row space satisfies $C(P^T) \perp N(P)$.
> 3. Because both $C(P)$ and $C(P^T)$ are the orthogonal complement of the exact same subspace $N(P)$, it follows that $C(P) = C(P^T)$ (column space equals row space).
> 4. Concluding from $C(P) = C(P^T)$ that $P = P^T$ is **invalid for general matrices**.
>
> Having equal column space and row space does not make a matrix symmetric. Any non-symmetric invertible matrix, such as the planar rotation matrix:
> $$
> R = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> has $C(R) = \mathbb{R}^2$ and $C(R^T) = \mathbb{R}^2$, yet $R^T = -R \ne R$. More generally, all normal matrices ($A^T A = A A^T$) have identical row and column spaces without necessarily being symmetric.

Steps 1 to 3 are perfectly fine, so the shortcut gets us to $C(P) = C(P^T)$. The failure is only in the last step, which would need extra structure. For projectors, we do have extra structure, namely idempotence, and that rescues the argument.

> [!note] Why $C(P) = C(P^T)$ Implies Symmetry Specifically for Projectors
> While $C(A) = C(A^T)$ does not imply symmetry in general, it **does** force symmetry if the matrix is known in advance to be idempotent ($P^2 = P$):
> - An idempotent matrix $P^2 = P$ is always diagonalizable, with eigenvalues strictly restricted to $\lambda \in \{0, 1\}$. Its eigenspaces are $E_1 = C(P)$ and $E_0 = N(P)$.
> - If $C(P) = C(P^T)$, then $C(P) \perp N(P)$, which means the eigenspaces $E_1$ and $E_0$ are mutually orthogonal.
> - An operator whose distinct eigenspaces are mutually orthogonal possesses an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors.
> - By the **Spectral Theorem**, any real matrix with a complete orthonormal set of eigenvectors can be factored as $P = Q \Lambda Q^T$ (where $Q$ is orthogonal and $\Lambda$ is diagonal), which guarantees:
>   $$
>   P^T = (Q \Lambda Q^T)^T = Q \Lambda^T Q^T = Q \Lambda Q^T = P
>   $$
> The inner product proof using $\langle P x, y \rangle = \langle x, P y \rangle$ is preferable because it establishes symmetry directly and elementarily, without invoking the Spectral Theorem.

## Problem Set 4.2 Solutions

Now let's put these ideas to work. The following curated exercises from Problem Set 4.2 each exercise one facet of projection matrices: building them from orthogonal pieces, understanding complements, and checking their rank, symmetry and idempotence properties.

### Exercise 7: Projection onto Orthogonal Vectors

**Problem:** If $a_1$ and $a_2$ are orthogonal vectors, prove that the projection matrix $P$ onto the subspace spanned by $\{a_1, a_2\}$ is $P = P_1 + P_2$, where $P_i = \frac{a_i a_i^T}{a_i^T a_i}$.

**Solution:** The natural plan is to put the two vectors in the columns of $A = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \end{bmatrix}$ and use the general formula $P = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$. Orthogonality should make $A^T A$ especially simple. Computing it:

$$
A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} a_1^T \\ a_2^T \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a_1^T a_1 & a_1^T a_2 \\ a_2^T a_1 & a_2^T a_2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Because $a_1 \perp a_2$, the off-diagonal entries vanish: $a_1^T a_2 = a_2^T a_1 = 0$. Letting $k_1 = a_1^T a_1$ and $k_2 = a_2^T a_2$, the matrix is diagonal and trivially inverted:

$$
A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} k_1 & 0 \\ 0 & k_2 \end{bmatrix} \implies (A^T A)^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{k_1} & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{k_2} \end{bmatrix}
$$

Now compute $P = A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P &= \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{k_1} & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{k_2} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a_1^T \\ a_2^T \end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \frac{a_1^T}{k_1} \\ \frac{a_2^T}{k_2} \end{bmatrix} \\
&= \frac{a_1 a_1^T}{k_1} + \frac{a_2 a_2^T}{k_2} \\
&= \frac{a_1 a_1^T}{a_1^T a_1} + \frac{a_2 a_2^T}{a_2^T a_2} = P_1 + P_2
\end{aligned}
$$

The same argument works for any number of vectors. For any set of mutually orthogonal vectors $\{a_1, \dots, a_n\}$, the projection matrix is the sum of the 1D projection matrices:

$$
P = \sum_{i=1}^n P_i = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{a_i a_i^T}{a_i^T a_i}
$$

In the extreme case $n = m$, these vectors span all of $\mathbb{R}^m$, and the projection onto the whole space is the identity: $P = \sum_{i=1}^m P_i = I$.

### Exercise 18: Idempotence of $I - P$

**Problem:** If $P^2 = P = P^T$, show that $(I - P)^2 = I - P$. When $P$ projects onto $C(A)$, what does $I - P$ project onto?

**Solution:** Just expand the square and use $P^2 = P$:

$$
(I - P)^2 = (I - P)(I - P) = I - P - P + P^2 = I - 2P + P = I - P
$$

Symmetry carries over as well:

$$
(I - P)^T = I^T - P^T = I - P
$$

So $I - P$ is again symmetric and idempotent, i.e. an orthogonal projector. When $P$ projects onto the column space $C(A)$, $I - P$ projects onto the orthogonal complement of $C(A)$, which is the **left nullspace** $N(A^T)$.

One doubt I had was how $e = b - p$ can be the *projection* of $b$ onto $N(A^{T})$. Initially I thought that $e$ is just some vector lying in $N(A^{T})$, which is not the same thing. But let $p'$ be the projection of $b$ onto $N(A^{T})$. Then $p'$ is unique, and $b - p'$ must lie in $C(A)$. We already know that $p = b - e$ lies in $C(A)$, hence $e = p'$.

### Exercise 21: Fast Projection onto a Hyperplane

**Problem:** Find the projection matrix $P$ onto the plane $x - y - 2z = 0$.

**Solution:** Rather than finding a basis for the plane and using $A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$, we can take a shortcut through the complement. The plane is a 2D subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$ defined by the normal vector $n = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}$, and the line through $n$ is orthogonal to the plane.

1. The projection matrix onto the normal line is:
   $$
   P_n = \frac{n n^T}{n^T n} = \frac{1}{1^2 + (-1)^2 + (-2)^2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & -2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{6} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & -2 \\ -1 & 1 & 2 \\ -2 & 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}
   $$
2. The projection matrix $P$ onto the plane is the complementary projector:
   $$
   P = I - P_n = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{1}{6} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & -2 \\ -1 & 1 & 2 \\ -2 & 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{6} \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 5 & -2 \\ 2 & -2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
   $$

### Exercise 24: Square Invertible $A$

**Problem:** Show that if $A$ is square and invertible, $P = I$.

**Solution:** Since $A$ is invertible, we can split the inverse of the product $A^T A$:

$$
P = A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T = A A^{-1} (A^T)^{-1} A^T = I \cdot I = I
$$

This is geometrically necessary because the columns of an invertible $n \times n$ matrix span all of $\mathbb{R}^n$, leaving no perpendicular component.

### Exercise 25: Projection of Vectors in the Left Nullspace

**Problem:** The left nullspace $N(A^T)$ is orthogonal to $C(A)$. If $A^T b = \mathbf{0}$, check that $P b = \mathbf{0}$.

**Solution:** The formula for $P$ ends with $A^T$, so it acts on $b$ first:

$$
P b = A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T b = A (A^T A)^{-1} \mathbf{0} = A \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}
$$

In other words, any vector entirely in $N(A^T)$ has zero projection onto $C(A)$.

### Exercise 26: Rank of the Projection Matrix

**Problem:** Prove that the projection matrix $P$ onto an $n$-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$ has rank $n$.

**Solution:** The key is to identify the column space of $P$. The range of $P$ is the subspace $S = C(A)$ itself, because for any $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$ we have $P b \in S$, and for any $s \in S$ we have $P s = s$. Thus $C(P) = S$, and

$$
\operatorname{rank}(P) = \dim(C(P)) = \dim S = n
$$

### Exercise 29: Diagonal Entries of $P$

**Problem:** Use $P^T = P$ and $P^2 = P$ to prove that the diagonal entry $P_{ii}$ is the squared length of the $i$-th column of $P$.

**Solution:** Let $p_i$ denote the $i$-th column of $P$. Since $P = P^T$, the $i$-th row of $P$ is $p_i^T$. Now use idempotence together with symmetry, $P = P^2 = P^T P$:

$$
P = \begin{bmatrix} p_1^T \\ p_2^T \\ \vdots \\ p_m^T \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} p_1 & p_2 & \dots & p_m \end{bmatrix}
$$

The $(i, i)$ entry is the inner product of the $i$-th row of $P^T$ with the $i$-th column of $P$:

$$
P_{ii} = p_i^T p_i = \|p_i\|^2
$$

As a bonus, this bounds the diagonal entries. Because $P_{ii} = \sum_j P_{ji}^2 = P_{ii}^2 + \sum_{j \ne i} P_{ji}^2 \ge P_{ii}^2$, it follows that $0 \le P_{ii} \le 1$.

### Exercise 30: Invertibility of $B B^T$

**Problem:** If matrix $B$ ($m \times n$) has full row rank ($m$), show that $B B^T$ is invertible.

**Solution:** $B B^T$ is an $m \times m$ square matrix, so it is invertible exactly when its rank is $m$. We analyze its nullspace. Let $x \in N(B B^T)$, so $B B^T x = \mathbf{0}$. Multiplying on the left by $x^T$:

$$
x^T (B B^T x) = 0 \implies (B^T x)^T (B^T x) = 0 \implies \|B^T x\|^2 = 0 \implies B^T x = \mathbf{0}
$$

Hence $x \in N(B^T)$. Conversely, $B^T x = \mathbf{0} \implies B B^T x = \mathbf{0}$. Therefore the two nullspaces coincide:

$$
N(B B^T) = N(B^T)
$$

By the Rank-Nullity Theorem applied to the $m \times m$ matrix $B B^T$:

$$
\operatorname{rank}(B B^T) = m - \dim N(B B^T) = m - \dim N(B^T) = \operatorname{rank}(B^T)
$$

Since $B$ has full row rank, $\operatorname{rank}(B) = \operatorname{rank}(B^T) = m$. Thus

$$
\operatorname{rank}(B B^T) = m
$$

An $m \times m$ matrix with rank $m$ is invertible.

### Exercise 31: Commutation with Subspace Projections

**Problem:** If $P_C$ projects onto $C(A)$ and $P_R$ projects onto $C(A^T)$, show that $P_C A = A P_R = P_C A P_R = A$.

**Solution:** The idea is that a projector leaves alone anything already in its target subspace.
- Every column of $A$ lies in $C(A)$, so $P_C$ acts as the identity on the columns of $A$: $P_C A = A$.
- Every row of $A$ lies in $C(A^T)$, so $A P_R = (P_R^T A^T)^T = (P_R A^T)^T = (A^T)^T = A$.
- Combining both: $P_C A P_R = P_C (A P_R) = P_C A = A$.

### Exercise 34: Product of Projection Matrices

**Problem:** If $P_1$ and $P_2$ are projection matrices, prove that $P_1 P_2$ is a projection matrix if and only if $P_1 P_2 = P_2 P_1$.

**Solution:** Here "projection matrix" means orthogonal projector, and we know from the characterization theorem that a matrix is a projection matrix if and only if it is symmetric and idempotent. So we check both properties for $P_1 P_2$.

1. **Symmetry:** Transposing the product reverses the order:
   $$
   (P_1 P_2)^T = P_2^T P_1^T = P_2 P_1
   $$
   For $P_1 P_2$ to be symmetric, we must have $(P_1 P_2)^T = P_1 P_2 \iff P_2 P_1 = P_1 P_2$.
2. **Idempotence:** If $P_1 P_2 = P_2 P_1$, then:
   $$
   (P_1 P_2)^2 = (P_1 P_2)(P_1 P_2) = P_1 (P_2 P_1) P_2 = P_1 (P_1 P_2) P_2 = P_1^2 P_2^2 = P_1 P_2
   $$

Thus commutativity $P_1 P_2 = P_2 P_1$ is necessary and sufficient.

# 4.3 Least Squares Approximations

## Overdetermined Linear Systems and the Minimization Problem

In real-world applications, we almost always collect more measurements than we have unknowns, so the system $Ax = b$ ends up with more equations than unknowns ($m > n$). Such an overdetermined system is typically inconsistent: there is no exact solution, because $b \notin C(A)$. So what do we do when the equation simply cannot be solved?

We give up on exactness and ask for the next best thing: a vector $\hat{x}$ that makes $A\hat{x}$ as close as possible to $b$. To make "close" precise, we add up the squares of all the individual errors. This quantity is known as the **least squares criterion**:

$$
E = \|Ax - b\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^m (a_i^T x - b_i)^2
$$

Here $a_i^T$ is the $i$-th row of $A$, so each term $(a_i^T x - b_i)$ is exactly how far the $i$-th equation misses its target, and $E$ collects all those misses.

### Geometric Justification via the Pythagorean Theorem

Why should minimizing $E$ lead anywhere nice? The answer comes from the four fundamental subspaces. Any vector $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$ can be decomposed uniquely into a piece in the column space and a piece in the left nullspace:

$$
b = p + e \quad \text{where } p \in C(A) \text{ and } e \in N(A^T)
$$

Now take any candidate $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$. The vector $Ax$ lies in $C(A)$, and so does $p$, which means $(Ax - p)$ is also a vector in $C(A)$. Since $C(A) \perp N(A^T)$, this vector must be orthogonal to the error piece $e$:

$$
(Ax - p) \perp e
$$

Two orthogonal vectors are exactly the situation where the Pythagorean Theorem applies, so we can split the total squared error into two clean pieces:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|Ax - b\|^2 &= \|(Ax - p) - e\|^2 \\
&= \|Ax - p\|^2 + \|e\|^2
\end{aligned}
$$

Look at what each piece depends on. The error $\|e\|^2$ is fixed by the geometry of $b$ and $C(A)$, so no choice of $x$ can touch it. The other term, $\|Ax - p\|^2$, is non-negative and can be driven all the way down to zero by choosing

$$
Ax = p
$$

Thus the squared error is minimized uniquely when $Ax = p$, and the minimum error is $E_{\min} = \|e\|^2$. In plain words: the best we can do is solve for the closest point $p$ in the column space, and what is left over is the unavoidable error $e$.

![[04. Orthogonality-1791533082468.webp]]

### The Normal Equations

Now let's turn this geometric picture into an equation we can actually solve. Write $p = A\hat{x}$, so that $e = b - A\hat{x}$. We know $e$ is perpendicular to $C(A)$, i.e. it lives in $N(A^T)$, so it must satisfy

$$
A^T e = \mathbf{0} \implies A^T (b - A\hat{x}) = \mathbf{0}
$$

Rearranging gives the famous **normal equations**:

$$
A^T A \hat{x} = A^T b
$$

When the columns of $A$ are linearly independent, $A^T A$ is invertible, and we can solve explicitly:

$$
\hat{x} = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T b
$$

The closest vector $p \in C(A)$ to $b$ is then

$$
p = A \hat{x} = A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T b = P b
$$

so the matrix $P = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T$ is precisely the projection matrix that sends $b$ to its closest point in the column space.

## Fitting a Straight Line

The classic application is line fitting. Suppose we collect $m$ data points $(t_1, b_1), (t_2, b_2), \dots, (t_m, b_m)$ with $m > 2$, and we wish to fit the best straight line

$$
y(t) = C + D t
$$

Each data point demands that the line passes through it, which gives one equation per point:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C + D t_1 &= b_1 \\
C + D t_2 &= b_2 \\
&\;\vdots \\
C + D t_m &= b_m
\end{aligned}
$$

With more than two points, these equations generally cannot all hold at once, which is exactly the overdetermined situation above. In matrix form $Ax = b$ the system reads

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & t_1 \\ 1 & t_2 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & t_m \end{bmatrix}, \quad x = \begin{bmatrix} C \\ D \end{bmatrix}, \quad b = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ \vdots \\ b_m \end{bmatrix}
$$

### Setting Up the Normal Equations

To solve it, we just need the two ingredients of the normal equations. First, $A^T A$:

$$
A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & \dots & 1 \\ t_1 & t_2 & \dots & t_m \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & t_1 \\ 1 & t_2 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & t_m \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} m & \sum_{i=1}^m t_i \\ \sum_{i=1}^m t_i & \sum_{i=1}^m t_i^2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

And second, $A^T b$:

$$
A^T b = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & \dots & 1 \\ t_1 & t_2 & \dots & t_m \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ \vdots \\ b_m \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \sum_{i=1}^m b_i \\ \sum_{i=1}^m t_i b_i \end{bmatrix}
$$

Putting them together, the normal equations $A^T A \hat{x} = A^T b$ become

$$
\begin{bmatrix} m & \sum t_i \\ \sum t_i & \sum t_i^2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} C \\ D \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \sum b_i \\ \sum t_i b_i \end{bmatrix}
$$

This $2 \times 2$ system is uniquely solvable as long as not all $t_i$ values are identical, since that is what guarantees the columns of $A$ are linearly independent.

## Calculus Derivation: Minimizing $E(C, D)$

You might wonder whether we could have reached the same equations without any linear algebra. We can, using plain calculus. The total squared error as a function of the two unknowns is

$$
E(C, D) = \sum_{i=1}^m \big(b_i - (C + D t_i)\big)^2
$$

To find the minimum, we set the partial derivatives with respect to $C$ and $D$ to zero:

$$
\frac{\partial E}{\partial C} = -2 \sum_{i=1}^m \big(b_i - C - D t_i\big) = 0 \implies m C + D \sum_{i=1}^m t_i = \sum_{i=1}^m b_i
$$

$$
\frac{\partial E}{\partial D} = -2 \sum_{i=1}^m t_i \big(b_i - C - D t_i\big) = 0 \implies C \sum_{i=1}^m t_i + D \sum_{i=1}^m t_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^m t_i b_i
$$

Writing these two conditions in matrix form reproduces the normal equations $A^T A \hat{x} = A^T b$ identically. So the geometric route and the calculus route agree, as they must.

> - **When $Ax = b$ is solvable:** $b \in C(A)$, and we decompose $x = x_r + x_n \in \mathbb{R}^n$.

## Fitting Higher-Degree Models: Parabolas

Least squares is not restricted to straight lines. To fit a quadratic curve $y(t) = C + D t + E t^2$, we again demand that the curve pass through every data point:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C + D t_1 + E t_1^2 &= b_1 \\
C + D t_2 + E t_2^2 &= b_2 \\
&\;\vdots \\
C + D t_m + E t_m^2 &= b_m
\end{aligned}
$$

In matrix notation $Ax = b$ this becomes

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & t_1 & t_1^2 \\ 1 & t_2 & t_2^2 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & t_m & t_m^2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad x = \begin{bmatrix} C \\ D \\ E \end{bmatrix}, \quad b = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ \vdots \\ b_m \end{bmatrix}
$$

The key observation is that although the model is quadratic in $t$, it is strictly **linear in the unknown coefficients** $C, D, E$. Since $A$ is built from the known $t_i$ values, the normal equations $A^T A \hat{x} = A^T b$ apply directly, with no changes to the method.

## Dependent Columns and the Minimum-Norm Solution

So far we assumed the columns of $A$ are independent. What happens when they are not? Suppose the $m \times n$ matrix $A$ has linearly dependent columns, so $\operatorname{rank}(A) = r < n$. Then $A^T A$ is an $n \times n$ matrix of rank $r < n$, which makes it singular and non-invertible. Consequently, the formula $\hat{x} = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T b$ cannot even be formed. Let's see what survives.

1. **Uniqueness of the Projection $p$:**
   The projection $p = P b$ onto the column space $C(A)$ remains completely unique, because $C(A)$ is a well-defined subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$ no matter how redundant the columns are.
2. **Infinitely Many Least Squares Solutions:**
   Since $p \in C(A)$, the system $A \hat{x} = p$ is always consistent and solvable. However, $A$ now has a non-trivial nullspace ($\dim N(A) = n - r > 0$), so any nullspace vector can be added to a solution without changing the outcome:
   $$
   A (\hat{x} + x_n) = A \hat{x} + A x_n = p + \mathbf{0} = p \quad \text{for all } x_n \in N(A)
   $$
   By the fundamental decomposition $\mathbb{R}^n = C(A^T) \oplus N(A)$, every least squares solution decomposes uniquely as
   $$
   \hat{x} = \hat{x}_r + x_n \quad \text{where } \hat{x}_r \in C(A^T) \text{ and } x_n \in N(A)
   $$
3. **The Minimum-Norm Solution:**
   Among all these solutions, is there a natural one to prefer? Because the row space and nullspace are orthogonal complements ($C(A^T) \perp N(A)$), the Pythagorean Theorem gives
   $$
   \|\hat{x}\|^2 = \|\hat{x}_r + x_n\|^2 = \|\hat{x}_r\|^2 + \|x_n\|^2
   $$
   Since $\|x_n\|^2 \ge 0$, the shortest possible solution, the one that minimizes the Euclidean norm $\|\hat{x}\|$, is achieved by setting $x_n = \mathbf{0}$:
   $$
   \hat{x}^+ = \hat{x}_r \in C(A^T)
   $$

### Definition: The Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse $A^+$

We would like a single matrix that always hands us this shortest solution, even when $A^T A$ is not invertible. That matrix is the **Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse**, denoted $A^+$ (an $n \times m$ matrix). The idea is to invert what can be inverted, namely the map from row space to column space, and to discard the rest.

> [!abstract] Definition: The Pseudoinverse on the Fundamental Subspaces
> The matrix $A$ acts as a one-to-one and onto (invertible) linear mapping from its row space $C(A^T)$ to its column space $C(A)$. The **pseudoinverse** $A^+$ reverses this mapping on the four fundamental subspaces:
> 1. **On the Column Space $C(A)$:** For any $p \in C(A)$, $A^+ p$ maps $p$ back to the unique row space vector $\hat{x}_r \in C(A^T)$ satisfying $A \hat{x}_r = p$:
>    $$
>    A^+ p = \hat{x}_r \quad \text{where } A \hat{x}_r = p \text{ and } \hat{x}_r \in C(A^T)
>    $$
> 2. **On the Left Nullspace $N(A^T)$:** For any error vector $e \in N(A^T)$, $A^+$ sends it to zero:
>    $$
>    A^+ e = \mathbf{0} \quad \text{for all } e \in N(A^T)
>    $$

Let's see why this does the job. Take any right-hand side $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$, which decomposes uniquely into $b = p + e$ with $p \in C(A)$ and $e \in N(A^T)$. Applying $A^+$ and using linearity,

$$
\hat{x}^+ = A^+ b = A^+(p + e) = A^+ p + A^+ e = \hat{x}_r + \mathbf{0} = \hat{x}_r
$$

Thus $\hat{x}^+ = A^+ b$ directly generates the unique minimum-norm least squares solution.

> [!note] Cross-Reference: Explicit Formulas for $A^+$
> - For a detailed discussion of the pseudoinverse across all rank cases, including the standard left inverse $A^+ = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T$ when $A$ has full column rank, and the right inverse $A^+ = A^T (A A^T)^{-1}$ when $A$ has full row rank, see [[#4.5 The Pseudoinverse of a Matrix]].
> - The general construction of $A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^T$ for any matrix via the **Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)** is developed in Chapter 7.

## Properties of Least Squares Solutions

With the machinery in place, let's see how least squares solutions respond when we modify the data.

### Linear Scaling and Constant Shifts

1. **Scaling $b$ by $c$:** If we multiply every measurement by $c$, the solution simply scales along with it:
   $$
   A \hat{x}_{\text{new}} = c b = c(A\hat{x}_{\text{old}}) \implies \hat{x}_{\text{new}} = c \hat{x}_{\text{old}} \quad(\because A \text{ is invertible})
   $$
2. **Shifting $b$ by a Constant $K$:**
   If $K$ is added to every measurement $b_i$, the vector becomes $b + K \mathbf{1}$, where $\mathbf{1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \dots & 1 \end{bmatrix}^T$.
   Because the first column of $A$ is $\mathbf{1}$, the vector $K \mathbf{1}$ is in $C(A)$ with exact solution $\begin{bmatrix} K \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$:
   $$
   A \begin{bmatrix} K \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = K \mathbf{1}
   $$
   Therefore:
   $$
   \hat{x}_{\text{new}} = \hat{x}_{\text{old}} + \begin{bmatrix} K \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
   $$
   The vertical intercept shifts by $K$, while the slope $D$ is unchanged.

## Orthogonal Columns and Centering Data

Solving the $2 \times 2$ normal equations is a bit messy because $C$ and $D$ are coupled. Can we make them independent? Yes: when the measurement times satisfy $\sum_{i=1}^m t_i = 0$, the two columns of $A$ are orthogonal, and the off-diagonal entries vanish:

$$
A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} m & \sum t_i \\ \sum t_i & \sum t_i^2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} m & 0 \\ 0 & \sum t_i^2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

The normal equations then decouple completely, and each unknown can be read off on its own:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} m & 0 \\ 0 & \sum t_i^2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} C \\ D \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \sum b_i \\ \sum t_i b_i \end{bmatrix} \implies C = \frac{\sum b_i}{m} = \bar{b}, \quad D = \frac{\sum t_i b_i}{\sum t_i^2}
$$

This is a pleasant situation, but our times rarely sum to zero on their own. The trick is that any set of times $t_i$ can be centered by subtracting their mean $\bar{t} = \frac{1}{m}\sum t_i$:

$$
T_i = t_i - \bar{t}
$$

Fitting $y = C^* + D T$ in terms of the centered times produces an uncoupled system. The equation in terms of the original times is then recovered by expanding:

$$
C^* + D(t - \bar{t}) = (C^* - D \bar{t}) + D t \implies C = C^* - D \bar{t}
$$

## Example: Outlier with Three Options for Error

Least squares is only one way of measuring error, and it behaves in a particular way when the data contains an outlier. The following example compares three different error measures on the same data.

**Problem:** Consider 10 measurements at times $t = 1, 2, \dots, 10$. Nine measurements are zero ($b_1 = \dots = b_9 = 0$), while $b_{10} = 40$ is an outlier. Fit the best horizontal line $y = C$ using three error norms:
1. **Least Squares ($L_2$ norm):** Minimize $E_2 = \sum_{i=1}^{10} e_i^2$.
2. **Least Maximum Error ($L_\infty$ norm):** Minimize $E_\infty = \max_i |e_i|$.
3. **Least Sum of Absolute Errors ($L_1$ norm):** Minimize $E_1 = \sum_{i=1}^{10} |e_i|$.

### 1. Least Squares ($L_2$ norm)

For a horizontal line, the matrix $A$ is just a single column of ones: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & \dots & 1 \end{bmatrix}^T_{10 \times 1}$. Then

$$
A^T A = [10], \quad A^T b = \sum_{i=1}^{10} b_i = 40
$$

and the normal equation gives

$$
10 C = 40 \implies C = 4
$$

So the least squares estimator is the sample **mean**:

$$
C = \bar{b} = \frac{40}{10} = 4
$$

### 2. Least Maximum Error ($L_\infty$ norm)

Here only the worst error matters. The error is $E_\infty(C) = \max(|C|, |40 - C|)$, and it is minimized when the two extreme errors balance:

$$
|C| = |40 - C| \implies C = 20
$$

The minimax estimator is the **midrange**, with $E_\infty = 20$.

### 3. Least Sum of Absolute Errors ($L_1$ norm)

Now we add up the absolute errors, so that

$$
E_1(C) = 9 |C| + |40 - C|
$$

Let's evaluate this across candidate points:
- For $C = 0$: $E_1(0) = 9(0) + 40 = 40$.
- For $C > 0$: every unit increase in $C$ increases the first term by $+9$ while decreasing the second by at most $-1$, yielding a net rate of $+8$.

Thus $E_1(C)$ is minimized at $C = 0$, giving $E_1 = 40$. The $L_1$ estimator is the sample **median** ($C = 0$). It completely ignores the outlier, demonstrating robustness, whereas the mean (least squares) is dragged toward the outlier and the midrange is dragged even further.

Before we leave least squares, there is one tidy consequence of the normal equations that is worth stating on its own. It follows directly from the fact that the error is orthogonal to every column of $A$.

> [!theorem] Zero Average Error in Least Squares
> Whenever $A$ includes a column of ones (constant intercept term), the error vector $e$ is orthogonal to that column:
> $$
> \mathbf{1}^T e = 0 \implies \sum_{i=1}^m e_i = 0
> $$
> The algebraic sum and sample mean of the residuals are always identically zero:
> $$
> \bar{e} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m e_i = 0
> $$

# 4.4 Orthonormal Bases and Gram-Schmidt

## Orthonormal Vectors and Orthogonal Matrices

Throughout this chapter, orthogonality has made life easier: perpendicular directions don't interfere with each other. Now let's push that idea as far as it can go. What if we also insist that every vector has length exactly $1$? Then we get the nicest possible set of building blocks for a subspace, and the formulas we've been wrestling with (projections, least squares) collapse into almost nothing.

> [!abstract] Definition: Orthonormal Vectors
> A set of vectors $\{q_1, q_2, \dots, q_n\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$ is **orthonormal** if every vector has unit length and distinct vectors are mutually orthogonal:
> $$
> q_i^T q_j = \delta_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } i = j \\ 0 & \text{if } i \ne j \end{cases}
> $$
> where $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta.

In words, the case $i = j$ says each $q_i$ is a unit vector ($q_i^T q_i = \|q_i\|^2 = 1$), while the case $i \ne j$ says any two different vectors are perpendicular. Both requirements are packed into the single symbol $\delta_{ij}$.

Now let's see what this looks like in matrix language. Put these vectors in as the columns of an $m \times n$ matrix:

$$
Q = \begin{bmatrix} q_1 & q_2 & \dots & q_n \end{bmatrix}
$$

When we compute $Q^T Q$, the $(i,j)$ entry is just the dot product $q_i^T q_j$, so the orthonormality condition translates directly into matrix multiplication:

$$
Q^T Q = \begin{bmatrix} q_1^T \\ q_2^T \\ \vdots \\ q_n^T \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} q_1 & q_2 & \dots & q_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} q_1^T q_1 & q_1^T q_2 & \dots & q_1^T q_n \\ q_2^T q_1 & q_2^T q_2 & \dots & q_2^T q_n \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ q_n^T q_1 & q_n^T q_2 & \dots & q_n^T q_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & \dots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I_n
$$

So "orthonormal columns" is exactly the statement $Q^T Q = I_n$. A special thing happens when the matrix is square, because then a left inverse is automatically a two-sided inverse.

> [!abstract] Definition: Orthogonal Matrix
> When $Q$ is square ($m = n$), the matrix is called an **Orthogonal Matrix**. In this case, the transpose is the two-sided inverse:
> $$
> Q^T = Q^{-1} \iff Q^T Q = Q Q^T = I_n
> $$

This is a remarkable gift: inverting an orthogonal matrix costs nothing, since we simply transpose it.

### Canonical Examples of Orthogonal Matrices

Let's meet three families that you'll see again and again.

**Rotation matrices.** A rotation of the plane by an angle $\theta$ is given by

$$
Q = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}
$$

Rotation preserves lengths, and its inverse is rotation by $-\theta$, which is exactly the transpose:

$$
Q^T = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} = Q^{-1}
$$

**Permutation matrices.** These reorder the coordinates of a vector without changing its length, so they are orthogonal as well:

$$
P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad P^T P = I
$$

**Householder reflection matrices.** For any unit vector $u \in \mathbb{R}^n$ ($\|u\| = 1$), the Householder matrix is

$$
H = I - 2 u u^T
$$

It's not obvious at first glance why this should be a reflection, so let's derive it from the projection matrices we already know.

> [!abstract] Derivation: Reflection Across a Hyperplane
> Let $u \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a unit vector ($\|u\| = 1$).
> The line spanned by $u$ is $L = \operatorname{span}(u)$, and the orthogonal hyperplane is $u^\perp = \{ x \in \mathbb{R}^n : u^T x = 0 \}$.
> 1. The orthogonal projection matrix onto $L$ is $P_u = u u^T$.
> 2. The projection matrix onto the mirror plane $u^\perp$ is:
>    $$
>    P = I - u u^T
>    $$
> 3. For any vector $x$, let $y$ denote its reflection across $u^\perp$. The midpoint between $x$ and $y$ must lie on the mirror plane and equal the projection $P x$:
>    $$
>    \frac{x + y}{2} = P x \implies x + y = 2 P x \implies y = (2 P - I) x
>    $$
> Substituting $P = I - u u^T$:
> $$
> y = \big(2(I - u u^T) - I\big) x = (I - 2 u u^T) x
> $$
> Therefore, the Householder reflection matrix is:
> $$
> H = I - 2 u u^T
> $$

The geometry is simple: the midpoint of a vector and its mirror image sits on the mirror, and that midpoint is just the projection onto the mirror. Solving for the mirror image gives $H$.

Let's now check the algebraic and spectral properties of $H$ one by one.

- **Symmetric:** $H^T = (I - 2 u u^T)^T = I - 2(u^T)^T u^T = I - 2 u u^T = H$.
- **Orthogonal (Involution):** Squaring $H$ and using $u^T u = 1$ gives
  $$
  H^2 = (I - 2 u u^T)(I - 2 u u^T) = I - 4 u u^T + 4 u(u^T u)u^T = I - 4 u u^T + 4 u u^T = I
  $$
  Since $H^T = H$ and $H^2 = I$, we have $H^T H = I$, proving $H$ is orthogonal ($H^{-1} = H^T = H$).
- **Eigenvalues and Determinant:** The vector $u$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $-1$, because the mirror flips the direction perpendicular to itself:
  $$
  H u = (I - 2 u u^T)u = u - 2 u(u^T u) = u - 2 u = -u
  $$
  Any vector $v \in u^\perp$ ($u^T v = 0$) lies in the mirror, so it is untouched, which is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $+1$:
  $$
  H v = (I - 2 u u^T)v = v - 2 u(0) = v
  $$
  Thus $H$ has one eigenvalue equal to $-1$ and $(n-1)$ eigenvalues equal to $+1$:
  $$
  \det(H) = (-1) \times (+1)^{n-1} = -1
  $$
  This confirms that geometric reflection reverses spatial orientation.

The same idea works for reflecting across a subspace that isn't a hyperplane, and it's worth stating in general form.

> [!note] General Way To Find Reflection
> In general, if we want to find the reflection of a vector about a subspace $W$, we first apply the orthogonal decomposition theorem and decompose $v$,
> $$
> v = \text{proj}_{W}(v) + \text{proj}_{W^{\perp}}(v)
> $$
> The reflected vector will have the same projection on $W$, but the projection on $W^{\perp}$ will be inverted,
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \text{refl}_{W}(v) & = \text{proj}_{W}(v) - \text{proj}_{W^{\perp}}(v) \\
>  & = \text{proj}_{W}(v) - (v - \text{proj}_{W}(v)) \\
>  & = 2 \cdot \text{proj}_{W}(v) - v
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> Any reflection matrix has the property $R^{2} = I$, since reflecting twice gives back the same vector.

### Preservation of Lengths and Angles (Isometry)

You may have noticed that all three examples above (rotations, permutations, reflections) move vectors around without stretching or squashing them. That's no coincidence, and it's the real reason orthonormal columns are so well behaved numerically. Let's make that precise.

> [!theorem] Preservation of Norms and Dot Products
> Multiplication by a matrix $Q$ with orthonormal columns preserves lengths and dot products:
> 1. **Length Preservation:** $\|Q x\| = \|x\|$ for every $x$.
> 2. **Dot Product Preservation:** $(Q x)^T (Q y) = x^T y$ for all $x, y$.
> 3. **Angle Preservation:** $\cos\angle(Qx, Qy) = \cos\angle(x, y)$.

**Proof:** For the length, we compute the squared norm and let $Q^T Q = I$ do the work:

$$
\|Q x\|^2 = (Q x)^T (Q x) = x^T Q^T Q x = x^T I x = x^T x = \|x\|^2 \implies \|Q x\| = \|x\|
$$

The same cancellation handles the dot product of two different vectors:

$$
(Q x)^T (Q y) = x^T Q^T Q y = x^T I y = x^T y
$$

Finally, because both lengths and dot products are preserved, angles between vectors remain identical:

$$
\cos\angle(Qx, Qy) = \frac{(Qx)^T (Qy)}{\|Qx\| \|Qy\|} = \frac{x^T y}{\|x\| \|y\|} = \cos\angle(x, y)
$$

$\blacksquare$

## Projections onto Orthonormal Bases

Let's now cash in on $Q^T Q = I$. Recall that projecting onto a subspace normally forces us to solve the normal equations, which involves inverting $A^T A$. When the subspace is spanned by orthonormal columns $Q$, that troublesome matrix is just the identity, and the projection formulas simplify dramatically.

The normal equations become

$$
Q^T Q \hat{x} = Q^T b \implies I \hat{x} = Q^T b \implies \hat{x} = Q^T b
$$

so no matrix inversion is needed. Each coordinate is found independently, as a single dot product:

$$
\hat{x}_i = q_i^T b
$$

The projection vector is then

$$
p = Q \hat{x} = Q Q^T b = \sum_{i=1}^n q_i (q_i^T b)
$$

which is simply the sum of the one-dimensional projections of $b$ onto each $q_i$. Likewise, the projection matrix reduces to

$$
P = Q (Q^T Q)^{-1} Q^T = Q I Q^T = Q Q^T
$$

### Orthogonal Expansions and Transforms

What if $Q$ is square ($n \times n$)? Then $Q Q^T = I$, so the projection of any vector $b \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is $b$ itself, since the columns already span the whole space:

$$
b = Q Q^T b = \begin{bmatrix} q_1 & q_2 & \dots & q_n \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} q_1^T b \\ q_2^T b \\ \vdots \\ q_n^T b \end{bmatrix} = \sum_{i=1}^n (q_i^T b) q_i
$$

This represents $b$ as the sum of its one-dimensional orthogonal projections. This expansion is the fundamental template for Fourier series, wavelet transforms, and the Discrete Cosine Transform.

## The Gram-Schmidt Process

Orthonormal bases are wonderful, but the vectors we are handed in practice are rarely perpendicular. So here is the natural question: given an arbitrary set of independent vectors, can we manufacture an orthonormal basis for the same space? Yes, and the recipe is the **Gram-Schmidt process**. It takes a set of linearly independent vectors $\{a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n\}$ and systematically converts them into an orthonormal basis $\{q_1, q_2, \dots, q_n\}$ spanning the same subspace.

### Step-by-Step Construction for Three Vectors ($a, b, c$)

The idea is to build the new directions one at a time, each time removing whatever component lies along the directions we've already built. Let's see this for three vectors $a, b, c$.

**First vector ($A$ and $q_1$).** There is nothing to subtract yet, so we accept $a$ as the first direction and normalize it:

$$
A = a, \quad q_1 = \frac{A}{\|A\|}
$$

**Second vector ($B$ and $q_2$).** We subtract from $b$ its projection onto $A$, which leaves only the part of $b$ that is genuinely new:

$$
B = b - \frac{A^T b}{A^T A} A = b - (q_1^T b) q_1, \quad q_2 = \frac{B}{\|B\|}
$$

Notice that $B$ is exactly the error vector from projecting $b$ onto $A$, so it is perpendicular to $A$: $A^T B = 0$.

**Third vector ($C$ and $q_3$).** Now we subtract from $c$ its projection onto the plane spanned by $A$ and $B$. Because $A \perp B$, the plane projection is just the sum of the separate projections onto $A$ and onto $B$, with no cross-terms to worry about:

$$
C = c - \frac{A^T c}{A^T A} A - \frac{B^T c}{B^T B} B = c - (q_1^T c) q_1 - (q_2^T c) q_2, \quad q_3 = \frac{C}{\|C\|}
$$

### The General Algorithm

The pattern is now clear: at step $k$, remove from $a_k$ its components along all the previous $q_j$, then normalize. For $k = 1, 2, \dots, n$:

$$
A_k = a_k - \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} (q_j^T a_k) q_j, \quad q_k = \frac{A_k}{\|A_k\|}
$$

At each step $k$, $q_k$ is orthogonal to all previously constructed vectors $q_1, \dots, q_{k-1}$, and the two lists span the same space at every stage:

$$
\operatorname{span}(q_1, q_2, \dots, q_k) = \operatorname{span}(a_1, a_2, \dots, a_k)
$$

As a consequence, since $q_k$ is orthogonal to the space spanned by $\{a_1, \dots, a_{k-1}\}$, we get:

$$
q_k^T a_j = 0 \quad \text{for all } j < k
$$

## The $A = QR$ Factorization

Gram-Schmidt does more than produce a nice basis. If we keep track of the coefficients it uses, the whole process can be expressed in compact matrix form as the **$QR$ factorization**, which is to Gram-Schmidt what $A = LU$ is to elimination.

Start by rearranging the Gram-Schmidt expansion for $a_k$. Each original vector is rebuilt from its components along the $q$'s:

$$
a_k = (q_1^T a_k) q_1 + (q_2^T a_k) q_2 + \dots + (q_k^T a_k) q_k
$$

where the diagonal coefficient is

$$
r_{kk} = q_k^T a_k = \|A_k\| > 0
$$

Since $q_i^T a_k = 0$ for all $i > k$, this relationship expresses each column of $A$ as a combination of only $q_1, \dots, q_k$. Collecting all the columns, we see a triangular matrix emerge:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & \dots & a_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} q_1 & q_2 & \dots & q_n \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} q_1^T a_1 & q_1^T a_2 & \dots & q_1^T a_n \\ 0 & q_2^T a_2 & \dots & q_2^T a_n \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & q_n^T a_n \end{bmatrix} = Q R
$$

Let's package this as a theorem.

> [!theorem] The $QR$ Decomposition
> Every $m \times n$ matrix $A$ with linearly independent columns can be factored into:
> $$
> A = Q R
> $$
> where:
> - $Q$ is an $m \times n$ matrix with orthonormal columns ($Q^T Q = I_n$).
> - $R = Q^T A$ is an $n \times n$ upper triangular matrix with strictly positive diagonal entries ($r_{ii} > 0$).

**Proof:** Apply Gram-Schmidt to the columns of $A$ to get the orthonormal columns of $Q$. Each $a_k$ is a combination of $q_1, \dots, q_k$ only, with coefficients $q_i^T a_k$ and diagonal entry $r_{kk} = q_k^T a_k = \|A_k\|$. This is strictly positive because the columns are independent, so $A_k \ne \mathbf{0}$. Collecting these coefficients column by column gives the upper triangular matrix $R$ in the factorization above, and multiplying $A = QR$ by $Q^T$ on the left gives $R = Q^T A$. $\blacksquare$

### Applying $A = QR$ to Least Squares

Why bother with this factorization? One reason is least squares. With $QR$ we can avoid forming $A^T A$ explicitly. Substituting $A = QR$ into the normal equations:

$$
A^T A \hat{x} = A^T b \implies (QR)^T (QR) \hat{x} = (QR)^T b
$$

$$
R^T (Q^T Q) R \hat{x} = R^T Q^T b \implies R^T R \hat{x} = R^T Q^T b
$$

Because $R$ is upper triangular with non-zero diagonal entries, $R^T$ is invertible. Multiplying by $(R^T)^{-1}$:

$$
R \hat{x} = Q^T b
$$

This triangular system is solved by **back-substitution**. It also eliminates the numerical instability associated with squaring the condition number in $(A^T A)^{-1}$.

## Problem Set 4.4 Solutions

### Exercise 6: Product of Orthogonal Matrices

**Problem:** If $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ are orthogonal matrices, prove that $Q_1 Q_2$ is also orthogonal.

**Solution:** We simply check that $Q_1 Q_2$ satisfies $Q^T Q = I$, using the reversal rule for transposes:

$$
(Q_1 Q_2)^T (Q_1 Q_2) = Q_2^T Q_1^T Q_1 Q_2 = Q_2^T (Q_1^T Q_1) Q_2 = Q_2^T I Q_2 = Q_2^T Q_2 = I
$$

Thus $(Q_1 Q_2)^{-1} = (Q_1 Q_2)^T$, confirming that the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal.

### Exercise 9: Projection Matrix from $Q$

**Problem:** Show that $P = Q Q^T$ satisfies the projection matrix properties.

**Solution:** A projection matrix must be symmetric and idempotent, so we check each property.

First, symmetry:

$$
P^T = (Q Q^T)^T = (Q^T)^T Q^T = Q Q^T = P
$$

Second, idempotence, where the middle $Q^T Q$ collapses to the identity:

$$
P^2 = (Q Q^T)(Q Q^T) = Q (Q^T Q) Q^T = Q I Q^T = Q Q^T = P
$$

### Exercise 10: Independence of Orthonormal Columns

**Problem:** Prove that orthonormal vectors $q_1, \dots, q_n$ are automatically linearly independent.

**Solution:** There are two natural ways to see this.

**Method 1 (Inner Product).** Suppose $\sum_{j=1}^n c_j q_j = \mathbf{0}$. Take the inner product of both sides with $q_i$:

$$
q_i^T \left(\sum_{j=1}^n c_j q_j\right) = q_i^T \mathbf{0} \implies \sum_{j=1}^n c_j (q_i^T q_j) = 0
$$

Because $q_i^T q_j = 0$ for $j \ne i$ and $q_i^T q_i = 1$, only one term survives:

$$
c_i (1) = 0 \implies c_i = 0 \quad \text{for all } i = 1, \dots, n
$$

Therefore, the vectors are linearly independent.

**Method 2 (Nullspace).** Let $Q = \begin{bmatrix} q_1 & \dots & q_n \end{bmatrix}$. If $Q x = \mathbf{0}$, multiply by $Q^T$:

$$
Q^T Q x = Q^T \mathbf{0} \implies I x = \mathbf{0} \implies x = \mathbf{0}
$$

Because the only vector in $N(Q)$ is $\mathbf{0}$, the columns are linearly independent.

### Exercise 19: Cholesky Relation $A^T A = R^T R$

**Problem:** If $A = QR$, express $A^T A$ in terms of $R$.

**Solution:** Substitute the factorization and let $Q^T Q = I$ cancel:

$$
A^T A = (QR)^T (QR) = R^T Q^T Q R = R^T I R = R^T R
$$

Since $R$ is upper triangular, $R^T$ is lower triangular. Thus $A^T A = R^T R$ is the **Cholesky factorization** of the symmetric positive definite matrix $A^T A$. In other words, Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization on $A$ is algebraically equivalent to Gaussian elimination on $A^T A$.

### Exercise 20(a): Inverses of Orthogonal Matrices

**Problem:** Show that $Q^{-1}$ is orthogonal when $Q$ is orthogonal.

**Solution:** Because $Q^{-1} = Q^T$, we get:

$$
(Q^{-1})^T Q^{-1} = (Q^T)^T Q^T = Q Q^T = I
$$

Thus $Q^{-1}$ is orthogonal.

# 4.5 The Pseudoinverse of a Matrix

What do we do when a matrix $A$ is rectangular or rank-deficient? In that case the usual two-sided inverse $A^{-1}$ simply does not exist. Still, the four fundamental subspaces give us enough structure to define a unique, generalized inverse, known as the **Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse** and denoted $A^+$.

## Connection to Previous Subspace Inverses

The nice thing is that $A^+$ is not a brand-new object. It quietly reproduces the one-sided inverses we have already met, and it extends them to the cases they couldn't handle. Let's go through the three situations one at a time.

**Full column rank ($\operatorname{rank}(A) = n$).** Here $A^T A$ is invertible, and $A^+$ is the standard left inverse:

$$
A^+ = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T \implies A^+ A = I_n
$$

**Full row rank ($\operatorname{rank}(A) = m$).** Here $A A^T$ is invertible, and $A^+$ is the standard right inverse:

$$
A^+ = A^T (A A^T)^{-1} \implies A A^+ = I_m
$$

**General rank-deficient case ($\operatorname{rank}(A) = r < \min(m, n)$).** Neither of the formulas above works now, so we fall back on the subspaces themselves. The key fact is that $A$ is an invertible mapping strictly from its row space $C(A^T)$ to its column space $C(A)$. The pseudoinverse $A^+$ reverses this mapping, sending $C(A)$ back to $C(A^T)$. What about the rest of the output space? Any $y \in N(A^T)$ is assigned $A^+ y = \mathbf{0}$.

> A complete, constructive derivation of the pseudoinverse $A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^T$ via the **Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)** is developed in Chapter 7.
