# 4.1 Orthogonality of Vectors and Subspaces

### Orthogonal Vectors

- Two vectors are called **orthogonal** when the angle between them is $90^\circ$.

$$\implies v^T w = 0 \implies v \text{ and } w \text{ are } \perp$$

### Orthogonal Subspaces

- Two subspaces $V$ and $W$ are called orthogonal if every vector in $V$ is $\perp$ to every vector in $W$.
- This means that the only vector common to $V$ and $W$ is the $\mathbf{0}$ vector, because a vector cannot be $\perp$ to itself unless it is $0$.

$$\implies \dim(V \cap W) = 0$$

$$\implies \dim(V + W) = \dim V + \dim W$$

- The above result also proves that if $V$ and $W$ are orthogonal subspaces in $\mathbb{R}^n$, then:

$$\dim V + \dim W \le n$$

- When the dimensions add to $n$, the two subspaces are called **orthogonal complements**.

**Statement:** All vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ can be written as a linear combination of vectors of two vector spaces that are orthogonal complements.

*Proof:* Consider two vector spaces $V$ and $W$ that are orthogonal complements.
- Let $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_s$ be a basis for $V$ and $w_1, w_2, \dots, w_t$ be a basis for $W$.
- $s + t = n$, and vectors $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_s, w_1, \dots, w_t$ are all independent; hence they form a basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$.
- Any vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ can be written as:

$$x = (x_1 v_1 + x_2 v_2 + \dots + x_s v_s) + (x_{s+1} w_1 + \dots + x_{s+t} w_t)$$

$$\implies x = x_V + x_W \quad \text{where } x_V \in V \text{ and } x_W \in W$$

---

### Orthogonal Subspaces of $A$

#### (1) $C(A^T)$ and $N(A)$ are orthogonal complements in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

*Proof:*
$$Ax = 0 \implies x \in N(A)$$

$A^T y$ gives all vectors in the row space:

$$\implies x^T (A^T y) = (x^T A^T) y = (Ax)^T y = 0$$

$$\implies x^T (A^T y) = 0$$

- Hence $C(A^T)$ and $N(A)$ are orthogonal subspaces.
- Also, $r + (n - r) = n \implies$ they are orthogonal complements.

#### (2) $C(A)$ and $N(A^T)$ are also orthogonal complement subspaces in $\mathbb{R}^m$.

*Proof:*
$$A^T x = 0 \implies x \in N(A^T)$$

$Ay$ gives all vectors in the column space of $A$:

$$\implies x^T (Ay) = (A^T x)^T y = 0$$

$$\implies x \perp \text{ to all } Ay$$

- Also, $\mathrm{rank}(A) + \mathrm{rank}(N(A^T)) = r + (m - r) = m$.
- Hence $C(A)$ and $N(A^T)$ are orthogonal complements of each other.

---

### Key Notes on Solutions to $Ax = b$

**Note (i):** All solutions to the equation $Ax = b$ can be written as the sum of a vector from the row space and a vector from the null space:

$$\implies x = x_r + x_n$$

**Note (ii):** All equations $Ax = b$ that are solvable have a **unique** solution $x_r \in C(A^T)$.

*Proof:* Let there be two different solutions $x_1$ and $x_2$:
$$\implies A x_1 = A x_2 = b \implies A(x_1 - x_2) = 0$$
$$\implies x_1 - x_2 \in N(A), \quad \text{but } x_1 - x_2 \in C(A^T)$$
The only vector common to $C(A^T)$ and $N(A)$ is $0$:
$$\implies x_1 - x_2 = 0 \implies x_1 = x_2$$

**Note (iii):**
$$A(x_r + x_n) = A x_r + A x_n = A x_r$$
$$\implies x = x_r \text{ is also a solution and is unique.}$$

**Note (iv):**
$$|x|^2 = |x_r|^2 + |x_n|^2$$
$$\implies \text{The minimum-norm solution to } Ax = b \text{ is } x = x_r \text{ in the row space plus } x_n = 0 \text{ from } N(A).$$

**Note (v):** Another proof that $x_r$ and $x_n$ are independent.

*Proof:* If $x_r + x_n = 0 \implies x_r = -x_n$ are in both subspaces, then $x_r = x_n = 0$.
- But $x_r$ is a linear combination of the basis of $C(A^T)$ and $x_n$ is a linear combination of the basis of $N(A)$; for these to be zero, every coefficient of the bases in $x_r$ and $x_n$ must be $0$.
$$\implies \text{Bases for } C(A^T) \text{ and } N(A) \text{ are independent.}$$

**Note (vi):** When combining bases from subspaces, we only need to prove one of the properties about bases, and if the count is just right then the other property is automatically implied:
- Any $n$ independent vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ must span $\mathbb{R}^n$. So they are a basis.
- Any $n$ vectors that span $\mathbb{R}^n$ must be independent. So they are a basis.

## 4.2 Projection Onto Lines and Subspaces

### Projection Onto Lines

- The projection of vector $b$ onto the line through vector $a$ is a point on the line that is closest to point $b$.

> **[Figure Placeholder: Projection onto a Line]**
> *Description:* 2D coordinate axes with a line passing through the origin along vector $a$. A vector $b$ originates from the origin at angle $\theta$ to $a$. The projection vector $p$ lies along vector $a$, and the perpendicular error vector $e = b - p$ drops from the tip of $b$ onto line $a$ at a right angle ($90^\circ$).
> *(Reference: `extracted/page-04.png`, left column)*

- The vector $e = b - p$ is called the **error**; it tells how much $b$ is off from $a$.
- Since $p$ is a vector on line $a$, we can write:
  $$p = a \hat{x}$$
- Computing $\hat{x}$ will give us $p$.
- We use the fact that $e$ is $\perp$ to $a$:
  $$\implies a^T e = a^T (b - p) = a^T (b - a \hat{x}) = 0$$
  $$\implies a^T (b - a \hat{x}) = 0$$

- Solving this gives:
  $$\hat{x} = \frac{a^T b}{a^T a}$$

- This gives the projection vector $p$ as:
  $$p = a \frac{a^T b}{a^T a}$$

- We can rearrange the matrix multiplication in the formula of $p$ to obtain the **Projection Matrix** $P$:
  $$P = \frac{a a^T}{a^T a} \quad \text{and} \quad p = P b$$

- The projection matrix gives the projection of any vector onto $a$. It has two key properties:
  1. **Symmetric:** $P^T = P$
  2. **Idempotent:** $P^2 = P$ (applying it twice is the same as applying it once, which makes sense because the projection of $p$ onto $a$ is just $p$ itself).

- We can now also calculate the error vector as:
  $$e = b - Pb = (I - P) b$$

- We can also calculate the projection length:
  $$|p| = |a| \frac{|a||b| \cos\theta}{|a||a|} = |b| \cos\theta$$

**Note (i):** If we multiply $a$ by a scalar $c$, the projection does not change:
$$P = \frac{(c a)(c a)^T}{(c a)^T (c a)} = \frac{c^2}{c^2} \frac{a a^T}{a^T a} \implies p = a \frac{a^T b}{a^T a}$$

**Note (ii):** But if we multiply $b$ by a scalar $c$, then the projection also scales by the same factor:
$$p' = \frac{a a^T}{a^T a} (c b) = c \frac{a a^T}{a^T a} b = c p$$

### Proof of $\mathrm{rank}(A^T A) = \mathrm{rank}(A)$

- Before moving to the projection of $b$ onto a subspace, we want to prove the fact that $A^T A$ and $A$ have the same rank.
- We do this by proving they have the same null space:
  - If $x \in N(A)$, then $(A^T A)x = A^T (Ax) = A^T(0) = 0 \implies x \in N(A^T A)$.
  - If $x \in N(A^T A)$, then $x^T (A^T A) x = 0$:
    $$\implies (x^T A^T)(Ax) = 0 \implies (Ax)^T Ax = 0$$
    $$\implies |Ax|^2 = 0 \implies Ax = 0$$
  - Hence $x \in N(A)$ also. This proves that $N(A^T A) = N(A)$.

- $A^T A$ is $n \times n$ and $A$ is $m \times n$. The dimensions of their null spaces are $n - r_1$ and $n - r_2$:
  $$\implies n - r_1 = n - r_2 \implies r_1 = r_2$$
  $$\implies \mathrm{rank}(A^T A) = \mathrm{rank}(A)$$

**Note:** From the above result we see that if $\mathrm{rank}(A) = n$, then $A^T A$ is an $n \times n$, symmetric, and invertible matrix.

**Note:** We can use the above result to also show that the left inverse $A^{-1}_{\text{left}}$ exists when $A$ has full column rank:
$$\implies (A^T A)^{-1} A^T A = I \implies \big((A^T A)^{-1} A^T\big) A = I$$
$$\implies A^{-1}_{\text{left}} = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T$$

### Projection Onto Subspaces

- Consider $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$ as $n$ independent vectors that form a basis for an $n$-dimensional subspace in $\mathbb{R}^m$.
- We create a matrix $A$ with these vectors as columns. The columns span the column space $C(A)$, and $A$ has full column rank, which tells us that $A^T A$ is invertible.
- We want to find the projection of vector $b$ on $C(A)$; let it be $p$:
  $$p = A \hat{x}$$

- The error follows the same formula:
  $$e = b - p = b - A \hat{x}$$

- The error vector is $\perp$ to $C(A)$ by definition. This gives us $n$ equations:
  $$a_1^T e = 0, \quad a_2^T e = 0, \quad \dots, \quad a_n^T e = 0$$

- All of these combined can be written as:
  $$\implies A^T e = 0 \implies A^T (b - A\hat{x}) = 0$$
  $$\implies A^T A \hat{x} = A^T b$$
  $$\implies \hat{x} = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T b$$

- Using $\hat{x}$, we get the projection $p$ as:
  $$p = A \hat{x} = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T b$$

- From the above formula we get the **Projection Matrix** $P$:
  $$P = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$$

- The two properties of $P$ still hold:
  1. $P^T = P$
  2. $P^2 = P$

**Note:** In general we cannot write $(A^T A)^{-1}$ as $A^{-1} (A^T)^{-1}$ because $A^{-1}$ might not exist. But if $A$ is square and invertible, then:
$$P = A A^{-1} (A^T)^{-1} A^T = I$$
This is expected: if $A$ is invertible, the columns of $A$ span the entire $\mathbb{R}^m$ and $b$ is inside $C(A)$; hence its projection will be $b$ itself:
$$\implies p = b \implies P b = b \implies P = I$$

**Note:** $e$ and $p$ are vectors in two orthogonal subspaces, and we are finding them to split any vector $b$ into two components:
1. $p \implies$ component in the subspace
2. $e \implies$ component $\perp$ to the subspace

$$\implies b = p + e$$

### Problem Set 4.2

**(7)** If $a_1$ and $a_2$ are orthogonal vectors, then $P = P_1 + P_2$, where $P$ is the projection matrix onto the subspace spanned by $a_1, a_2$, and $P_i$ is the projection matrix onto $a_i$. Prove this is true.

*Solution:*
$$P_1 = \frac{a_1 a_1^T}{a_1^T a_1}, \quad P_2 = \frac{a_2 a_2^T}{a_2^T a_2}$$

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad A^T = \begin{bmatrix} a_1^T \\ a_2^T \end{bmatrix}$$

$$A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} a_1^T \\ a_2^T \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a_1^T a_1 & a_1^T a_2 \\ a_2^T a_1 & a_2^T a_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a_1^T a_1 & 0 \\ 0 & a_2^T a_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Since $a_1^T a_2 = a_2^T a_1 = 0$ as $a_1$ and $a_2$ are orthogonal. Let $k_i = a_i^T a_i$:

$$(A^T A)^{-1} = \frac{1}{k_1 k_2} \begin{bmatrix} k_2 & 0 \\ 0 & k_1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{k_1} & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{k_2} \end{bmatrix}$$

$$P = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{k_1} & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{k_2} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a_1^T \\ a_2^T \end{bmatrix}$$

$$P = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \frac{a_1^T}{k_1} \\ \frac{a_2^T}{k_2} \end{bmatrix} = \frac{a_1 a_1^T}{k_1} + \frac{a_2 a_2^T}{k_2} = \frac{a_1 a_1^T}{a_1^T a_1} + \frac{a_2 a_2^T}{a_2^T a_2}$$

$$\implies P = P_1 + P_2$$

**Note:**
(i) We can extend this idea to any number of orthogonal basis vectors.
(ii) If $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$ are $n$ independent orthogonal vectors, then $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$ span $\mathbb{R}^n$ and hence:
$$P = P_1 + P_2 + \dots + P_n = I$$

**(18)** If $P^2 = P$, then show that $(I - P)^2 = I - P$. When $P$ projects onto the column space of $A$, then $I - P$ projects onto \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_.

*Solution:*
$$(I - P)^2 = (I - P)(I - P) = I^2 - P - P + P^2 = I - 2P + P = I - P$$
$$\implies (I - P)^2 = I - P$$
When $P$ projects onto the column space of $A$, then $I - P$ projects onto the **left nullspace of $A$** ($N(A^T)$).

**(21) Conclusion:** To find the projection matrix $P$ onto the plane given by $x - y - 2z = 0$, we can find two vectors on the plane, construct the matrix $A$, and then $P = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$.

  $$P' = \frac{e e^T}{e^T e}$$
  Then the projection matrix $P$ onto the plane is:
  $$P' = I - P \implies P = I - P'$$

**(30)** If $B$ has rank $m$, then show that $B B^T$ is invertible if $B$ has full row rank.

*Solution:* We can prove this the same way we did for $A^T A$. Let $B$ ($m \times n$) have full row rank ($= m$).
- $B B^T$ is an $m \times m$ square matrix.
- If $x \in N(B B^T) \implies B B^T x = 0 \implies B(B^T x) = 0 \implies B^T x \in N(B)$.
- But $B^T x$ is also in the row space of $B$ ($C(B^T)$), and the only vector in both $N(B)$ and $C(B^T)$ is the $0$ vector.
$$\implies B^T x = 0 \implies x \in N(B^T)$$
$$\implies N(B B^T) = N(B^T)$$
$$\implies \dim N(B B^T) = \dim N(B^T)$$
$$\implies m - r_1 = m - r_2 \implies r_1 = r_2$$
- The rank of $B B^T = \mathrm{rank}(B^T) = m$:
$$\implies \mathrm{rank}(B B^T) = m$$
- So if $B$ has full row rank, then $B B^T$ is invertible.

**(34)** If $P_1$ and $P_2$ are projection matrices, then $P_1 P_2$ is a projection matrix if and only if:
$$P_1 P_2 = P_2 P_1$$

*Solution:* If $P_1 P_2$ is a projection matrix, then:
$$(P_1 P_2)^T = P_1 P_2$$
$$\implies (P_1 P_2)^T = P_2^T P_1^T = P_2 P_1 \quad (\because P_1 \text{ and } P_2 \text{ are symmetric projection matrices})$$
$$\implies P_1 P_2 = P_2 P_1$$

Also, idempotence holds:
$$(P_1 P_2)^2 = (P_1 P_2)(P_1 P_2) = (P_1 P_2)^T (P_1 P_2) = P_2^T P_1^T P_1 P_2 = P_2^T P_1^T P_2 = (P_1 P_2)^T P_2 = P_1 P_2 P_2 = P_1 P_2^2 = P_1 P_2$$
$$\implies (P_1 P_2)^2 = P_1 P_2$$

## 4.3 Least Squares Approximations

- A lot of times in the real world the equations given by $Ax = b$ are not solvable; usually this is due to having more equations than unknowns ($m > n$).
- $Ax = b$ not solvable means that $b \notin C(A)$. But we still want to do something: we want to find some vector $Ax$ such that the error $E = |Ax - b|^2$ is minimized:
  $$E = |Ax - b|^2 = (a_1 - b_1)^2 + (a_2 - b_2)^2 + \dots + (a_m - b_m)^2$$

- We know that we can write:
  $$b = p + e$$
  $$E = |Ax - b|^2 = |(Ax - p) - e|^2$$

- The vectors $Ax - p$ and $e$ are orthogonal, so by the Pythagorean theorem:
  $$E = |Ax - b|^2 = |Ax - p|^2 + |e|^2$$

- The error $e$ is a fixed value that cannot be changed, but we can change the term $Ax - p$.
- Hence, to minimize the right-hand side, we choose $Ax = p$, and then the error is minimized with:
  $$E = |e|^2$$

- In short, whenever $Ax = b$ is not solvable, we should solve the **Normal Equations** given by:
  $$A^T A \hat{x} = A^T b$$

- The normal equations are always solvable, and the $\hat{x}$ that we get is such that the error $E = |A\hat{x} - b|^2 = |e|^2$ is minimized.
- This is called the **least squares approximation** because we are minimizing the sum of squared errors.

### Fitting a Straight Line

- Consider we have $m$ points ($m > 2$) in a two-dimensional plane: at times $t_1, t_2, \dots, t_m$ the heights are $b_1, b_2, \dots, b_m$.
- The best line $C + Dt$ minimizes the error $E = e_1^2 + e_2^2 + \dots + e_m^2$:
  $$E = \big(b_1 - (C + D t_1)\big)^2 + \big(b_2 - (C + D t_2)\big)^2 + \dots + \big(b_m - (C + D t_m)\big)^2$$

- Here we have $m$ equations with two unknowns $C$ and $D$:
  $$\begin{aligned}
  C + D t_1 &= b_1 \\
  C + D t_2 &= b_2 \\
  &\vdots \\
  C + D t_m &= b_m
  \end{aligned}$$

- These equations can be written as $Ax = b$, where:
  $$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & t_1 \\ 1 & t_2 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & t_m \end{bmatrix}, \quad x = \begin{bmatrix} C \\ D \end{bmatrix}, \quad b = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ \vdots \\ b_m \end{bmatrix}$$

- To find the best $C$ and $D$, we need to solve $A^T A x = A^T b$:
  $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & \dots & 1 \\ t_1 & t_2 & \dots & t_m \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & t_1 \\ 1 & t_2 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & t_m \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} C \\ D \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & \dots & 1 \\ t_1 & t_2 & \dots & t_m \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ \vdots \\ b_m \end{bmatrix}$$

- Computing the matrix products:
  $$A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} m & \sum t_i \\ \sum t_i & \sum t_i^2 \end{bmatrix}_{2 \times 2}, \quad A^T b = \begin{bmatrix} \sum b_i \\ \sum t_i b_i \end{bmatrix}_{2 \times 1}$$

  $$\begin{bmatrix} m & \sum t_i \\ \sum t_i & \sum t_i^2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} C \\ D \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \sum b_i \\ \sum t_i b_i \end{bmatrix}$$

- The above system gives the best $C$ and $D$. The equations are always solvable as $A^T A$ is an invertible square matrix (provided not all $t_i$ are equal).

---

### Using Calculus to Minimize $E$

- If we minimize $E$ using calculus, we differentiate with respect to $C$ and $D$ and set them to zero:
  $$\frac{\partial E}{\partial C} = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\partial E}{\partial D} = 0$$

- Surprisingly, the two equations that we get from calculus are the exact same equations we get from $A^T A \hat{x} = A^T b$.

### Fitting a Parabola

- We don't always want to fit $m$ points into a line; sometimes we want to fit them to the nearest parabola:
  $$\begin{aligned}
  C + D t_1 + E t_1^2 &= b_1 \\
  C + D t_2 + E t_2^2 &= b_2 \\
  &\vdots \\
  C + D t_m + E t_m^2 &= b_m
  \end{aligned}$$

  $$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & t_1 & t_1^2 \\ 1 & t_2 & t_2^2 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & t_m & t_m^2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad x = \begin{bmatrix} C \\ D \\ E \end{bmatrix}, \quad b = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ \vdots \\ b_m \end{bmatrix}$$

- Least squares still works because the coefficients $C, D, E$ are still linear.

---

### Dependent Columns in $A$: What is $\hat{x}$?

- So far we assumed that $A$ has independent columns, but that might not always be the case. In that situation, what do we do?
- Let $p$ be the projection of $b$ in $C(A)$:
  $$A \hat{x} = p$$
- Since $A$ has dependent columns, there are infinitely many solutions for $\hat{x} = x_r + x_n$.
- Which $\hat{x}$ is the best? We will learn later that the best (shortest) solution is the one where:
  $$x_n = 0 \implies \hat{x} = x_r$$

---

### Effects of Scaling and Shifting $b$

#### Scaling $b$:
- If we multiply $b$ by a factor $c$, then $C$ and $D$ also increase by the same factor:
  $$A \hat{x}_{\text{new}} = c b = c(A \hat{x}_{\text{old}}) = A(c \hat{x}_{\text{old}})$$
  Since $A$ has full column rank:
  $$\implies \hat{x}_{\text{new}} = c \hat{x}_{\text{old}}$$

#### Shifting $b$ by a constant $K$:
- If we add $K$ to all $b_i$, then $K$ gets added to $C$ as well:

> **[Figure Placeholder: Vertical Shift of Fitted Line]**
> *Description:* Plot showing the original data points fitted by the line $C + Dt$, and the shifted data points (each increased vertically by constant $K$) fitted by the parallel line $(K + C) + Dt$.
> *(Reference: `extracted/page-12.png`, left column)*

$$A \hat{x}_{\text{new}} = \begin{bmatrix} K \\ \vdots \\ K \end{bmatrix} + b = \begin{bmatrix} K \\ \vdots \\ K \end{bmatrix} + A \hat{x}_{\text{old}}$$

The solution to $Ax = \begin{bmatrix} K \\ \vdots \\ K \end{bmatrix}$ is straightforward: $x = \begin{bmatrix} K \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$A \hat{x}_{\text{new}} = A \begin{bmatrix} K \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + A \hat{x}_{\text{old}} \implies \hat{x}_{\text{new}} = \begin{bmatrix} K \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \hat{x}_{\text{old}}$$

### Orthogonal Columns

- If the measurement times $t_i$ add up to $0$ ($\sum t_i = 0$), then the columns of $A$ are orthogonal ($\perp$).
- In this case:
  $$A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} m & \sum t_i \\ \sum t_i & \sum t_i^2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} m & 0 \\ 0 & \sum t_i^2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \text{a diagonal matrix.}$$

- This makes solving $A^T A \hat{x} = A^T b$ much easier! Sometimes it is worth making the times orthogonal by subtracting the mean $t_{\text{mean}} = \frac{\sum t_i}{m}$:
  $$T = t - \frac{\sum t_i}{m}$$

- Now we solve for $C + DT$, which is super simple, and then we can always recover the equation for the original measurements:
  $$C + DT = C + D(t - t_{\text{mean}}) = (C - D t_{\text{mean}}) + Dt$$

### Example: Outlier with Three Options for Error

**Problem:** Start with 9 measurements $b_1$ to $b_9$, all zero, at times $t = 1, \dots, 9$. The tenth measurement $b_{10} = 40$ is an outlier. Find the best horizontal line $y = C$ to fit the ten points $(1, 0)$ to $(10, 40)$ using three error norms:
1. **Least Squares:** $E_2 = e_1^2 + \dots + e_{10}^2$
2. **Least Maximum Error ($L_\infty$):** $E_\infty = |e_{\max}|$
3. **Least Sum of Absolute Errors ($L_1$):** $E_1 = |e_1| + \dots + |e_{10}|$

*Solution:*
1. **Least Squares:** Using the normal equation with $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ \vdots \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}_{10 \times 1}$:
   $$A^T A = [10], \quad A^T A \hat{x} = [10][C] = [10C]$$
   $$A^T b = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & \dots & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \\ 40 \end{bmatrix} = [40]$$
   $$10C = 40 \implies C = 4$$

2. **Least Maximum Error:**
   The error is minimized when $C$ is halfway between the extremes $0$ and $40$:
   $$C = 20 \implies E_\infty = 20$$

3. **Least Sum of Errors:**
   $$E_1 = 9|C| + |40 - C|$$
   This is minimized at the median of the data:
   $$C = 0 \implies E_1 = 40$$

**Note:** The first column of $A$ is all $1$s, and the error vector $e$ is $\perp C(A)$:
$$\implies e_1 + e_2 + \dots + e_m = 0$$
This means that the average error is always zero:
$$\hat{e} = \frac{\sum e_i}{m} = 0$$

## 4.4 Orthonormal Bases and Gram-Schmidt

**Definition:** The $n$ vectors $q_1, \dots, q_n$ are **orthonormal** if:
$$q_i^T q_j = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{when } i \ne j \\ 1 & \text{when } i = j \end{cases}$$

- In short: all vectors are unit length and mutually perpendicular ($\perp$).
- A matrix $Q$ with orthonormal columns has the fundamental property:
  $$Q^T Q = I$$

- When $Q$ is square, $Q^T$ is the two-sided inverse:
  $$Q^T = Q^{-1} \implies Q^T Q = Q Q^T = I$$

---

### Examples of $Q$

#### (i) Rotation
$$Q = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$$
- $Q$ rotates a 2D vector by angle $\theta$.
- $Q^T$ rotates a 2D vector by $-\theta$:
  $$Q^T = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$$
- Substituting $\theta \to -\theta$ in $Q$ directly gives back $Q^T$.

#### (ii) Permutation
Matrices that change the order of rows/columns, e.g.:
$$P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
$$P^T P = I \implies P^T = P^{-1}$$
- Every permutation matrix is an orthogonal matrix.

#### (iii) Reflection
If $u$ is any unit vector ($|u| = 1$), then:
$$Q = I - 2uu^T$$
- This $Q$ is an orthogonal matrix:
  $$Q^T = Q, \quad Q^{-1} = Q^T, \quad Q^2 = Q^T Q = I$$
- $Qx$ reflects $x$ across the mirror hyperplane $\perp$ to $u$.
  - In 2D: reflection across the line $\perp$ to $u$.
  - In higher dimensions: reflection across the hyperplane $\perp$ to $u$.
- This is called a **Householder reflection** (or Householder transformation):
  $$H = I - 2uu^T$$

---

### Orthogonal Matrix vs. Matrix $Q$

- **Orthogonal Matrix:** Any matrix with orthonormal columns is denoted by $Q$. But only when it is a **square** matrix is it formally called an **Orthogonal Matrix**.

**Note (Length Preservation):** If $Q$ has orthonormal columns, it leaves lengths unchanged:
$$|Qx| = |x|$$

*Proof:*
$$|Qx|^2 = (Qx)^T (Qx) = x^T Q^T Q x = x^T I x = x^T x = |x|^2 \implies |Qx| = |x|$$

**Note (Dot Product Preservation):** $Q$ also preserves dot products:
$$(Qx)^T (Qy) = x^T Q^T Q y = x^T I y = x^T y$$

---

### Projections Using Orthonormal Bases

- Suppose the basis vectors are orthonormal; in that case $A \to Q$.
- The projection formulas simplify tremendously:
  1. $A^T A \hat{x} = A^T b \implies Q^T Q \hat{x} = Q^T b \implies \hat{x} = Q^T b$
  2. $p = A \hat{x} \implies p = Q Q^T b$
  3. $P = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T \implies P = Q(Q^T Q)^{-1} Q^T \implies P = Q Q^T$

- Orthonormal vectors make calculations very simple and are preferred in numerical computing.
- If $Q$ is square orthogonal, then $Q Q^T = I$ and its columns span the entire space:
  $$p = Q Q^T b = b$$

- If $q_1, \dots, q_n$ are the columns of $Q$, we can decompose $b$ into the sum of its 1D projections:
  $$b = Q Q^T b = \begin{bmatrix} q_1 & \dots & q_n \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} q_1^T b \\ \vdots \\ q_n^T b \end{bmatrix}$$
  $$b = q_1 (q_1^T b) + q_2 (q_2^T b) + \dots + q_n (q_n^T b)$$

- The transform $Q Q^T = I$ is the foundation of Fourier series and all great transforms of applied mathematics: break vectors $b$ (or functions) into perpendicular pieces, then adding the pieces puts the vectors and functions back together.

---

### The Gram-Schmidt Process

- This process converts any set of independent vectors into a set of orthogonal / orthonormal vectors that span the same subspace.
- It iteratively converts each vector $a_i$ into an orthogonal vector by subtracting its projection onto the previously computed orthogonal vectors.

**Procedure for vectors $a, b, c$:**
1. Choose $A$ as the first vector:
   $$A = a$$

2. Convert $b$ to $B$ by subtracting its projection onto $A$:
   $$B = b - \frac{A^T b}{A^T A} A$$
   *(Note that we subtracted the projection of $b$ onto $A$; this is the perpendicular error $e$.)*

3. Convert $c$ to $C$ by subtracting its projection onto the subspace spanned by $A$ and $B$. Since $A \perp B$, the projection onto the plane is simply the sum of projections onto $A$ and $B$:
   $$P = P_1 + P_2 = \frac{A^T c}{A^T A} A + \frac{B^T c}{B^T B} B$$
   $$\implies C = c - \frac{A^T c}{A^T A} A - \frac{B^T c}{B^T B} B$$

4. Normalize each orthogonal vector by dividing by its length to obtain orthonormal vectors:
   $$q_1 = \frac{A}{|A|}, \quad q_2 = \frac{B}{|B|}, \quad q_3 = \frac{C}{|C|}$$

**Note:** In the $i$-th step we create $q_i$ from vector $a_i$. This vector is $\perp$ to all of $q_1, q_2, \dots, q_{i-1}$.
- But since $q_1, q_2, \dots, q_{i-1}$ are linear combinations of $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_{i-1}$ (and vice-versa), this means:
  $$q_i^T a_j = 0 \quad \text{when } j < i$$

---

### The Factorization $A = QR$

- We converted matrix $A$ to $Q$ using the Gram-Schmidt process. There is a matrix $R$ that connects them and captures the entire process:
  $$A = Q R$$
  *(where $R$ is not the row-reduced echelon matrix).*

- To get the matrix $R$, multiply both sides on the left by $Q^T$:
  $$Q^T A = Q^T Q R = I R \implies R = Q^T A$$

- Writing out the matrix multiplication:
  $$R = \begin{bmatrix} q_1^T \\ q_2^T \\ \vdots \\ q_n^T \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & \dots & a_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} q_1^T a_1 & q_1^T a_2 & \dots & q_1^T a_n \\ q_2^T a_1 & q_2^T a_2 & \dots & q_2^T a_n \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ q_n^T a_1 & q_n^T a_2 & \dots & q_n^T a_n \end{bmatrix}$$

- Since $q_i^T a_j = 0$ whenever $j < i$, all elements below the main diagonal vanish:
  $$R = \begin{bmatrix} q_1^T a_1 & q_1^T a_2 & \dots & q_1^T a_n \\ 0 & q_2^T a_2 & \dots & q_2^T a_n \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & q_n^T a_n \end{bmatrix}$$
  Hence $R$ is **upper triangular**.

- **Summary:** Any $m \times n$ matrix $A$ with independent columns can be factorized as $A = QR$, where $Q$ has orthonormal columns ($Q^T Q = I$) and $R$ is upper triangular with positive diagonal entries.

#### Using $A = QR$ in Least Squares Calculations:
$$A^T A \hat{x} = A^T b \implies (QR)^T (QR) \hat{x} = (QR)^T b$$
$$R^T Q^T Q R \hat{x} = R^T Q^T b \implies R^T R \hat{x} = R^T Q^T b$$
Since $R$ is upper triangular and invertible ($R^T$ is invertible):
$$R \hat{x} = Q^T b$$
*(This is solved easily by back-substitution!)*

   $$q_1^T (c_1 q_1 + c_2 q_2 + \dots + c_n q_n) = 0$$
   $$c_1 (q_1^T q_1) + c_2 (q_1^T q_2) + \dots + c_n (q_1^T q_n) = 0$$

# 5. Determinants

- The determinant was introduced to assign a single number to the 2D arrangement of coefficients of an $n \times n$ system of linear equations.
- **Significance:**
  - If this number comes out to be **non-zero**, the system is solvable and has a **unique** solution.
  - If it comes out to be **$0$**, the system may have **$0$ or infinitely many solutions**.

### 3 Basic Properties of Determinants

1. **Identity:**
   $$\det I = 1$$
2. **Row Exchange:**
   Row exchanges reverse the sign:
   $$\det A \to -\det A$$
3. **Linearity with respect to each row:**
   $$\det \begin{bmatrix} c v + d w \\ \text{row } 2 \\ \vdots \\ \text{row } n \end{bmatrix} = c \det \begin{bmatrix} v \\ \text{row } 2 \\ \vdots \\ \text{row } n \end{bmatrix} + d \det \begin{bmatrix} w \\ \text{row } 2 \\ \vdots \\ \text{row } n \end{bmatrix}$$

**Note:** The above properties can be easily observed to be true if we consider the determinant as a **volume function** of an $n$-dimensional parallelepiped. Here, volume means **signed volume** and not just absolute value. We will see this later in this chapter.
