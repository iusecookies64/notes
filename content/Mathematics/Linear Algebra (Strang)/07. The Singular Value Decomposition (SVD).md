# Overview

## Core Idea of the Singular Value Decomposition

The Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) extends the concept of eigenvectors and diagonalization to **every** matrix $A$, whether square or rectangular of size $m \times n$. Because $A$ maps vectors from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m$, diagonalization requires **two sets of orthonormal vectors**:
- **Input (right singular) vectors:** $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n \in \mathbb{R}^n$.
- **Output (left singular) vectors:** $u_1, u_2, \dots, u_m \in \mathbb{R}^m$.

For a matrix $A$ of rank $r = \operatorname{rank}(A)$:
- The first $r$ input vectors $v_1, \dots, v_r$ form an orthonormal basis for the **row space** $C(A^T)$.
- The first $r$ output vectors $u_1, \dots, u_r$ form an orthonormal basis for the **column space** $C(A)$.

> [!abstract] Definition: Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)
> Any $m \times n$ matrix $A$ of rank $r$ can be decomposed using orthogonal matrices $U$ ($m \times m$) and $V$ ($n \times n$) and an $m \times n$ diagonal matrix $\Sigma$ containing $r$ positive **singular values** $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0$:
> $$
> A = U\Sigma V^T = \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^T + \sigma_2 u_2 v_2^T + \dots + \sigma_r u_r v_r^T
> $$
> Equivalently, $A$ is recovered as a sum of $r$ matrices of rank one, each formed by the outer product $u_i v_i^T$ scaled by $\sigma_i$.

## Properties of Singular Vectors and Connection to Symmetric Matrices

1. **Right Singular Vectors ($v_i$):** The vectors $v_i$ are orthonormal eigenvectors of the symmetric matrix $A^T A$. They provide orthonormal bases for the row space $C(A^T)$ and the nullspace $N(A)$.
2. **Left Singular Vectors ($u_i$):** The vectors $u_i$ are orthonormal eigenvectors of the symmetric matrix $AA^T$. They provide orthonormal bases for the column space $C(A)$ and the left nullspace $N(A^T)$.
3. **Diagonalization by Two Orthogonal Bases:** Each right singular vector is mapped by $A$ into a scalar multiple of the corresponding left singular vector:
   $$
   Av_i = \sigma_i u_i \quad \text{for } i \le r
   $$
   In matrix form, $A$ is diagonalized by the two orthogonal bases $V$ and $U$:
   $$
   AV = U\Sigma
   $$
4. **Column-Times-Row Multiplication:** The nonzero columns of $U\Sigma$ are $\sigma_1 u_1, \dots, \sigma_r u_r$. Multiplying $(U\Sigma)V^T$ multiplies columns of $U\Sigma$ by rows of $V^T$, paralleling the spectral decomposition $S = Q\Lambda Q^T$ for a symmetric matrix $S$.
5. **Reduction to Spectral Theorem for Symmetric Positive Definite Matrices:** When $A$ is a symmetric positive definite matrix $S$, each $u_i = v_i$ coincides with the orthonormal eigenvector $q_i$, and the singular values $\sigma_i$ equal the eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ of $S$, so $A = U\Sigma V^T$ reduces directly to $S = Q\Lambda Q^T$. When $A$ is unsymmetric or rectangular, $A = U\Sigma V^T$ replaces $Q\Lambda Q^T$.
6. **Geometric Transformation:** Acting on the unit circle (or unit sphere) of vectors $x$, the sequence of transformations
   $$
   x \longrightarrow V^T x \longrightarrow \Sigma V^T x \longrightarrow U\Sigma V^T x = Ax
   $$
   stretches the circle of unit vectors $x$ into an ellipse of vectors $Ax$.

## Applications in Data Science and Image Processing

- **Sample Covariance and Principal Component Analysis (PCA):** When $A$ is a matrix of centered data, $\frac{1}{n-1} AA^T$ is the **sample covariance matrix**. Its nonzero eigenvalues are $\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_r^2$ and its eigenvectors are the left singular vectors $u_i$ in the SVD of $A$. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is built directly on the singular vectors of the data matrix $A$.
- **Low-Rank Image Approximation:** Representing a photograph as a matrix of pixel intensities $A$, the SVD separates the image into rank-one components $\sigma_i u_i v_i^T$ ordered by magnitude $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r$. Including successive rank-one pieces progressively reconstructs and sharpens the image.

# 7.1 Singular Values and Singular Vectors

## Fundamental Equations of the SVD

### Core Properties of Singular Values and Vectors

For a symmetric matrix $S$, the eigenvectors are orthogonal and satisfy $Sx = \lambda x$. To generalize orthogonal diagonalization to an arbitrary $m \times n$ matrix $A$ without sacrificing orthogonality, a single eigenvector basis is replaced by two orthonormal bases: $v_1, \dots, v_n \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $u_1, \dots, u_m \in \mathbb{R}^m$ satisfying $Av = \sigma u$.

> [!summary] Fundamental Properties of the SVD
> 1. **Factorization:** The Singular Value Decomposition of any matrix $A$ is $A = U\Sigma V^T$, or equivalently $AV = U\Sigma$.
> 2. **Orthonormality:** The singular vectors $v_i, u_i$ satisfying $Av_i = \sigma_i u_i$ are orthonormal, yielding orthogonal matrices:
>    $$
>    V^T V = I \quad \text{and} \quad U^T U = I
>    $$
> 3. **Positive Ordered Singular Values:** The diagonal matrix $\Sigma$ contains the positive singular values ordered by magnitude:
>    $$
>    \sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0
>    $$
> 4. **Eigenvalue Connection:** The squares $\sigma_i^2$ of the singular values are the nonzero eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ of both $A^T A$ and $AA^T$.

### Matrix and Rank-One Forms

Given $r$ orthogonal input vectors that map to $r$ orthogonal output vectors under $A$, normalizing the inputs by their lengths produces orthonormal right singular vectors $v_i$, and normalizing the outputs produces orthonormal left singular vectors $u_i$, with the ratio of lengths giving the singular values $\sigma_i$:

$$
Av_i = \sigma_i u_i
$$

Placing the input vectors $v_i$ into the columns of $V$ and the scaled output vectors $\sigma_i u_i$ into the columns of $U\Sigma$ combines the vector equations into a single matrix equation:

$$
AV = U\Sigma
$$

Because $V$ and $U$ are square orthogonal matrices ($V^{-1} = V^T$), multiplying $AV = U\Sigma$ on the right by $V^T$ isolates $A$:

$$
A = U\Sigma V^T = \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^T + \sigma_2 u_2 v_2^T + \dots + \sigma_r u_r v_r^T
$$

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Diagonalization and Dominance of Rank-One Pieces
> Every matrix $A$ is diagonalized by **two sets of singular vectors**, rather than one set of eigenvectors. In the rank-one expansion $A = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i u_i v_i^T$, the terms are ordered by importance: because $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r$, the first rank-one piece $\sigma_1 u_1 v_1^T$ captures the largest component of $A$.

## The Geometry of the SVD

### Rotation-Stretch-Rotation Factorization

The SVD factors any matrix $A = U\Sigma V^T$ into three sequential geometric operations:

$$
A = (\text{orthogonal}) \times (\text{diagonal}) \times (\text{orthogonal})
$$

In $\mathbb{R}^2$, orthogonal matrices $U$ and $V$ act as **rotations** (and possible reflections) of the plane, while the diagonal matrix $\Sigma$ **stretches** the plane along the coordinate axes:
1. **First Rotation ($V^T$):** Rotates any vector $x$ to $V^T x$. In particular, the orthonormal vectors $v_1, v_2$ on the unit circle are rotated onto the standard coordinate axes.
2. **Axis Scaling ($\Sigma$):** Stretches $V^T x$ to $\Sigma V^T x$ by multiplying the first coordinate by $\sigma_1$ and the second coordinate by $\sigma_2$, deforming the unit circle into an axis-aligned ellipse with semi-axes $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$.
3. **Second Rotation ($U$):** Rotates the axis-aligned ellipse $\Sigma V^T x$ to $U\Sigma V^T x = Ax$, aligning the semi-axes of the ellipse along $\sigma_1 u_1$ and $\sigma_2 u_2$. Vectors $v$ on the unit circle map to $Av = \sigma u$ on the ellipse.

![[07. The Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)-1792564112429.webp]]

For a $2 \times 2$ invertible matrix $A$ with positive determinants chosen for $U$ and $V$ to avoid reflections ($\sigma_1 > 0, \sigma_2 > 0$), the four entries $a, b, c, d$ of $A$ are parameterized by **two rotation angles** $\theta, \phi$ and **two singular values** $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \cos\phi & \sin\phi \\ -\sin\phi & \cos\phi \end{bmatrix}
$$

### Parameter Counting and Special Cases

1. **Symmetric $2 \times 2$ Matrix ($b = c$):** A symmetric $2 \times 2$ matrix has only $3$ independent parameters instead of $4$. In the SVD parameterization, the four parameters $\theta, \phi, \sigma_1, \sigma_2$ reduce to $3$ because symmetry forces the two rotation angles to be equal ($\theta = \phi$, up to sign reflections when eigenvalues are negative), so $U$ and $V$ share the same rotation angle.
2. **$3 \times 3$ Matrix ($9$ Parameters):** For a $3 \times 3$ matrix $A$, the diagonal matrix $\Sigma$ contributes $3$ singular values, while the $3 \times 3$ orthogonal matrices $U$ and $V$ each depend on $3$ independent rotation angles (corresponding in three-dimensional aviation mechanics to **pitch**, **roll**, and **yaw**, or Euler angles), accounting for all $3 + 3 + 3 = 9$ parameters.
3. **Symmetric Positive Definite Matrix ($S = Q\Lambda Q^T$):** If $S$ is symmetric positive definite, all eigenvalues in $\Lambda$ are strictly positive. Its SVD is identically $U\Sigma V^T = Q\Lambda Q^T$, where $U = V = Q$ is the orthogonal eigenvector matrix and $\Sigma = \Lambda$ is the diagonal eigenvalue matrix.
4. **Symmetric Matrix with a Negative Eigenvalue ($Sx = -\alpha x, \alpha > 0$):** Because singular values must be strictly positive ($\sigma > 0$), a negative eigenvalue $-\alpha$ corresponds to the positive singular value $\sigma = +\alpha$. To maintain $Sv = \sigma u$, one of the corresponding singular vectors (either $u$ or $v$) must reverse sign to $-x$ while the other remains $x$. The two sign changes (in $\sigma = +\alpha$ and in $u = -v$) cancel out so that $Sx = -\alpha x$ is written as $Sv = \sigma u$.

## Full Size vs. Reduced Form of the SVD

### The Full Size SVD

To form square orthogonal matrices $V$ ($n \times n$) and $U$ ($m \times m$), the row-space basis $v_1, \dots, v_r$ and column-space basis $u_1, \dots, u_r$ are completed by appending orthonormal basis vectors for the two nullspaces:
- $v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n$ form an orthonormal basis for the **nullspace** $N(A)$.
- $u_{r+1}, \dots, u_m$ form an orthonormal basis for the **left nullspace** $N(A^T)$.

> [!abstract] Definition: Full Size SVD
> For an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ of rank $r$, the **full size SVD** $AV = U\Sigma$ (or $A = U\Sigma V^T$) has dimensions $(m \times n)(n \times n) = (m \times m)(m \times n)$:
> $$
> A \begin{bmatrix} v_1 & \cdots & v_r & v_{r+1} & \cdots & v_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 & \cdots & u_r & u_{r+1} & \cdots & u_m \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1 & & & 0 \\ & \ddots & & \vdots \\ & & \sigma_r & 0 \\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> Here $V^T = V^{-1}$ ($n \times n$), $U^T = U^{-1}$ ($m \times m$), and $\Sigma$ is an $m \times n$ matrix that is all zero except for $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0$ on its main diagonal.

Multiplying $AV = U\Sigma$ on the right by $V^T = V^{-1}$ gives $A = U\Sigma V^T$, where the square matrix $V$ combines orthonormal bases for $\text{row space} + N(A)$ and the square matrix $U$ combines orthonormal bases for $\text{column space} + N(A^T)$.

### The Reduced Form of the SVD

When $\operatorname{rank}(A) = r$ is small relative to $m$ and $n$, the nullspaces are large and the full matrix $\Sigma$ contains many rows and columns of zeros that contribute nothing to the product $U\Sigma V^T$. Discarding the nullspace vectors and zero blocks leaves the **reduced SVD**.

> [!abstract] Definition: Reduced SVD
> Retaining only the first $r$ columns $V_r = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 & \cdots & v_r \end{bmatrix}$ ($n \times r$, row space basis), the first $r$ columns $U_r = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 & \cdots & u_r \end{bmatrix}$ ($m \times r$, column space basis), and the square diagonal matrix $\Sigma_r = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r)$ ($r \times r$) yields the **reduced SVD** of dimensions $(m \times n)(n \times r) = (m \times r)(r \times r)$:
> $$
> A \begin{bmatrix} v_1 & \cdots & v_r \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 & \cdots & u_r \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1 & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & \sigma_r \end{bmatrix} \iff AV_r = U_r \Sigma_r
> $$
> In outer-product form, the reduced SVD reconstructs $A$ exactly:
> $$
> A = U_r \Sigma_r V_r^T = u_1 \sigma_1 v_1^T + \dots + u_r \sigma_r v_r^T
> $$

> [!warning] Caution: One-Sided Orthogonality of $V_r$ and $U_r$
> Because the columns of $V_r$ and $U_r$ are orthonormal unit vectors, their inner-product matrices are the $r \times r$ identity:
> $$
> V_r^T V_r = I_r \quad \text{and} \quad U_r^T U_r = I_r
> $$
> However, when $r < n$ or $r < m$, the rectangular matrices $V_r$ and $U_r$ do **not** have two-sided inverses:
> $$
> V_r V_r^T \neq I_n \quad \text{and} \quad U_r U_r^T \neq I_m
> $$
> Nevertheless, $A = U_r \Sigma_r V_r^T$ holds exactly because all discarded multiplications from the full form $U\Sigma V^T$ are multiplied by zeros in $\Sigma$. When $r = m = n$, the reduced SVD is identical to the full SVD.

## Proof of the SVD

### Connection to $A^T A$ and $AA^T$

To identify the singular vectors in $A = U\Sigma V^T$, we form the symmetric positive semidefinite matrices $A^T A$ ($n \times n$) and $AA^T$ ($m \times m$) and use the orthogonality relations $U^T U = I$ and $V^T V = I$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^T A &= (V \Sigma^T U^T)(U \Sigma V^T) = V \Sigma^T \Sigma V^T \\
AA^T &= (U \Sigma V^T)(V \Sigma^T U^T) = U \Sigma \Sigma^T U^T
\end{aligned}
$$

Both right-hand sides take the symmetric spectral decomposition form $Q\Lambda Q^T$:
- $V$ contains orthonormal eigenvectors of $A^T A$, with diagonal eigenvalue matrix $\Sigma^T \Sigma$ ($n \times n$).
- $U$ contains orthonormal eigenvectors of $AA^T$, with diagonal eigenvalue matrix $\Sigma \Sigma^T$ ($m \times m$).
- $\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_r^2$ are the nonzero eigenvalues of both $A^T A$ and $AA^T$.

### Coupling of Sign and Repeated Eigenvalue Choices

> [!warning] Caution: $U$ and $V$ Cannot Be Chosen Independently
> Diagonalizing $A^T A$ and $AA^T$ separately is **not** sufficient to produce $A = U\Sigma V^T$, because the SVD requires the direct coupling condition:
> $$
> Av_k = \sigma_k u_k \quad \text{for } k = 1, \dots, r
> $$
> 1. **Sign Ambiguity:** If $u_k$ is a unit eigenvector of $S = AA^T$ ($Su_k = \lambda_k u_k$), then $-u_k$ is also a unit eigenvector ($S(-u_k) = \lambda_k(-u_k)$). Once the signs of $v_k$ are chosen, the relation $Av_k = \sigma_k u_k$ uniquely fixes the signs of $u_k$.
> 2. **Repeated Eigenvalues:** When $\lambda_k = \sigma_k^2$ is a repeated eigenvalue of multiplicity $d > 1$, there is an entire $d$-dimensional subspace of eigenvectors. Choosing any $d$ orthonormal eigenvectors $v$ in that eigenspace of $A^T A$ uniquely determines the corresponding $d$ orthonormal vectors $u = Av/\sigma$ in the eigenspace of $AA^T$.

### Constructive Proof of the SVD

> [!theorem] Theorem: Existence and Construction of the SVD
> Every $m \times n$ matrix $A$ of rank $r$ admits a Singular Value Decomposition $A = U\Sigma V^T$ with orthogonal matrices $U \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ and $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$.

**Proof:**

1. **Choose Right Singular Vectors in the Row Space:** Since $A^T A$ is symmetric positive semidefinite of rank $r$, choose orthonormal eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_r$ of $A^T A$ corresponding to its positive eigenvalues $\lambda_k = \sigma_k^2 > 0$ ($\sigma_k = \sqrt{\lambda_k} > 0$):
   $$
   A^T A v_k = \sigma_k^2 v_k, \quad k = 1, \dots, r
   $$
2. **Define Left Singular Vectors in the Column Space:** Define $u_1, \dots, u_r$ directly from $v_1, \dots, v_r$ by requiring $Av_k = \sigma_k u_k$:
   $$
   u_k = \frac{Av_k}{\sigma_k} \quad \text{for } k = 1, \dots, r
   $$
3. **Verify that $u_k$ are Eigenvectors of $AA^T$:** Applying $AA^T$ to $u_k$ and using the associative law of matrix multiplication $(AA^T)A = A(A^T A)$:
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   AA^T u_k &= AA^T \left( \frac{Av_k}{\sigma_k} \right) \\
   &= A \left( \frac{A^T A v_k}{\sigma_k} \right) \\
   &= A \left( \frac{\sigma_k^2 v_k}{\sigma_k} \right) = \sigma_k^2 \left( \frac{Av_k}{\sigma_k} \right) = \sigma_k^2 u_k
   \end{aligned}
   $$
   Thus each $u_k$ is an eigenvector of $AA^T$ with eigenvalue $\sigma_k^2$.
4. **Verify that $u_1, \dots, u_r$ are Orthonormal:** Computing the inner product $u_j^T u_k$ using the orthonormality of $v_1, \dots, v_r$:
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   u_j^T u_k &= \left( \frac{Av_j}{\sigma_j} \right)^T \left( \frac{Av_k}{\sigma_k} \right) \\
   &= \frac{v_j^T (A^T A v_k)}{\sigma_j \sigma_k} \\
   &= \frac{\sigma_k^2}{\sigma_j \sigma_k} v_j^T v_k = \frac{\sigma_k}{\sigma_j} v_j^T v_k = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } j = k \\ 0 & \text{if } j \neq k \end{cases}
   \end{aligned}
   $$
   Hence $u_1, \dots, u_r$ are orthonormal unit vectors.
5. **Complete the Bases Using Nullspaces:** Choose any orthonormal basis $v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n$ for the nullspace $N(A)$ (where $Av_k = 0$) and any orthonormal basis $u_{r+1}, \dots, u_m$ for the left nullspace $N(A^T)$. By the **Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra**, the fundamental subspaces are orthogonal complements:
   $$
   N(A) \perp C(A^T) \quad \text{and} \quad N(A^T) \perp C(A)
   $$
   Therefore, $v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n$ are automatically orthogonal to $v_1, \dots, v_r \in C(A^T)$, and $u_{r+1}, \dots, u_m$ are automatically orthogonal to $u_1, \dots, u_r \in C(A)$. This completes the square orthogonal matrices $V$ and $U$ such that $AV = U\Sigma$ and $A = U\Sigma V^T$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Numerical SVD Computation
> Although the proof constructs $V$ and $U$ via $A^T A$ and $AA^T$, good numerical algorithms **do not** start by forming $A^T A$ and $AA^T$. Instead, they first apply orthogonal transformations directly to $A$ to introduce zeros and reduce $A$ to a matrix with only two nonzero diagonals (bidiagonal form), leaving all singular values $\sigma_i$ unchanged. For a complete treatment of why $A^T A$ causes catastrophic loss of precision and how the two-stage algorithm operates, see [Computing Eigenvalues and Singular Values](#Computing%20Eigenvalues%20and%20Singular%20Values).

>[!note] Theorem: $AB$ and $BA$ Have the Same Nonzero Eigenvalues
> In the full SVD, the eigenvalues $\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_r^2$ of the $n \times n$ matrix $A^T A$ (in $\Sigma^T \Sigma$) are identical to the nonzero eigenvalues of the $m \times m$ matrix $AA^T$ (in $\Sigma \Sigma^T$). This is a special case of a general theorem on matrix products, we have already seen a proof of it in previous chapter [The Spectrum of Matrix Products: AB and BA](06.%20Eigenvalues%20and%20Eigenvectors.md#The%20Spectrum%20of%20Matrix%20Products%20$AB$%20and%20$BA$)
> 
> **Similarity vs. Rectangular Products and Zero Eigenvalues**
> - **Square Invertible Case:** If $A$ and $B$ are square and $B$ is invertible, then $B^{-1}(BA)B = AB$, meaning $BA$ and $AB$ are **similar matrices** and share all eigenvalues. The algebraic proof above requires no invertibility and applies to rectangular matrices $A$ ($m \times n$) and $B$ ($n \times m$).
> - **Application to SVD ($B = A^T$):** Setting $B = A^T$ proves immediately that $AA^T$ ($m \times m$) and $A^T A$ ($n \times n$) share the exact same $r$ nonzero eigenvalues $\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_r^2$.
> - **Dimension Mismatch ($m > n$):** If $m > n$, the larger matrix $AB$ ($m \times m$) has $m - n$ extra zero eigenvalues compared to $BA$ ($n \times n$).

## Picture Proof of the $2 \times 2$ SVD

For an invertible $2 \times 2$ matrix $A$, the existence of perpendicular unit inputs $v \perp w$ whose outputs $Av \perp Aw$ are also perpendicular can be established geometrically by continuity:
1. Start with the standard perpendicular unit vectors $V = (1, 0)$ and $W = (0, 1)$.
2. Suppose the angle $\theta$ from $AV$ to $AW$ is acute ($\theta < 90^\circ$).
3. Rotate the input pair by $90^\circ$ so that $V$ moves to $W$ and $W$ moves to $-V$. The outputs become $AW$ and $A(-V) = -AV$, and the angle between them is supplementary: $180^\circ - \theta > 90^\circ$ (obtuse).
4. As the perpendicular unit pair $(v, w)$ rotates continuously from $(V, W)$ to $(W, -V)$, the angle between $Av$ and $Aw$ varies continuously from $\theta < 90^\circ$ to $180^\circ - \theta > 90^\circ$. By the Intermediate Value Theorem, there exists an intermediate position where the angle from $Av$ to $Aw$ is **exactly $90^\circ$**. At this position, orthogonal inputs $v \perp w$ produce orthogonal outputs $Av \perp Aw$, proving the $2 \times 2$ SVD.

## Variational Characterization: The First Singular Vector $v_1$

### Maximizing the Amplification Ratio (Rayleigh Quotient)

Rather than finding all singular vectors simultaneously as eigenvectors of $A^T A$, the singular vectors can be characterized **one at a time** via an optimization problem. Geometrically, since $A$ maps the unit circle to an ellipse with semi-major axis length $\|Av_1\| = \sigma_1$, the first right singular vector $v_1$ is the direction of maximum stretching.

> [!theorem] Theorem: Variational Characterization of $\sigma_1$ and $v_1$
> For any matrix $A$, the maximum of the amplification ratio $\frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|}$ over all nonzero vectors $x$ is the largest singular value $\sigma_1$, achieved at the first right singular vector $x = v_1$:
> $$
> \max_{x \neq 0} \frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|} = \sigma_1 \quad \text{at } x = v_1
> $$

**Proof:**
To maximize $\frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|}$ without assuming prior knowledge of $U, \Sigma, V$, square the ratio and express it in terms of the symmetric matrix $S = A^T A$:

$$
\frac{\|Ax\|^2}{\|x\|^2} = \frac{x^T A^T A x}{x^T x} = \frac{x^T S x}{x^T x}
$$

This ratio is the **Rayleigh quotient** of the symmetric matrix $S = A^T A$. Let $x_1, \dots, x_n$ be an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $S$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n \ge 0$ ($Sx_i = \lambda_i x_i$). Expanding any nonzero vector $x = c_1 x_1 + \dots + c_n x_n$ yields:

$$
\frac{x^T S x}{x^T x} = \frac{c_1^2 \lambda_1 + \dots + c_n^2 \lambda_n}{c_1^2 + \dots + c_n^2} \le \frac{(c_1^2 + \dots + c_n^2)\lambda_1}{c_1^2 + \dots + c_n^2} = \lambda_1
$$

Thus the maximum of $\frac{\|Ax\|^2}{\|x\|^2}$ is the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_1(S) = \sigma_1^2(A)$, and it is attained when $c_2 = \dots = c_n = 0$, i.e., when $x = x_1 = v_1$ is the unit eigenvector of $S = A^T A$ corresponding to $\lambda_1$. Taking square roots gives $\max_{x \neq 0} \frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|} = \sigma_1$ at $x = v_1$. $\blacksquare$

### Higher Singular Vectors via Constrained Maximization

Once $v_1$ and $\sigma_1$ are found, the subsequent singular vectors and singular values are obtained by restricting the maximization to vectors orthogonal to the previously found singular vectors:

> [!theorem] Theorem: Courant-Fischer / Stepwise Maximization for Singular Vectors
> - **Second Singular Vector ($v_2$):** Maximize $\frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|}$ subject to the orthogonality constraint $v_1^T x = 0$. The maximum value is $\sigma_2$, achieved at $x = v_2$.
> - **General $(k+1)$-th Singular Vector ($v_{k+1}$):** Every singular vector $v_{k+1}$ maximizes the ratio $\frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|}$ over all nonzero vectors $x$ perpendicular to the first $k$ singular vectors ($v_1^T x = 0, \dots, v_k^T x = 0$), with maximum value $\sigma_{k+1}$:
>   $$
>   \sigma_{k+1} = \max_{\substack{x \neq 0 \\ v_1^T x = \dots = v_k^T x = 0}} \frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|} \quad \text{at } x = v_{k+1}
>   $$

Constrained optimization problems with orthogonality conditions like $v_1^T x = 0$ can be solved either via **Lagrange multipliers** or directly by expanding $x = c_2 x_2 + \dots + c_n x_n$ in the remaining eigenvectors of $S = A^T A$. Geometrically, this procedure finds the principal axes of an $n$-dimensional ellipsoid one axis at a time.

### Eigenvalue Bound: $|\lambda| \le \sigma_1$

> [!theorem] Theorem: Maximum Singular Value Bounds All Eigenvalues
> Every eigenvalue $\lambda$ of a square matrix $A$ satisfies:
> $$
> |\lambda| \le \sigma_1
> $$

**Proof:**
Multiplying a vector by the orthogonal matrices $U$ and $V^T$ preserves Euclidean length ($\|Uy\| = \|y\|$ and $\|V^T x\| = \|x\|$). Moreover, multiplying a vector $z = V^T x$ by the diagonal matrix $\Sigma$ scales each component $z_i$ by $\sigma_i \le \sigma_1$, so $\|\Sigma z\| \le \sigma_1 \|z\|$. Combining these steps for any vector $x$:
$$
\|Ax\| = \|U\Sigma V^T x\| = \|\Sigma V^T x\| \le \sigma_1 \|V^T x\| = \sigma_1 \|x\|
$$
If $x \neq 0$ is an eigenvector of $A$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$ ($Ax = \lambda x$), then $\|Ax\| = |\lambda| \|x\|$. Substituting this into the inequality yields:
$$
|\lambda| \|x\| \le \sigma_1 \|x\| \implies |\lambda| \le \sigma_1 \quad \blacksquare
$$
**Alternative Proof:**
We already proved that $\sigma_{1}$ is the maximum value of the ratio $\frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|}$, let $x$ be an eigenvector of $A$, then we have,
$$
\begin{align}
(Ax)^{H}(Ax) & = (\lambda x)^{H}(\lambda x) \\
\|Ax\|^{2} & = \bar{\lambda}\lambda x^{H}x \\
\implies |\lambda|^{2} & = \frac{\|Ax\|^{2}}{\|x\|^{2}} \\
\implies |\lambda| & = \frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|} \leq \sigma_{1}
\end{align}
$$

### Rank-One Matrix SVD and the Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

Consider a rank-one matrix $A = xy^T$ formed by nonzero vectors $x \in \mathbb{R}^m$ and $y \in \mathbb{R}^n$.
1. **Explicit SVD of $A = xy^T$:** Normalizing $x$ and $y$ produces the unit singular vectors $u_1 = \frac{x}{\|x\|}$ and $v_1 = \frac{y}{\|y\|}$. Factoring out their norms gives the Reduced SVD directly:
   $$
   A = xy^T = \frac{x}{\|x\|} \left(\|x\| \|y\|\right) \frac{y^T}{\|y\|} = u_1 \sigma_1 v_1^T
   $$
   Thus the sole nonzero singular value in $\Sigma$ is $\sigma_1 = \|x\| \|y\|$.
2. **Eigenvalue and Equivalence to Cauchy-Schwarz:** When $A = xy^T$ is square ($m = n$), its only potentially nonzero eigenvalue is $\lambda_1 = y^T x$, with eigenvector $x$:
   $$
   Ax = (xy^T)x = x(y^T x) = \lambda_1 x
   $$
   Applying the eigenvalue bound $|\lambda_1| \le \sigma_1$ to $A = xy^T$ yields the **Cauchy-Schwarz inequality**:
   $$
   |y^T x| \le \|x\| \|y\|
   $$

## Computing Eigenvalues and Singular Values

### The Core Problem: Why Textbook Formulas Fail in Practice

In theory, finding the singular values of an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ looks straightforward:
1. Multiply $A^T$ by $A$ to form the symmetric $n \times n$ matrix $S = A^T A$.
2. Find the eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ of $S$ by solving the characteristic polynomial $\det(S - \lambda I) = 0$.
3. Take the square roots $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$.

When computing by hand for a $2 \times 2$ or $3 \times 3$ matrix, this recipe works well. However, for real-world matrices on a computer, **both Step 1 and Step 2 fail completely**:

1. **Obstacle 1 — No Exact Formula for Eigenvalues (The Algebra Obstacle):**
   For an $n \times n$ matrix, $\det(S - \lambda I) = 0$ is a polynomial equation of degree $n$. A fundamental result in mathematics—the **Abel-Ruffini Theorem**—proves that for degree $n \ge 5$, there is *no general algebraic formula* (using only $+, -, \times, \div$, and roots $\sqrt[k]{\cdot}$) to find the roots of a polynomial.
   - **Implication:** No finite sequence of algebraic steps can reduce a matrix of size $n \ge 5$ directly into a diagonal matrix of eigenvalues or singular values. Instead, any computer algorithm must use an **iterative method**—a loop that repeatedly refines an approximation until the off-diagonal entries shrink to zero.

2. **Obstacle 2 — Multiplying $A^T A$ Destroys Small Numbers (The Numerical Obstacle):**
   Computers store numbers in **floating-point arithmetic** with a fixed number of significant digits (roughly $16$ decimal digits in standard double precision). The smallest relative gap a computer can distinguish from $1$ is called **machine epsilon**, denoted $\epsilon_{\text{mach}} \approx 10^{-16}$. That means the computer rounds $1 + 10^{-16}$ directly to $1$, losing the $10^{-16}$ completely.

> [!abstract] Definition: Spectral Condition Number
> For a matrix $A$ of rank $r$, the **spectral condition number** $\kappa_2(A)$ is the ratio of its largest stretching factor to its smallest nonzero stretching factor:
> $$
> \kappa_2(A) = \frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_r}
> $$
> - Geometrically, $\kappa_2(A)$ measures how "elongated" or flattened the output ellipse of $A$ is. If $\kappa_2(A) = 1$, $A$ stretches all directions equally (like a sphere). If $\kappa_2(A)$ is very large, $A$ stretches some directions strongly while nearly squashing others to zero.
> - Numerically, $\kappa_2(A)$ measures how sensitive the matrix is to tiny rounding errors: the larger $\kappa_2(A)$ is, the more digits of accuracy can be lost in computations.

> [!warning] Caution: Catastrophic Loss of Precision When Forming $A^T A$
> Because the singular values of $A^T A$ are the *squares* $\sigma_i^2$ of the singular values of $A$, explicitly multiplying $A^T A$ **squares the condition number**:
> $$
> \kappa_2(A^T A) = \frac{\sigma_1^2}{\sigma_r^2} = \left(\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_r}\right)^2 = \kappa_2(A)^2
> $$
> When $A^T A$ is multiplied out, small singular values $\sigma_i$ get squared into $\sigma_i^2$ and added directly to large numbers of size $\sigma_1^2$. Whenever $\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_1} < \sqrt{\epsilon_{\text{mach}}} \approx 10^{-8}$, the squared ratio $\frac{\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_1^2}$ drops below $\epsilon_{\text{mach}} \approx 10^{-16}$, so $\sigma_i^2$ is completely wiped out by computer roundoff error when added to $\sigma_1^2$.
>
> **Counterexample (Läuchli Matrix):** Consider the $3 \times 2$ matrix with $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$:
> $$
> A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ \epsilon & 0 \\ 0 & \epsilon \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> Its exact singular values are $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{2 + \epsilon^2} \approx \sqrt{2}$ and $\sigma_2 = \epsilon = 10^{-8}$. Both are well above $\epsilon_{\text{mach}} \approx 10^{-16}$ and can be stored accurately on a computer. However, if we explicitly form $A^T A$:
> $$
> A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 + \epsilon^2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 + \epsilon^2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 + 10^{-16} & 1 \\ 1 & 1 + 10^{-16} \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> In double-precision floating-point arithmetic ($\operatorname{fl}$), $1 + 10^{-16}$ rounds to $1$:
> $$
> \operatorname{fl}(A^T A) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> This rounded matrix is singular with eigenvalues $2$ and $0$. Taking square roots gives $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{2}$ and $\sigma_2 = 0$, completely losing the true nonzero singular value $\sigma_2 = 10^{-8}$.

### The Solution Strategy: Orthogonal Reduction Without Forming $A^T A$

How can we find the singular values of $A$ **without** ever multiplying $A^T A$, and without running a slow iterative loop on a dense matrix full of nonzeros?

#### Spectral Invariance Under Orthogonal Transformations

Recall that orthogonal matrices ($Q^T Q = I$) act as rigid rotations or reflections: they preserve vector lengths and angles.
- **For Symmetric Eigenvalues ($S = Q\Lambda Q^T$):** If we multiply a symmetric matrix $S$ on the left by $Q_1^T = Q_1^{-1}$ and on the right by the **same** $Q_1$, the transformed matrix $S_{\text{new}} = Q_1^T S Q_1$ is *similar* to $S$ and has the **exact same eigenvalues** $\Lambda$.
- **For Singular Values ($A = U\Sigma V^T$):** If we multiply $A$ on the left by an orthogonal matrix $Q_L^T$ ($m \times m$) and on the right by a **possibly different** orthogonal matrix $Q_R$ ($n \times n$), we get:
  $$
  B = Q_L^T A Q_R = (Q_L^T U) \Sigma (Q_R^T V)^T
  $$
  Because the product of two orthogonal matrices is still orthogonal ($\tilde{U} = Q_L^T U$ and $\tilde{V} = V^T Q_R$ remain orthogonal), $B$ has the **exact same singular values $\Sigma$** as $A$. Crucially, multiplying $A$ by orthogonal matrices never squares the entries of $A$, completely avoiding the numerical precision loss of $A^T A$.

#### The Two-Stage Strategy

Because the Abel-Ruffini theorem forbids reducing $A$ all the way to a diagonal matrix $\Sigma$ in a finite number of steps, practical algorithms split the work into **two stages**:
1. **Stage 1 (Finite Direct Pre-Reduction):** Use a finite sequence of exact orthogonal transformations to introduce as many zeros as possible into the matrix, stripping it down to a skeletal form that has only **two or three nonzero diagonals**.
2. **Stage 2 (Iterative Solver):** Run a fast iterative algorithm on that sparse skeletal matrix until the remaining off-diagonal entries converge to zero, leaving the eigenvalues or singular values on the main diagonal.

### Tridiagonal vs. Bidiagonal Forms: Why SVD Has an Extra Advantage

How many zeros can Stage 1 actually create using orthogonal matrices without destroying zeros that were created in earlier steps? Here lies a key structural difference between the **symmetric eigenvalue problem** ($Sx = \lambda x$) and the **singular value problem** ($Av = \sigma u$).

> [!abstract] Definition: Tridiagonal and Upper Bidiagonal Matrices
> - **Symmetric Tridiagonal Matrix:** A square matrix whose only nonzero entries lie on the **main diagonal**, the **superdiagonal** (immediately above the main diagonal), and the **subdiagonal** (immediately below the main diagonal)—a total of **three** nonzero diagonals.
> - **Upper Bidiagonal Matrix:** A matrix whose only nonzero entries lie on the **main diagonal** and the **superdiagonal**—a total of **two** nonzero diagonals, with all entries below the main diagonal and to the right of the superdiagonal equal to zero.

1. **Symmetric Eigenvalue Problem ($S \mapsto Q^{-1} S Q = Q^T S Q$):**
   - To preserve eigenvalues, we must apply a **similarity transformation** using the *same* orthogonal matrix $Q$ on both sides: $Q^T S$ (which mixes rows of $S$) and $(Q^T S) Q$ (which mixes columns).
   - **Why we cannot eliminate the first subdiagonal:** Suppose we choose $Q^T$ so that multiplying $Q^T S$ zeros out the entire first column below the $(1,1)$ entry (including the $(2,1)$ entry). Because $Q$ must be the exact transpose of $Q^T$, multiplying on the right by $Q$ mixes the first column with the remaining columns and **destroys the zero just created at $(1,2)$ and $(2,1)$**.
   - **Safe compromise:** If we instead choose $Q^T$ to leave Row $1$ alone and only zero out entries in Column $1$ *below the $(2,1)$ entry* (rows $3, \dots, n$), then $Q$ only mixes Columns $2, \dots, n$. Column $1$ is never touched by the right-multiplication, and by symmetry the newly created zeros in Row $1$ (columns $3, \dots, n$) are also preserved!
   - Repeating this column by column reduces $S$ in finitely many steps to a symmetric **tridiagonal** matrix (three nonzero diagonals):
     $$
     Q^{-1} S Q = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & b_1 & 0 & 0 \\ b_1 & a_2 & b_2 & 0 \\ 0 & b_2 & \ddots & \ddots \\ 0 & 0 & \ddots & a_n \end{bmatrix}
     $$

2. **Singular Value Problem ($A \mapsto Q_1^{-1} A Q_2 = Q_1^T A Q_2$):**
   - To preserve singular values, the left orthogonal matrix $Q_1$ and the right orthogonal matrix $Q_2$ can be **completely different and independent**.
   - Because $Q_2$ is not forced to equal $Q_1$, we have extra freedom:
     - First, we choose $Q_1^T$ on the left to zero out the **entire first column below $(1,1)$** (rows $2, \dots, n$).
     - Next, we choose an *independent* $Q_2$ on the right that leaves Column $1$ untouched and only mixes Columns $2, \dots, n$ to zero out Row $1$ **to the right of $(1,2)$** (columns $3, \dots, n$). Because Column $1$ is untouched, the zeros below $(1,1)$ are completely preserved!
   - Repeating this alternating process reduces $A$ in finitely many steps to an upper **bidiagonal** matrix (only two nonzero diagonals):
     $$
     Q_1^{-1} A Q_2 = \begin{bmatrix} c_1 & d_1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & c_2 & d_2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \ddots & \ddots \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & c_n \end{bmatrix}
     $$

3. **Algebraic Link Between Bidiagonal and Tridiagonal Forms:**
   If $A^* = Q_1^{-1} A Q_2$ is the upper bidiagonal reduction of $A$, what happens if we form $(A^*)^T A^*$? Multiplying the transpose of a bidiagonal matrix by itself automatically produces a symmetric **tridiagonal** matrix:
   $$
   (A^*)^T A^* = (Q_1^{-1} A Q_2)^T (Q_1^{-1} A Q_2) = Q_2^T A^T (Q_1 Q_1^{-1}) A Q_2 = Q_2^{-1} (A^T A) Q_2 = \text{tridiagonal}
   $$
   Thus, bidiagonalizing $A$ via $Q_1^T A Q_2$ implicitly achieves the exact same tridiagonal reduction $Q_2^{-1} (A^T A) Q_2$ of $S = A^T A$, **without ever multiplying $A^T A$**.

### The Orthogonal Tools: Householder Reflections and Givens Rotations

To zero out specific entries in a matrix using orthogonal transformations, numerical linear algebra relies on two elementary building blocks:

> [!abstract] Definition: Householder Reflections and Givens Rotations
> 1. **Householder Reflection ($H$):** An orthogonal matrix that reflects vectors across a mirror hyperplane (perpendicular to a unit vector $u$), given by $H = I - 2uu^T$.
>    - **Purpose:** Given any column (or row) vector, a single Householder reflection can be aimed to reflect that entire vector onto a single coordinate axis—**zeroing out a whole block of entries in a column or row all at once**.
> 2. **Givens Rotation ($G$):** An orthogonal matrix that rotates vectors by an angle $\theta$ within a single two-dimensional coordinate plane (affecting only two rows when multiplied on the left, or two columns when multiplied on the right).
>    - **Purpose:** While Householder reflections are bulk tools that zero out entire columns, a Givens rotation is a surgical tool used to **zero out a single targeted entry** without disturbing rows or columns outside that pair.

### The Two-Phase Golub-Kahan-Reinsch SVD Algorithm

Combining these ideas gives the standard industrial algorithm (used in LAPACK, MATLAB, and NumPy) for computing the SVD of an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ (assuming $m \ge n$):

1. **Phase 1: Golub-Kahan Bidiagonalization (Finite Pre-Reduction in $O(mn^2)$ Operations):**
   We alternate **left Householder reflections** $H_k^T$ (which mix rows to zero out entries *below the main diagonal* in column $k$) and **right Householder reflections** $G_k$ (which mix columns to zero out entries *to the right of the superdiagonal* in row $k$).
   - **Step-by-Step Zero Pattern (Illustrated for $4 \times 4$):**
     1. Apply left reflection $H_1^T$ (acting on rows $1, 2, 3, 4$) to zero out column $1$ below $(1,1)$:
        $$
        \begin{bmatrix}
        \times & \times & \times & \times \\
        \times & \times & \times & \times \\
        \times & \times & \times & \times \\
        \times & \times & \times & \times
        \end{bmatrix}
        \xrightarrow{H_1^T (\cdot)}
        \begin{bmatrix}
        \mathbf{\times} & \times & \times & \times \\
        \mathbf{0} & \times & \times & \times \\
        \mathbf{0} & \times & \times & \times \\
        \mathbf{0} & \times & \times & \times
        \end{bmatrix}
        $$
     2. Apply right reflection $G_1$ (acting **only on columns $2, 3, 4$** so column $1$ is untouched) to zero out row $1$ to the right of $(1,2)$:
        $$
        \xrightarrow{(\cdot) G_1}
        \begin{bmatrix}
        \times & \mathbf{\times} & \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{0} \\
        0 & \times & \times & \times \\
        0 & \times & \times & \times \\
        0 & \times & \times & \times
        \end{bmatrix}
        $$
     3. Apply left reflection $H_2^T$ (acting **only on rows $2, 3, 4$** so row $1$ is untouched) to zero out column $2$ below $(2,2)$:
        $$
        \xrightarrow{H_2^T (\cdot)}
        \begin{bmatrix}
        \times & \times & 0 & 0 \\
        0 & \mathbf{\times} & \times & \times \\
        0 & \mathbf{0} & \times & \times \\
        0 & \mathbf{0} & \times & \times
        \end{bmatrix}
        $$
     4. Apply right reflection $G_2$ (acting only on columns $3, 4$) to zero out $(2,4)$, followed by left reflection $H_3^T$ (acting only on rows $3, 4$) to zero out $(4,3)$:
        $$
        \xrightarrow{(\cdot) G_2}
        \begin{bmatrix}
        \times & \times & 0 & 0 \\
        0 & \times & \mathbf{\times} & \mathbf{0} \\
        0 & 0 & \times & \times \\
        0 & 0 & \times & \times
        \end{bmatrix}
        \xrightarrow{H_3^T (\cdot)}
        \begin{bmatrix}
        d_1 & f_1 & 0 & 0 \\
        0 & d_2 & f_2 & 0 \\
        0 & 0 & d_3 & f_3 \\
        0 & 0 & 0 & d_4
        \end{bmatrix} = B
        $$
   After $n$ left reflections and $n - 2$ right reflections (costing $O(mn^2)$ arithmetic operations), $A$ is reduced to an upper bidiagonal matrix $B$ with the exact same singular values:
   $$
   U_B^T A V_B = \begin{bmatrix} B \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad U_B = H_1 H_2 \cdots H_n, \quad V_B = G_1 G_2 \cdots G_{n-2}
   $$

2. **Phase 2: Implicit Iterative Solver / "Bulge Chasing" ($O(n^2)$ Operations):**
   Now that $A$ is compressed into a tiny upper bidiagonal matrix $B$ (with main diagonal $d_k$ and superdiagonal $f_k$), we need an iterative loop to drive the remaining superdiagonal entries $f_k$ to zero.
   - **Background — The Shifted $QR$ Idea:** For a symmetric tridiagonal matrix $T$, the classic iterative eigenvalue solver is the **shifted $QR$ algorithm**, which repeatedly picks a shift $\mu$ close to an eigenvalue, factors $T - \mu I = QR$, and reverses the factors to form $T_{\text{new}} = RQ + \mu I = Q^T T Q$. This rapidly shrinks the off-diagonal entries of $T$ to zero.
   - **The Implicit Trick on $B$:** We want the fast convergence of running a shifted $QR$ step on the tridiagonal matrix $T = B^T B$, *without ever computing $B^T B$*. Instead, we work directly on $B$ using $2 \times 2$ **Givens rotations**:
     1. **Estimate a Shift ($\mu$):** Look at the bottom-right $2 \times 2$ corner of $B^T B$ (computed on the fly from just the last two rows and columns of $B$) and pick its eigenvalue $\mu$ (the **Wilkinson shift**) as an estimate of $\sigma_n^2$.
     2. **Create a "Bulge":** Apply a single right Givens rotation $G_1$ to columns $1$ and $2$ of $B$ (matching the first rotation that $QR$ on $B^T B - \mu I$ would use). Because $G_1$ mixes columns $1$ and $2$, it knocks a single unwanted nonzero entry—called a **"bulge"**—into the $(2,1)$ subdiagonal position just below the diagonal.
     3. **Chase the Bulge Down the Diagonal:** We now use a chain of surgical $2 \times 2$ Givens rotations to push this unwanted nonzero entry off the bottom of the matrix:
        - Multiply on the left by a Givens rotation $U_1^T$ on rows $1$ and $2$ to zero out the bulge at $(2,1)$. However, mixing rows $1$ and $2$ creates a new bulge at $(1,3)$!
        - Multiply on the right by a Givens rotation $G_2$ on columns $2$ and $3$ to zero out the bulge at $(1,3)$; this pushes the bulge down to $(3,2)$.
        - Continue alternating left and right Givens rotations down the diagonal until the bulge is chased completely off the bottom-right corner, restoring $B$ to upper bidiagonal form:
          $$
          B_{\text{new}} = \tilde{U}^T B \tilde{V}
          $$
   - **Rapid Convergence:** A mathematical uniqueness theorem (the **Implicit $Q$ Theorem**) guarantees that chasing this single bulge down $B$ in $O(n)$ steps produces the exact same updated matrix as a full shifted $QR$ step on $B^T B$, without ever squaring a single number! Each sweep makes the superdiagonal entries $f_k$ shrink **cubically** (for example, an entry of size $10^{-2}$ shrinks to $\approx 10^{-6}$, then $\approx 10^{-18}$), reducing $B$ to the diagonal matrix $\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_n)$ in just a few sweeps ($O(n^2)$ total operations).

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Randomized Linear Algebra for Massive Matrices
> When a modern data matrix $A$ is massive (e.g., millions of rows and columns), even the $O(mn^2)$ cost of Phase 1 bidiagonalization is too slow. Modern **randomized linear algebra** overcomes this by multiplying $A$ by a thin random matrix to sample a low-dimensional subspace that captures the main action of $A$. Running the standard SVD on that much smaller projected matrix recovers highly accurate approximations of the largest singular values $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_k$ and singular vectors with overwhelming probability in a fraction of the time.
