# 7.1 Singular Values and Singular Vectors

The spectral theorem establishes that every real symmetric matrix $S \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is orthogonally diagonalizable using a single orthonormal basis of eigenvectors: $S = Q \Lambda Q^T$. However, the majority of matrices encountered in science, engineering, and data science are neither symmetric nor square. For a general rectangular matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, the classical eigenvalue problem $Ax = \lambda x$ is geometrically and algebraically undefined whenever $m \ne n$, as the linear transformation maps vectors between two entirely different vector spaces: from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m$. Furthermore, even when a matrix is square, unsymmetric matrices often fail to provide an orthogonal eigenbasis or may not be diagonalizable at all.

The **Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)** resolves this limitation universally. The SVD diagonalizes *any* matrix—whether square, rectangular, symmetric, or unsymmetric—by employing two distinct orthonormal bases: an orthonormal basis of input vectors $v_1, \dots, v_n \in \mathbb{R}^n$ (the **right singular vectors**) and an orthonormal basis of output vectors $u_1, \dots, u_m \in \mathbb{R}^m$ (the **left singular vectors**), linked by non-negative scaling factors $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0$ (the **singular values**).

## Fundamental Definitions and Matrix Factorization

> [!abstract] Definition: Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)
> Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ be a matrix of rank $r$. The **Singular Value Decomposition** of $A$ is the factorization:
> $$
> A = U \Sigma V^T \quad \text{or equivalently} \quad A V = U \Sigma
> $$
> where:
> - $V = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 & v_2 & \cdots & v_n \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is an orthogonal matrix whose columns $v_i$ are the **right singular vectors** ($V^T V = I_n$).
> - $U = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 & u_2 & \cdots & u_m \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ is an orthogonal matrix whose columns $u_i$ are the **left singular vectors** ($U^T U = I_m$).
> - $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is a rectangular diagonal matrix containing the **singular values** on its main diagonal, ordered non-increasingly:
>   $$
>   \sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \sigma_{r+1} = \cdots = \sigma_{\min(m, n)} = 0
>   $$

### Spectral Expansion: Sum of Rank-One Pieces
Multiplying the orthogonal matrices $U$ and $V^T$ column-by-row reveals the fundamental structure of $A$ as a superposition of rank-one outer products:
$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= U \Sigma V^T \\
  &= \begin{bmatrix} u_1 & \cdots & u_r & \cdots & u_m \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1 & & & 0 \\ & \ddots & & \vdots \\ & & \sigma_r & 0 \\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} v_1^T \\ \vdots \\ v_r^T \\ \vdots \\ v_n^T \end{bmatrix} \\
  &= \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1 u_1 & \cdots & \sigma_r u_r & 0 & \cdots & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} v_1^T \\ \vdots \\ v_r^T \\ \vdots \\ v_n^T \end{bmatrix} \\
  &= \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^T + \sigma_2 u_2 v_2^T + \dots + \sigma_r u_r v_r^T \\
  &= \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i u_i v_i^T
\end{aligned}
$$
Every matrix $A$ of rank $r$ is the exact sum of $r$ rank-one matrices $\sigma_i u_i v_i^T$. Each piece has matrix 2-norm $\|\sigma_i u_i v_i^T\|_2 = \sigma_i$. Because the singular values are sorted in descending order ($\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0$), this decomposition arranges the intrinsic energy and geometric information of $A$ in order of decreasing structural importance.

### SVD and the Four Fundamental Subspaces
The SVD diagonalizes the linear operator $A$ across the four fundamental subspaces defined by $A$, providing mutually orthogonal coordinate systems for all four spaces simultaneously:

1. **Row Space $C(A^T) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$:**
   Spanned by the first $r$ right singular vectors $\{v_1, \dots, v_r\}$.
2. **Nullspace $N(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$:**
   Spanned by the remaining $n - r$ right singular vectors $\{v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n\}$.
3. **Column Space $C(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$:**
   Spanned by the first $r$ left singular vectors $\{u_1, \dots, u_r\}$.
4. **Left Nullspace $N(A^T) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$:**
   Spanned by the remaining $m - r$ left singular vectors $\{u_{r+1}, \dots, u_m\}$.

The operational mapping between these subspaces is diagonal:
$$
A v_i = \begin{cases} \sigma_i u_i & \text{for } 1 \le i \le r \\ 0 & \text{for } r < i \le n \end{cases}
$$
Conversely, the transposed operator $A^T: \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^n$ maps the output basis back onto the input basis:
$$
A^T u_i = \begin{cases} \sigma_i v_i & \text{for } 1 \le i \le r \\ 0 & \text{for } r < i \le m \end{cases}
$$

## Full Size Form vs. Reduced Form of the SVD

### The Full Size Form
In the **Full Size SVD**, the orthogonal matrices $U$ and $V$ are complete square matrices encompassing the full ambient dimensions of the input and output spaces:
$$
A \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|ccc} v_1 & \cdots & v_r & v_{r+1} & \cdots & v_n \end{array} \right] 
= \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|ccc} u_1 & \cdots & u_r & u_{r+1} & \cdots & u_m \end{array} \right] 
\begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_r & 0_{r \times (n-r)} \\ 0_{(m-r) \times r} & 0_{(m-r) \times (n-r)} \end{bmatrix}
$$

where:
- $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ satisfies $V^T V = V V^T = I_n$.
- $U \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ satisfies $U^T U = U U^T = I_m$.
- $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ has identical dimensions to $A$, with $\Sigma_r = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r) \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times r}$.

Multiplying on the right by $V^T = V^{-1}$ yields:
$$
A = U \Sigma V^T
$$

### The Reduced (Compact) Form
When $\operatorname{rank}(A) = r < \min(m, n)$, the full matrix $\Sigma$ contains extensive zero blocks. Because $A v_i = 0$ for all $v_i \in N(A)$ and the last $m - r$ columns of $U$ multiply the zero rows of $\Sigma$, the nullspace and left nullspace components contribute nothing to the reconstruction of $A$. Removing these zero blocks produces the **Reduced SVD**:
$$
A V_r = U_r \Sigma_r \implies A = U_r \Sigma_r V_r^T
$$
where:
- $V_r = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 & \cdots & v_r \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$.
- $U_r = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 & \cdots & u_r \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$.
- $\Sigma_r = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r) \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times r}$ is strictly positive and square invertible.

> [!warning] Caution: Incomplete Projection Matrices
> In the reduced form, the columns of $V_r$ and $U_r$ remain orthonormal:
> $$
> V_r^T V_r = I_r \quad \text{and} \quad U_r^T U_r = I_r
> $$
> However, because $r < n$ and $r < m$, $V_r$ and $U_r$ are not square, and their outer products are not identity matrices. Instead, they form orthogonal projection operators onto the fundamental subspaces:
> $$
> V_r V_r^T = P_{C(A^T)} \ne I_n \quad \text{and} \quad U_r U_r^T = P_{C(A)} \ne I_m
> $$

## The Geometry of the SVD

### Action on the Unit Sphere: Circle to Ellipse
The SVD decomposes the action of any linear transformation $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ into three elementary geometric operations:
$$
A x = (U \Sigma V^T) x = U (\Sigma (V^T x))
$$
Applied to a unit sphere $\|x\|_2 = 1$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$:
1. **Initial Orthogonal Transformation ($V^T$):**
   The mapping $x \mapsto V^T x$ performs a rigid rotation (or reflection) of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Because orthogonal transformations preserve the Euclidean inner product and length ($\|V^T x\|_2 = \|x\|_2 = 1$), the unit sphere remains a unit sphere. The singular directions $v_1, \dots, v_n$ are aligned with the standard coordinate axes $e_1, \dots, e_n$.
2. **Axis-Aligned Scaling ($\Sigma$):**
   The diagonal matrix $\Sigma$ stretches or compresses the sphere along the coordinate axes by the factors $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r$, while flattening all directions corresponding to indices $i > r$ into zero. This transforms the unit sphere into an axis-aligned hyper-ellipsoid in $\mathbb{R}^m$.
3. **Final Orthogonal Transformation ($U$):**
   The mapping $y \mapsto U y$ applies a rigid rotation (or reflection) in $\mathbb{R}^m$. The axis-aligned hyper-ellipsoid is rotated into its final orientation. The principal semi-axes of the resulting hyper-ellipsoid align precisely with the left singular vectors $u_1, \dots, u_r$, with lengths equal to the singular values $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r$.

### Parameter Counting and Degrees of Freedom
The SVD provides an exact parameterization of the degrees of freedom possessed by a linear transformation:

1. **General $2 \times 2$ Invertible Matrix (4 Parameters):**
   A real $2 \times 2$ matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$ has 4 independent entries. In the SVD representation (excluding reflections by requiring $\det(U) = \det(V) = 1$):
   - $V^T$ represents a plane rotation through angle $\phi$ (1 parameter).
   - $\Sigma$ contains two positive singular values $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ (2 parameters).
   - $U$ represents a plane rotation through angle $\theta$ (1 parameter).
   $$
   A = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \cos\phi & \sin\phi \\ -\sin\phi & \cos\phi \end{bmatrix}
   $$
   The total number of parameters is $1 + 2 + 1 = 4$.

2. **Reduction for Symmetric Matrices (3 Parameters):**
   If $A = A^T$, the off-diagonal symmetry condition $b = c$ reduces the number of degrees of freedom from 4 to 3. In the SVD, this forces the input and output rotation angles to match: $\theta = \phi$ (or $\theta = -\phi$ if an eigenvalue is negative), giving $U = V = Q$ and recovering the spectral decomposition $S = Q \Lambda Q^T$.

3. **General $3 \times 3$ Matrix (9 Parameters):**
   A $3 \times 3$ matrix possesses 9 independent entries. In the SVD:
   - $\Sigma$ contains 3 singular values $\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3$ (3 parameters).
   - $V$ requires 3 independent rotation angles (the Euler angles: pitch, roll, yaw) (3 parameters).
   - $U$ requires 3 independent rotation angles (3 parameters).
   The sum is $3 + 3 + 3 = 9$ parameters.

### SVD vs. Symmetric Eigenvalue Decomposition
For a real symmetric matrix $S = Q \Lambda Q^T$:
- **Positive Semidefinite Case ($S \succeq 0$):**
  All eigenvalues are non-negative ($\lambda_i \ge 0$). Here, the singular values equal the eigenvalues: $\sigma_i = \lambda_i$, and the singular vectors coincide with the eigenvectors: $u_i = v_i = q_i$, yielding $U = V = Q$.
- **Indefinite or Negative Eigenvalue Case:**
  Singular values are defined to be strictly non-negative ($\sigma_i \ge 0$). If $S q = \lambda q$ with $\lambda = -\alpha < 0$ ($\alpha > 0$), the singular value is $\sigma = |\lambda| = \alpha$. To satisfy the SVD equation $S v = \sigma u$:
  $$
  S q = -\alpha q = \alpha (-q) = \sigma u
  $$
  The negative sign is absorbed by setting $v = q$ and $u = -q$ (or vice versa). Thus, $U \ne V$, but the left and right singular vectors remain collinear with the eigenvectors.

## Analytical Proof of the SVD

The derivation of the SVD relies on transforming the unsymmetric rectangular system $A$ into two coupled symmetric positive semidefinite systems: the Gram matrices $A^T A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ and $A A^T \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$.

### Normal Equations and Gram Matrices: $A^T A$ and $A A^T$
Assuming the SVD factorization $A = U \Sigma V^T$ exists with $U^T U = I_m$ and $V^T V = I_n$, the Gram products expand as:
$$
\begin{aligned}
A^T A &= (U \Sigma V^T)^T (U \Sigma V^T) = V \Sigma^T U^T U \Sigma V^T = V (\Sigma^T \Sigma) V^T \\
A A^T &= (U \Sigma V^T) (U \Sigma V^T)^T = U \Sigma V^T V \Sigma^T U^T = U (\Sigma \Sigma^T) U^T
\end{aligned}
$$
Because $A^T A$ and $A A^T$ are symmetric and positive semidefinite, the spectral theorem guarantees that:
1. $V$ must be the matrix of orthonormal eigenvectors of $A^T A$.
2. $U$ must be the matrix of orthonormal eigenvectors of $A A^T$.
3. The diagonal matrices $\Sigma^T \Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ and $\Sigma \Sigma^T \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ contain the eigenvalues of $A^T A$ and $A A^T$, respectively. Consequently, the non-zero singular values $\sigma_i$ are the positive square roots of the non-zero eigenvalues of $A^T A$ (and $A A^T$):
   $$
   \sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i(A^T A)} = \sqrt{\lambda_i(A A^T)} \quad (i = 1, \dots, r)
   $$

### The Sign-Coupling Problem and Definition of Left Singular Vectors
Computing the eigenvectors of $A^T A$ and $A A^T$ independently does not yield a valid SVD:
- Eigenvectors are determined only up to a factor of $\pm 1$ (or arbitrary rotations within multi-dimensional eigenspaces corresponding to repeated eigenvalues).
- The SVD requires that the vectors satisfy the directional coupling condition $A v_k = \sigma_k u_k$ for every $k \le r$.

To resolve this, the orthonormal eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_r$ of $A^T A$ are chosen first. The left singular vectors $u_1, \dots, u_r$ are then defined constructively:
$$
u_k = \frac{A v_k}{\sigma_k} \quad (k = 1, \dots, r)
$$

### Formal Verification of the Coupling

> [!theorem] Construction of the Left Singular Vectors
> Let $v_1, \dots, v_r$ be orthonormal eigenvectors of $A^T A$ corresponding to eigenvalues $\sigma_1^2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r^2 > 0$. Then the vectors:
> $$
> u_k = \frac{A v_k}{\sigma_k} \quad (k = 1, \dots, r)
> $$
> are orthonormal eigenvectors of $A A^T$ corresponding to the identical eigenvalues $\sigma_k^2$.

**Analytical Proof:**

1. **Verification of the Eigenvector Property for $A A^T$:**
   Applying $A A^T$ to $u_k$ and invoking the associative property $(A A^T) A = A (A^T A)$:
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   A A^T u_k &= A A^T \left( \frac{A v_k}{\sigma_k} \right) \\
             &= A \left( \frac{A^T A v_k}{\sigma_k} \right) \\
             &= A \left( \frac{\sigma_k^2 v_k}{\sigma_k} \right) \\
             &= \sigma_k^2 \left( \frac{A v_k}{\sigma_k} \right) \\
             &= \sigma_k^2 u_k
   \end{aligned}
   $$
   Hence, each $u_k$ is an eigenvector of $A A^T$ with eigenvalue $\sigma_k^2$.

2. **Verification of the Orthonormality of $\{u_k\}$:**
   Computing the inner product of $u_j$ and $u_k$:
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   u_j^T u_k &= \left( \frac{A v_j}{\sigma_j} \right)^T \left( \frac{A v_k}{\sigma_k} \right) \\
             &= \frac{v_j^T (A^T A v_k)}{\sigma_j \sigma_k} \\
             &= \frac{v_j^T (\sigma_k^2 v_k)}{\sigma_j \sigma_k} \\
             &= \frac{\sigma_k^2}{\sigma_j \sigma_k} (v_j^T v_k) \\
             &= \frac{\sigma_k}{\sigma_j} \delta_{jk} \\
             &= \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } j = k \\ 0 & \text{if } j \ne k \end{cases}
   \end{aligned}
   $$
   since the set $\{v_k\}$ is orthonormal ($v_j^T v_k = \delta_{jk}$). When $j = k$, $\sigma_k / \sigma_j = 1$. When $j \ne k$, $v_j^T v_k = 0$.

3. **Completion of the Orthonormal Bases:**
   - The vectors $\{v_1, \dots, v_r\}$ form an orthonormal basis for $C(A^T)$. Select an arbitrary orthonormal basis $\{v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n\}$ for $N(A)$ (eigenvectors of $A^T A$ for $\lambda = 0$). By the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra, $C(A^T) \perp N(A)$, making $V = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 & \cdots & v_n \end{bmatrix}$ a complete orthogonal matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$.
   - The vectors $\{u_1, \dots, u_r\}$ form an orthonormal basis for $C(A)$. Select an arbitrary orthonormal basis $\{u_{r+1}, \dots, u_m\}$ for $N(A^T)$ (eigenvectors of $A A^T$ for $\lambda = 0$). Because $C(A) \perp N(A^T)$, $U = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 & \cdots & u_m \end{bmatrix}$ is a complete orthogonal matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$.
   - For $k > r$, $A v_k = 0$. In block form:
     $$
     A V = \begin{bmatrix} A v_1 & \cdots & A v_r & 0 & \cdots & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1 u_1 & \cdots & \sigma_r u_r & 0 & \cdots & 0 \end{bmatrix} = U \Sigma
     $$
   - Multiplying by $V^T$ yields $A = U \Sigma V^T$. This completes the existence proof.

## Equal Nonzero Eigenvalues of $AB$ and $BA$

The identity of the non-zero singular values obtained from $A^T A$ and $A A^T$ is a specific instance of a general algebraic theorem regarding matrix products.

> [!theorem] Nonzero Eigenvalue Equivalence of $AB$ and $BA$
> Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$. Then the product matrices $AB \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ and $BA \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ possess identical non-zero eigenvalues with identical algebraic and geometric multiplicities.

### Analytical Proof
Let $\lambda \ne 0$ be a non-zero eigenvalue of $AB$, and let $x \in \mathbb{R}^m$ be an associated non-zero eigenvector:
$$
ABx = \lambda x \quad (x \ne 0)
$$
Multiply both sides on the left by $B$:
$$
B(ABx) = B(\lambda x) \implies (BA)(Bx) = \lambda (Bx)
$$
Define the candidate vector $y = Bx \in \mathbb{R}^n$. To establish that $y$ is an eigenvector of $BA$, it must be non-zero:
- Assume for contradiction that $y = Bx = 0$.
- Multiplying by $A$ yields $ABx = A(Bx) = A(0) = 0$.
- However, by hypothesis $ABx = \lambda x$, which implies $\lambda x = 0$.
- Because $\lambda \ne 0$, this requires $x = 0$, contradicting the assumption that $x$ is an eigenvector.
- Therefore, $y = Bx \ne 0$.

Thus, $BA y = \lambda y$ with $y \ne 0$, proving that $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $BA$. By symmetry of the argument (reversing the roles of $A$ and $B$), every non-zero eigenvalue of $BA$ is also an eigenvalue of $AB$.

### Application to SVD Gram Matrices
Setting $B = A^T$:
- $AB = A A^T$ is an $m \times m$ matrix.
- $BA = A^T A$ is an $n \times n$ matrix.
- Both matrices share exactly the same $r$ non-zero eigenvalues $\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_r^2$.
- The zero eigenvalues account for dimensional differences:
  - $A A^T$ has $m - r$ zero eigenvalues corresponding to the left nullspace $N(A^T)$.
  - $A^T A$ has $n - r$ zero eigenvalues corresponding to the nullspace $N(A)$.

> [!note] Invertible Similarity vs. Rectangular Equivalence
> When $m = n$ and $B$ is invertible, $BA$ is similar to $AB$ via the similarity transformation $B^{-1}(BA)B = AB$. However, the theorem above holds even when $m \ne n$ and neither matrix is square or invertible.

## Topological Existence of 2D SVD via Continuity

In two dimensions, the existence of orthogonal vectors that map to orthogonal vectors can be established using continuity and the Intermediate Value Theorem, providing direct geometric intuition without invoking determinants or characteristic polynomials.

### Intermediate Value Theorem on Orthogonal Vector Pairs
Consider the unit circle in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Let $v(\theta)$ and $w(\theta)$ be a pair of mutually perpendicular unit vectors parameterized by angle $\theta$:
$$
v(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta \\ \sin\theta \end{bmatrix}, \quad w(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} -\sin\theta \\ \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} = v\left(\theta + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)
$$
By definition, $v(\theta) \perp w(\theta)$ for all $\theta \in [0, \pi/2]$. Define the inner product of their images under $A$:
$$
f(\theta) = \langle Av(\theta), Aw(\theta) \rangle = (Av(\theta))^T (Aw(\theta)) = v(\theta)^T A^T A w(\theta)
$$
The function $f(\theta)$ is a continuous real-valued function of $\theta$.

Evaluate $f(\theta)$ across the quarter-turn interval $[0, \pi/2]$:
1. **At $\theta = 0$:**
   Let $V = v(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and $W = w(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. The initial inner product is:
   $$
   f(0) = (AV)^T (AW)
   $$
   If $f(0) = 0$, then $AV \perp AW$, and the vectors $V, W$ already form the right singular basis.
2. **At $\theta = \pi/2$:**
   The vectors rotate by $90^\circ$:
   $$
   v\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = W, \quad w\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = -V
   $$
   Evaluating $f(\pi/2)$:
   $$
   f\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = (AW)^T (A(-V)) = -(AW)^T (AV) = -f(0)
   $$

Because $f(\pi/2) = -f(0)$, the continuous function $f(\theta)$ takes values of opposite sign at the endpoints of the interval $[0, \pi/2]$. By the **Intermediate Value Theorem**, there must exist some angle $\theta^* \in [0, \pi/2]$ such that:
$$
f(\theta^*) = (Av(\theta^*))^T (Aw(\theta^*)) = 0
$$
At this angle $\theta^*$, the perpendicular unit input vectors $v = v(\theta^*)$ and $w = w(\theta^*)$ are mapped to strictly perpendicular output vectors $Av \perp Aw$. Normalizing these outputs:
$$
u_1 = \frac{Av}{\|Av\|_2}, \quad u_2 = \frac{Aw}{\|Aw\|_2}, \quad \sigma_1 = \|Av\|_2, \quad \sigma_2 = \|Aw\|_2
$$
produces the complete SVD: $A [v \; w] = [u_1 \; u_2] \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma_2 \end{bmatrix}$.

## Variational Formulation of Singular Values

Rather than defining singular values solely as matrix roots of eigenvalues, singular values possess an intrinsic variational characterization as solutions to constrained optimization problems over vector norms.

### The First Singular Vector as Operator Norm Maximizer
The largest singular value $\sigma_1$ measures the maximum amplification factor that $A$ can apply to any vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$:
$$
\sigma_1 = \|A\|_2 = \max_{x \ne 0} \frac{\|Ax\|_2}{\|x\|_2} = \max_{\|x\|_2 = 1} \|Ax\|_2
$$
Squaring this ratio connects the optimization directly to the **Rayleigh quotient** of the symmetric matrix $S = A^T A$:
$$
R(x) = \frac{\|Ax\|_2^2}{\|x\|_2^2} = \frac{x^T A^T A x}{x^T x} = \frac{x^T S x}{x^T x}
$$
Because $S$ is symmetric, its eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_n$ form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Expanding an arbitrary vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ in this basis:
$$
x = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i v_i \implies \|x\|_2^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2
$$
The Rayleigh quotient evaluates to a weighted average of the eigenvalues $\lambda_i(S) = \sigma_i^2$:
$$
R(x) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2 \sigma_i^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2} \le \sigma_1^2 \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2} = \sigma_1^2
$$
Equality holds if and only if $c_2 = c_3 = \cdots = c_n = 0$, which occurs when $x$ is an eigenvector corresponding to the maximum eigenvalue $\lambda_1(A^T A) = \sigma_1^2$. Thus:
$$
\max_{x \ne 0} \frac{\|Ax\|_2}{\|x\|_2} = \sigma_1, \quad \text{achieved at } x = v_1
$$

### Sequential Constrained Optimization (Courant-Fischer Characterization)
Subsequent singular vectors are found by maximizing the amplification ratio subject to orthogonality constraints against all previously discovered singular vectors:
$$
\sigma_{k+1} = \max_{\substack{x \ne 0 \\ v_1^T x = 0 \\ \vdots \\ v_k^T x = 0}} \frac{\|Ax\|_2}{\|x\|_2} = \|A v_{k+1}\|_2
$$
Geometrically, this mirrors finding the orthogonal semi-axes of the image hyper-ellipsoid in descending order: $v_1$ finds the longest semi-axis ($\sigma_1 u_1$), $v_2$ finds the longest semi-axis perpendicular to $u_1$ ($\sigma_2 u_2$), and so forth.

### Spectral Radius Bound: $|\lambda| \le \sigma_1$

> [!theorem] Spectral Radius Bound by Maximal Singular Value
> Let $A \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$ be an arbitrary square matrix. For every eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $A$:
> $$
> |\lambda| \le \sigma_1
> $$
> Consequently, the spectral radius $\rho(A) = \max_i |\lambda_i|$ satisfies $\rho(A) \le \|A\|_2 = \sigma_1$.

**Analytical Proof:**
Let $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ be an eigenvalue of $A$, and let $x \in \mathbb{C}^n$ be a normalized eigenvector such that $Ax = \lambda x$ with $\|x\|_2 = 1$. Taking Euclidean norms:
$$
\|Ax\|_2 = \|\lambda x\|_2 = |\lambda| \|x\|_2 = |\lambda|
$$
Express $A$ using its SVD, $A = U \Sigma V^T$. Because orthogonal (and unitary) matrices preserve Euclidean lengths:
$$
\|Ax\|_2 = \|U \Sigma V^T x\|_2 = \|\Sigma (V^T x)\|_2
$$
Define $y = V^T x$. Because $V^T$ is an isometry, $\|y\|_2 = \|x\|_2 = 1$. Evaluating the norm:
$$
\|\Sigma y\|_2^2 = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i^2 |y_i|^2 \le \sigma_1^2 \sum_{i=1}^n |y_i|^2 = \sigma_1^2 \|y\|_2^2 = \sigma_1^2
$$
Taking the square root yields:
$$
\|Ax\|_2 \le \sigma_1
$$
Combining this with $\|Ax\|_2 = |\lambda|$ establishes:
$$
|\lambda| \le \sigma_1
$$

## Rank-One Matrices and Cauchy-Schwarz Equivalence

### SVD of Outer Products $A = x y^T$
Consider an arbitrary rank-one matrix formed by non-zero vectors $x \in \mathbb{R}^m$ and $y \in \mathbb{R}^n$:
$$
A = x y^T
$$
Factoring out the vector magnitudes:
$$
A = (\|x\|_2 \|y\|_2) \left( \frac{x}{\|x\|_2} \right) \left( \frac{y}{\|y\|_2} \right)^T
$$
Setting:
$$
u_1 = \frac{x}{\|x\|_2}, \quad v_1 = \frac{y}{\|y\|_2}, \quad \sigma_1 = \|x\|_2 \|y\|_2
$$
Because $u_1$ and $v_1$ are unit vectors, this expression is precisely the reduced SVD of $A$:
$$
A = \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^T
$$
Since $\operatorname{rank}(A) = 1$, all remaining singular values are zero: $\sigma_2 = \cdots = \sigma_{\min(m, n)} = 0$.

### Equivalence with Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality
For a square rank-one matrix $A = x y^T \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, the trace equals the sum of eigenvalues. Because the row space and column space are one-dimensional, $A$ has at most one non-zero eigenvalue $\lambda_1$:
$$
A x = (x y^T) x = x (y^T x) = (y^T x) x
$$
Thus, $x$ is an eigenvector of $A$ corresponding to the eigenvalue:
$$
\lambda_1 = y^T x = x^T y
$$
All other eigenvalues are zero ($\lambda_2 = \cdots = \lambda_n = 0$).

Applying the spectral radius bound $|\lambda_1| \le \sigma_1$ to this matrix:
$$
|x^T y| \le \|x\|_2 \|y\|_2
$$
This demonstrates that the matrix eigenvalue inequality $|\lambda| \le \sigma_1$ on rank-one matrices is algebraically equivalent to the **Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality**.

## Computational Algorithms for the SVD

### Instability of Forming Normal Equations ($A^T A$)
The analytical definition $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i(A^T A)}$ suggests an elementary numerical procedure: construct $A^T A$, compute its eigenvalues via symmetric QR, and extract their square roots. In finite-precision floating-point arithmetic, this approach is numerically catastrophic:

1. **Squaring of the Condition Number:**
   The $L_2$ condition number of $A$ is defined by $\kappa_2(A) = \sigma_{\max} / \sigma_{\min}$. Forming $A^T A$ squares the condition number:
   $$
   \kappa_2(A^T A) = (\kappa_2(A))^2
   $$
   If $A$ has condition number $\kappa_2(A) \approx 10^8$, then $\kappa_2(A^T A) \approx 10^{16}$. In standard IEEE 754 double precision ($\epsilon_{\text{mach}} \approx 10^{-16}$), $A^T A$ appears completely singular, and all accuracy in the smaller singular values is destroyed.
2. **Loss of Information:**
   Entries of $A^T A$ compute sums of products of entries of $A$. Small singular values satisfying $\sigma_i < \sqrt{\epsilon_{\text{mach}}} \sigma_1$ are completely swamped by roundoff error and cannot be recovered.

### Golub-Kahan Two-Stage Bidiagonalization Algorithm
Stable numerical algorithms avoid forming $A^T A$ entirely, working directly on $A$ through orthogonal transformations.

In the symmetric eigenvalue problem $S = Q \Lambda Q^T$, a single orthogonal matrix $Q$ acts on both sides via similarity ($Q^T S Q$). To preserve symmetry, the matrix can only be reduced to **tridiagonal form**.

In contrast, the SVD allows two independent orthogonal matrices $Q_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ and $Q_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$. The orthogonal equivalence transformation $Q_1^T A Q_2$ provides double the degrees of freedom. This allows $A$ to be reduced to **upper bidiagonal form**:
$$
B = Q_1^T A Q_2 = \begin{bmatrix} c_1 & d_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & c_2 & d_2 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & c_n & d_n \\ 0 & \cdots & \cdots & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$
Because $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ are orthogonal, $B$ has the exact same singular values as $A$:
$$
B = U_B \Sigma V_B^T \implies A = Q_1 B Q_2^T = (Q_1 U_B) \Sigma (Q_2 V_B)^T = U \Sigma V^T
$$
Notice that $B^T B$ is tridiagonal, sharing the eigenvalues of $A^T A$, but $B^T B$ is never formed explicitly.

The **Golub-Kahan Algorithm** proceeds in two distinct stages:
1. **Bidiagonal Reduction:**
   Householder reflections are applied alternately from the left and right to zero out entries below the main diagonal and to the right of the first superdiagonal. This requires $\mathcal{O}(m n^2)$ floating-point operations.
2. **Implicitly Shifted QR Iteration:**
   An implicitly shifted QR algorithm is applied directly to the bidiagonal matrix $B$. The off-diagonal entries $d_i$ are driven to zero iteratively with quadratic or cubic convergence rates, requiring $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ operations, computing all singular values to high relative accuracy.

### Large-Scale Matrices: Randomized Linear Algebra
For massive datasets where $m$ and $n$ reach millions, deterministic $\mathcal{O}(m n^2)$ bidiagonalization becomes computationally intractable. Modern implementations employ **Randomized SVD (RSVD)**:
- A random Gaussian projection matrix $\Omega \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (k+p)}$ compresses the action of $A$ into a low-dimensional sample matrix $Y = A \Omega$.
- An orthonormal basis $Q$ for the range of $Y$ is computed via QR factorization.
- The small matrix $B = Q^T A$ is formed, and its SVD is computed using standard deterministic algorithms.
With high probability, this extracts near-optimal low-rank singular vectors in $\mathcal{O}(m n \log k)$ operations.
