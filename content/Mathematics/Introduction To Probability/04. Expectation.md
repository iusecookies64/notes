## 4.1 Definition of Expectation

### Motivation

The distribution of a random variable (r.v.) provides full information about the probability that the r.v. falls into any particular set (e.g., $P(X > 1000)$, $P(X = 5)$, $P(X \in [0,7])$). However, managing so many probabilities can be unwieldy, so it is often useful to summarize the r.v. with a single number representing its "average" value.

- The most common notion of "average" is the **mean**, also called the **expected value**.
- **Variance** and **standard deviation** quantify how "spread out" a distribution is, and are themselves defined in terms of expected values — so expectation is a foundational tool that goes far beyond simple averaging.

### Arithmetic Mean and Weighted Mean

Given a list of numbers $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$, the familiar **arithmetic mean** is:

$$
\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} x_j
$$

More generally, the **weighted mean** of $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ is:

$$
\text{weighted-mean}(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} x_j p_j
$$

where the weights $p_1, \ldots, p_n$ are pre-specified nonnegative numbers summing to 1. Note that setting $p_j = 1/n$ for all $j$ recovers the unweighted mean $\bar{x}$.

>[!Note]
>In a weighted mean, the value of the mean is in the range $[m, M]$, where $m$ is the smallest element ($x_{j}$) and $M$ is the largest element. It can be proven as follows,
>$$
>\begin{align}
>M & = (p_{1} + p_{2} + \dots + p_{n}) \cdot M \\
> & = p_{1}M + p_{2}M + \dots + p_{n}M \\
> & \geq p_{1}x_{1} + p_{2}x_{2} + \dots + p_{n}x_{n} \\
> & = \bar{x} \\
> \implies \bar{x} & \leq M
>\end{align}
>$$
>Similarly we can also prove the inequality $m \leq \bar{x}$
### Definition of Expectation (Discrete Case)

The expectation of a discrete r.v. is inspired by the weighted mean, with the weights given by probabilities.

> **Definition 4.1.1 (Expectation of a discrete r.v.).** The expected value (also called the _expectation_ or _mean_) of a discrete r.v. $X$ whose distinct possible values are $x_1, x_2, \ldots$ is defined by
> $$
> E(X) = \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} x_j P(X = x_j)
> $$
> If the support is finite, the sum is replaced by a finite sum. Equivalently,
> $$
> E(X) = \sum_{x} x \cdot P_{\text{PMF}}(X = x)
> $$
> where the sum ranges over the support of $X$ (since $x P(X=x) = 0$ for any $x$ not in the support, restricting to the support does not change the sum).
> 
> The expectation is **undefined** if the series $\sum_{j=1}^{\infty} |x_j| P(X = x_j)$ diverges, since in that case the value of $E(X)$ would depend on the order in which the $x_j$ are listed.

**In words:** $E(X)$ is a weighted average of the possible values of $X$, weighted by their probabilities.

### Worked Examples

**1. Fair 6-sided die.** Let $X$ take values $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$ with equal probability. Intuitively, the average is obtained by summing the values and dividing by 6:
$$
E(X) = \frac{1}{6}(1 + 2 + \cdots + 6) = 3.5
$$
Note that $X$ never actually equals its mean of $3.5$ — this is analogous to a country having an average of 1.8 children per household, even though no household literally has 1.8 children.

**2. Bernoulli random variable.** Let $X \sim \text{Bern}(p)$ and $q = 1 - p$. Then:
$$
E(X) = 1 \cdot p + 0 \cdot q = p
$$
This makes intuitive sense: $E(X)$ lies between the two possible values of $X$ (0 and 1), compromising based on how likely each outcome is.

- **Physical analogy (center of mass):** For $p < 1/2$, imagine two pebbles on a seesaw with masses $q$ and $p$. The fulcrum must be placed at $p$ for the seesaw to balance — this is the "center of mass" interpretation of expectation.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260912125858.png)

- **Frequentist interpretation:** Consider many independent Bernoulli trials, coding "success" as 1 and "failure" as 0. In the long run, the proportion of 1's in the data converges to $p$. Since the average of a list of 0's and 1's is exactly the proportion of 1's, this matches $E(X) = p$.

**3. Random variable with three possible values.** Let $X$ have three distinct possible values $a_1, a_2, a_3$ with probabilities $p_1, p_2, p_3$ respectively. Imagine simulating $n$ independent draws from the distribution of $X$. For large $n$, we would expect approximately $p_1 n$ draws equal to $a_1$, $p_2 n$ draws equal to $a_2$, and $p_3 n$ draws equal to $a_3$.

If the simulation matches these expectations, the arithmetic mean of the simulated results is approximately:
$$
\frac{p_1 n \cdot a_1 + p_2 n \cdot a_2 + p_3 n \cdot a_3}{n} = p_1 a_1 + p_2 a_2 + p_3 a_3 = E(X)
$$
_(A more rigorous version of this idea is developed later via the Law of Large Numbers.)_

### Expectation Depends Only on the Distribution

> **Proposition 4.1.2.** If $X$ and $Y$ are discrete r.v.s with the same distribution, then $E(X) = E(Y)$ (if either side exists).

**Proof.** The definition of $E(X)$ depends only on the PMF of $X$. $\blacksquare$

**Important caveat — the converse is false:** Two random variables with the _same_ expected value can have completely different distributions. The expected value is only a one-number summary of the "center" of a distribution; it does not determine the spread, or how likely the r.v. is to be positive, etc.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260912130059.png)

### Common Pitfalls

> **h 4.1.3 (Replacing an r.v. by its expectation).** For any discrete r.v. $X$, the expected value $E(X)$ is a number (assuming it exists). A common mistake is to replace a random variable by its expectation without justification. This is incorrect:
> 
> - **Mathematically:** $X$ is a function (random), while $E(X)$ is a constant.
> - **Statistically:** doing so ignores the variability of $X$.
> 
> The only exception is the degenerate case where $X$ is itself a constant.

> **Notation 4.1.4.** $E(X)$ is often abbreviated $EX$. Similarly:
> 
> - $E(X^2)$ is abbreviated $EX^2$
> - $E(X^n)$ is abbreviated $EX^n$

> **h 4.1.5 (Order of operations).** Care is required when working with expectations of powers of random variables.
> 
> - $EX^2$ denotes the expectation of the random variable $X^2$ — **not** the square of the number $EX$. That is, in general:
> $$
> EX^2 \neq (EX)^2
> $$
> - Unless parentheses explicitly indicate otherwise, for the expectation of an r.v. raised to a power, the power is applied **first**, then the expectation is taken. For example:
> $$
> E(X-1)^4 = E\left[(X-1)^4\right], \quad \text{NOT} \quad \left(E(X-1)\right)^4
> $$

# 4.2 Linearity of Expectation

## The Core Theorem

The most important property of expectation is **linearity**: the expected value of a sum of random variables equals the sum of the individual expected values.

> **Theorem 4.2.1 (Linearity of expectation).** For any r.v.s $X, Y$ and any constant $c$:
> $$
> \begin{align}E(X + Y) & = E(X) + E(Y) \\ E(cX) & = c,E(X)\end{align}
> $$

- The second equation says constant factors can be pulled out of an expectation — intuitively reasonable and easily verified directly from the definition.
- The first equation seems reasonable when $X$ and $Y$ are **independent**. What is surprising is that it holds **even when $X$ and $Y$ are dependent**.

**Extreme-dependence intuition:** Suppose $X$ always equals $Y$. Then $X + Y = 2X$, and both sides of $E(X+Y) = E(X) + E(Y)$ equal $2E(X)$ — so linearity holds even in this most extreme case of dependence.

_Note: linearity holds for all r.v.s, not just discrete ones, but this chapter proves it only for the discrete case._

## Building Intuition: Grouped vs. Ungrouped Averages

Consider the list of numbers $(1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 3, 3, 5)$.

**Ungrouped mean** — sum all values and divide by the list length (each element weighted $1/8$):
$$
\frac{1}{8}(1+1+1+1+1+3+3+5) = 2
$$
**Grouped mean** — group identical values together and take a weighted average:
$$
\frac{5}{8} \cdot 1 + \frac{2}{8} \cdot 3 + \frac{1}{8} \cdot 5 = 2
$$
Both methods give the same answer. **This insight — that averages can be computed either grouped or ungrouped — is exactly what is needed to prove linearity.**

### Connecting to Random Variables

Recall that a random variable $X$ is a function assigning a real number to every outcome $s$ in the sample space, and $X$ may assign the same value to multiple outcomes. When this happens:

- The **definition of expectation** groups these outcomes into a "super-pebble," whose weight $P(X=x)$ is the total weight of its constituent pebbles. This is the **grouped** way of averaging.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260912134316.png)

**Advantage of the grouped definition:** it lets us work directly with the distribution of $X$, without returning to the sample space.

**Disadvantage:** If $Y$ is another r.v. on the same sample space, the super-pebbles created by $Y$ differ from those created by $X$ — with different weights $P(Y=y)$ — making it hard to directly combine $\sum_x x P(X=x)$ and $\sum_y y P(Y=y)$.

### The Ungrouped Approach

Instead, we can take a weighted average over **individual pebbles** $s$, using $X(s)$ (the value $X$ assigns to $s$) and $P({s})$ (the weight of $s$):
$$
E(X) = \sum_{s} X(s) P({s})
$$
Similarly, for another r.v. $Y$ on the same sample space:
$$
E(Y) = \sum_{s} Y(s) P({s})
$$
**Key advantage:** this breaks the sample space into its smallest units, so the _same_ weights $P({s})$ apply to every random variable defined on that space — allowing direct combination:
$$
E(X) + E(Y) = \sum_{s} X(s) P({s}) + \sum_{s} Y(s) P({s}) = \sum_{s} (X+Y)(s) P({s}) = E(X+Y)
$$
This completes the proof of linearity for discrete r.v.s.

## Intuition via Simulation

If we simulate many draws from the distribution of $X$, the histogram of simulated values approximates the true PMF of $X$, and the arithmetic mean of the simulated values approaches $E(X)$ (formalized later by the **Law of Large Numbers**, Chapter 10).

**Setup:** Suppose we run an experiment $n$ times (large $n$), recording $X$, $Y$, and $X+Y$ each time. Each row of Figure 4.4 is one repetition; the columns are the draws of $X$, $Y$, and $X+Y$.

| $X$      | $Y$      | $X+Y$    |
| -------- | -------- | -------- |
| 3        | 4        | 7        |
| 2        | 2        | 4        |
| 6        | 8        | 14       |
| 10       | 23       | 33       |
| 1        | $-3$     | $-2$     |
| 1        | 0        | 1        |
| 5        | 9        | 14       |
| 4        | 1        | 5        |
| $\vdots$ | $\vdots$ | $\vdots$ |

There are two equivalent ways to sum the last column:

1. Directly add all numbers in the $X+Y$ column.
2. Add all numbers in the $X$ column, add all numbers in the $Y$ column, then add the two column sums.

Dividing by $n$ throughout, this shows the following two procedures are equivalent:
$$
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i + \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i + y_i)
$$
$$
E(X) + E(Y) = E(X+Y)
$$
- Taking the mean of the last column approximates $E(X+Y)$ (by the Law of Large Numbers).
- Taking the mean of column 1 plus the mean of column 2 approximates $E(X) + E(Y)$.

**Linearity of expectation is thus just a fact about arithmetic** — adding the same numbers in two different orders.

**Crucially, independence was never used.** In the above table, $X$ and $Y$ appear dependent (large $Y$ tends to accompany large $X$ — i.e., they are _positively correlated_, a term formalized in Chapter 7). Shuffling the draws of $Y$ could completely change the dependence structure between $X$ and $Y$, but would not affect either column's sum — so dependence is irrelevant to linearity.

## Applications of Linearity

Linearity is an extremely powerful computational tool, often letting us bypass the definition of expectation entirely.

### Example 4.2.2 — Binomial Expectation

For $X \sim \text{Bin}(n,p)$, we compute $E(X)$ two ways.

**Method 1: Direct definition.**
$$
E(X) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} k P(X=k) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} k \binom{n}{k} p^k q^{n-k}
$$
Using the identity $k\binom{n}{k} = n\binom{n-1}{k-1}$:
$$
\sum_{k=0}^{n} k \binom{n}{k} p^k q^{n-k} = n \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n-1}{k-1} p^k q^{n-k} = np \sum_{k=1}^{n} \binom{n-1}{k-1} p^{k-1} q^{n-k}
$$
$$
= np \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \binom{n-1}{j} p^{j} q^{n-1-j} = np
$$
The final sum equals 1 because it is the total probability of the $\text{Bin}(n-1,p)$ PMF (or by the Binomial Theorem). Hence:
$$
E(X) = np
$$
This method requires recalling combinatorial identities and manipulating binomial coefficients.

**Method 2: Linearity of expectation (much shorter).**

Write $X$ as a sum of $n$ independent $\text{Bern}(p)$ r.v.s:
$$
X = I_1 + \cdots + I_n
$$
where each $I_j$ has $E(I_j) = 1\cdot p + 0 \cdot q = p$. By linearity:
$$
E(X) = E(I_1) + \cdots + E(I_n) = np
$$
### Example 4.2.3 — Hypergeometric Expectation

Let $X \sim \text{HGeom}(w,b,n)$: the number of white balls in a sample of size $n$ drawn **without replacement** from an urn with $w$ white and $b$ black balls.

As with the Binomial case, write $X$ as a sum of Bernoulli indicators:
$$
X = I_1 + \cdots + I_n
$$
where $I_j = 1$ if the $j$th ball drawn is white, and $0$ otherwise. By symmetry, $I_j \sim \text{Bern}(p)$ with:
$$
p = \frac{w}{w+b}
$$
since unconditionally, the $j$th ball drawn is equally likely to be any ball in the urn.

**Key distinction from the Binomial case:** the $I_j$ are **not independent** (sampling is without replacement — given that one drawn ball is white, the chance another is white decreases). However:

> **Linearity still holds for dependent random variables.**

Therefore:
$$
E(X) = \frac{nw}{w+b}
$$
**Doing It Algebraically:** Let's try to derive the above formula algebraically for fun.
$$
\begin{align}
E(X) & = \sum_{k=0}^{n} k\cdot \frac{\binom{w}{k}\binom{b}{n-k}}{\binom{w+b}{n}} \\
 & = \frac{1}{\binom{w+b}{n}} \sum_{k=0}^{n} k \cdot \binom{w}{k} \binom{b}{n-k}  \\
 & = \frac{1}{\binom{w+b}{n}} (w\cdot \binom{w+b-1}{n-1}) \\
 & = \frac{n!\cdot (w+b-n)!}{(w+b)!} \cdot w \cdot \frac{(w+b-1)!}{(n-1)! \cdot (w+b-n)!} \\
 & = \frac{nw}{w+b}
\end{align}
$$
The identity that we is similar to one in exercise 18 in [01. Probability and Counting](01.%20Probability%20and%20Counting.md).

## Monotonicity of Expectation

Linearity also gives a quick proof that "bigger r.v.s have bigger expectations."

> **Proposition 4.2.4 (Monotonicity of expectation).** Let $X$ and $Y$ be r.v.s such that $X \geq Y$ with probability 1. Then:
> $$
> E(X) \geq E(Y)
> $$
> with equality if and only if $X = Y$ with probability 1.

**Proof.** _(Proved here for discrete r.v.s; the result holds generally.)_

Let $Z = X - Y$. Since $X \geq Y$ with probability 1, $Z$ is nonnegative with probability 1, so $E(Z) \geq 0$ (as $E(Z)$ is a sum of nonnegative terms).

By linearity:
$$
E(X) - E(Y) = E(X-Y) \geq 0
$$
which gives $E(X) \geq E(Y)$.

If $E(X) = E(Y)$, then by linearity $E(Z) = 0$. This implies $P(X=Y) = P(Z=0) = 1$, since if even one term in the sum defining $E(Z)$ were positive, the entire sum would be positive. $\blacksquare$

# 4.3 Geometric and Negative Binomial

## The Geometric Distribution

> **Story 4.3.1 (Geometric distribution).** Consider a sequence of independent Bernoulli trials, each with the same success probability $p \in (0,1)$, performed until a success occurs. Let $X$ be the number of **failures before** the first success. Then $X$ has the **Geometric distribution** with parameter $p$, denoted $X \sim \text{Geom}(p)$.

**Example:** Flipping a fair coin until the first Heads — the number of Tails before the first Heads is $\text{Geom}(1/2)$.

### Deriving the PMF

Picture the Bernoulli trials as a string of 0's (failures) ending in a single 1 (success). Each 0 has probability $q = 1-p$, and the final 1 has probability $p$. A string of $k$ failures followed by one success has probability $q^k p$.

> **Theorem 4.3.2 (Geometric PMF).** If $X \sim \text{Geom}(p)$, then
> $$
> P(X = k) = q^k p, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots
> $$
> where $q = 1-p$.

**Validity check** (via geometric series):
$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} q^k p = p \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} q^k = p \cdot \frac{1}{1-q} = 1
$$
Just as the Binomial Theorem validates the Binomial PMF, a geometric series validates the Geometric PMF.

### The CDF

> **Theorem 4.3.3 (Geometric CDF).** If $X \sim \text{Geom}(p)$, then
> $$
> F(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x < 0 \\ 1 - q^{\lfloor x \rfloor + 1}, & x \geq 0 \end{cases}
> $$
> where $q = 1-p$ and $\lfloor x \rfloor$ is the greatest integer $\leq x$.

**Proof.**

- For $x < 0$: $F(x) = 0$ since $X$ cannot be negative.
- For $n$ a nonnegative integer:
$$
F(n) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} P(X=k) = p\sum_{k=0}^{n} q^k = p \cdot \frac{1-q^{n+1}}{1-q} = 1 - q^{n+1}
$$
Alternatively, since $X \geq n+1$ means the first $n+1$ trials were all failures:
$$
F(n) = 1 - P(X > n) = 1 - P(X \geq n+1) = 1 - q^{n+1}
$$
- For real $x \geq 0$: since $X$ only takes integer values, $F(x) = P(X \leq x) = P(X \leq \lfloor x \rfloor)$. For example, $P(X \leq 3.7) = P(X \leq 3) + P(3 < X \leq 3.7) = P(X \leq 3)$.

This establishes the claimed formula for $F$. $\blacksquare$

### A Note on Conventions

> **h 4.3.4 (Conventions for the Geometric).** Different sources define "Geometric" differently — some include the success trial in the count. **In this book:**
> 
> - The **Geometric** distribution excludes the success (counts only failures).
> - The **First Success** distribution includes the success.

> **Definition 4.3.5 (First Success distribution).** In a sequence of independent Bernoulli trials with success probability $p$, let $Y$ be the number of trials until (and including) the first success. Then $Y \sim \text{FS}(p)$.

**Converting between Geometric and First Success:**

If $Y \sim \text{FS}(p)$, then $Y - 1 \sim \text{Geom}(p)$, and:
$$
P(Y = k) = P(Y - 1 = k - 1).
$$
Conversely, if $X \sim \text{Geom}(p)$, then $X + 1 \sim \text{FS}(p)$.

### Geometric Expectation

> **Example 4.3.6 (Geometric expectation).** Let $X \sim \text{Geom}(p)$. By definition:
> $$
> E(X) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} k q^k p, \qquad q = 1-p
> $$

This isn't a plain geometric series due to the extra factor of $k$. The trick: notice $kq^{k-1}$ is the derivative of $q^k$ with respect to $q$. Start from:
$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} q^k = \frac{1}{1-q}
$$
Since $0 < q < 1$, this series converges. Differentiating both sides with respect to $q$:
$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} k q^{k-1} = \frac{1}{(1-q)^2}
$$
Multiplying both sides by $pq$ recovers the original sum:
$$
E(X) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} k q^k p = pq \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} k q^{k-1} = \frac{pq}{(1-q)^2} = \frac{q}{p}.
$$
_(A story proof based on first-step analysis — conditioning on the outcome of the first trial — is given later in Example 9.1.8.)_

> **Example 4.3.7 (First Success expectation).** Since $Y \sim \text{FS}(p)$ can be written as $Y = X + 1$ with $X \sim \text{Geom}(p)$:
> $$
> E(Y) = E(X+1) = \frac{q}{p} + 1 = \frac{1}{p}
> $$

## The Negative Binomial Distribution

The Negative Binomial generalizes the Geometric: instead of waiting for one success, we wait for a fixed number $r$ of successes.

> **Story 4.3.8 (Negative Binomial distribution).** In a sequence of independent Bernoulli trials with success probability $p$, let $X$ be the number of **failures before the $r$th success**. Then $X \sim \text{NBin}(r,p)$.

**Comparison with the Binomial:**

|                | Binomial            | Negative Binomial                      |
| -------------- | ------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Fixed quantity | Number of trials    | Number of successes ($r$)              |
| Counts         | Number of successes | Number of failures until $r$th success |

### The PMF

> **Theorem 4.3.9 (Negative Binomial PMF).** If $X \sim \text{NBin}(r,p)$, then
> $$
> P(X = n) = \binom{n+r-1}{r-1} p^r q^n, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots
> $$
> where $q = 1-p$.

**Proof.** Picture a string of 0's and 1's (1 = success). Any specific string with $n$ zeros and $r$ ones has probability $p^r q^n$. Since the sequence must stop right after the $r$th success, the string ends in a 1. Among the remaining $n + r - 1$ positions, we choose $r-1$ of them to place the other successes. Hence:
$$
P(X=n) = \binom{n+r-1}{r-1} p^r q^n
$$
Let's verify that this is a valid PMF, for that the following sum must equal $1$.
$$
\begin{align}
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} P(X=n) & =\sum_{n = 0}^{\infty} \binom{n + r - 1}{r - 1} p^{r} q^{n} \\
 & = p^{r} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \binom{n + r - 1}{n} q^{n} \\
 & = p^{r} (1 - q)^{-r} \tag{Negative Binomial Expansion} \\
 & = 1
\end{align}
$$
Hence, this is a valid PMF. It is called binomial as the sum expands like negative binomial expansion [01. Probability and Counting](01.%20Probability%20and%20Counting.md) (Section 1.4 end).
### Representation as a Sum of i.i.d. Geometrics

Just as a Binomial can be written as a sum of i.i.d. Bernoullis, a Negative Binomial can be written as a sum of i.i.d. Geometrics.

> **Theorem 4.3.10.** Let $X \sim \text{NBin}(r,p)$ (failures before the $r$th success). Then:
> $$
> X = X_1 + \cdots + X_r
> $$
> where the $X_i$ are i.i.d. $\text{Geom}(p)$.

**Proof.** Let $X_1$ be the number of failures until the first success, $X_2$ the number of failures between the 1st and 2nd successes, and generally $X_i$ the number of failures between the $(i-1)$th and $i$th successes.

- $X_1 \sim \text{Geom}(p)$ directly from the Geometric story.
- After the first success, the number of additional failures until the next success is _still_ Geometric (the trials "reset"), so $X_2 \sim \text{Geom}(p)$, and similarly for all $X_i$.
- The $X_i$ are independent because the underlying Bernoulli trials are all independent.

Summing the $X_i$ gives the total number of failures before the $r$th success, i.e., $X$. $\blacksquare$

### Negative Binomial Expectation

> **Example 4.3.11 (Negative Binomial expectation).** Let $X \sim \text{NBin}(r,p) = X_1 + \cdots + X_r$ with $X_i$ i.i.d. $\text{Geom}(p)$. By linearity:
> $$
> E(X) = E(X_1) + \cdots + E(X_r) = r \cdot \frac{q}{p}
> $$

No additional calculation was needed beyond linearity — a major payoff of the representation in Theorem 4.3.10.

## Example 4.3.12 — The Coupon Collector Problem

**Setup:** There are $n$ types of toys, collected one at a time, each draw equally likely to be any of the $n$ types. What is the expected number of toys $E(N)$ needed to get a complete set?

**Strategy:** Decompose $N$ into simpler pieces amenable to linearity:
$$
N = N_1 + N_2 + \cdots + N_n,
$$
where:

- $N_1$ = number of toys until the first new type (always $1$, since the very first toy is always new).
- $N_2$ = _additional_ toys until the second new type.
- $N_j$ = additional toys until the $j$th new type.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260916114117.png)

**Distribution of each $N_j$:** After collecting the first toy type, there is a $1/n$ chance of a repeat (failure) and $(n-1)/n$ chance of a new type (success). So:
$$
N_2 \sim \text{FS}!\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right).
$$
More generally:
$$
N_j \sim \text{FS}!\left(\frac{n-j+1}{n}\right).
$$
**Applying linearity:**
$$
E(N) = E(N_1) + E(N_2) + \cdots + E(N_n) = \frac{n}{n} + \frac{n}{n-1} + \frac{n}{n-2} + \cdots + \frac{n}{1} = n \sum_{j=1}^{n} \frac{1}{j}.
$$
For large $n$, this is approximately:
$$
E(N) \approx n(\log n + 0.577).
$$
### Connecting Back to the Negative Binomial

Both problems involve waiting for a specified number of successes and examining the gaps between successes, but with two key differences:

| |Negative Binomial (Thm 4.3.10)|Coupon Collector|
|---|---|---|
|Counts successes themselves?|Excluded — counts only failures between successes|Included — counts total toys, so uses First Success r.v.s|
|Success probability per trial|Constant $\Rightarrow$ i.i.d. Geometrics|Decreases after each success (harder to find new types) $\Rightarrow$ independent but **not** identically distributed|

## Nonlinear Functions of Random Variables

> **h 4.3.13 (Expectation of a nonlinear function of an r.v.).** Expectation is linear, but in general:
> $$
> E(g(X)) \neq g(E(X))
> $$
> for arbitrary (nonlinear) functions $g$. Care must be taken not to move $E$ across a nonlinear function.

## Example 4.3.14 — The St. Petersburg Paradox

**Setup:** Flip a fair coin until the first Heads. Payoff is
\$$2$ if the game lasts 1 round, \$$4$ for 2 rounds,\$$8$ for 3 rounds, and in general \$$2^n$ for $n$ rounds. What is the fair value (expected payoff)?

**Solution:**

Let $X$ be the winnings, so $X = 2^N$ where $N \sim \text{FS}(1/2)$ is the number of rounds. Then $X = 2$ with probability $1/2$, $X=4$ with probability $1/4$, $X=8$ with probability $1/8$, etc.:
$$
E(X) = \frac{1}{2}\cdot 2 + \frac{1}{4}\cdot 4 + \frac{1}{8}\cdot 8 + \cdots = \infty.
$$
**The expected winnings are infinite!** Meanwhile, since $N \sim \text{FS}(1/2)$:
$$
E(N) = \frac{1}{1/2} = 2.
$$
So $E(2^N) = \infty$ while $2^{E(N)} = 2^2 = 4$. Infinity clearly does not equal 4 — a stark illustration of the danger of confusing $E(g(X))$ with $g(E(X))$ for nonlinear $g$.

### Why Is This a "Paradox"?

Although the expected payoff is infinite, most people would not pay much to play — even if they could afford to lose the stake. One resolution: real-world money is finite.

**Scenario A — capped game, stranger flees after 40 rounds (payoff becomes $0$):**
$$
E(X) = \sum_{n=1}^{40} \frac{1}{2^n}\cdot 2^n + \sum_{n=41}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^n}\cdot 0 = 40.
$$
_(Since $2^{40} \approx 1.1 \times 10^{12}$, over a trillion dollars is still potentially available, and reaching round 40 is extremely unlikely.)_

**Scenario B — capped payoff at $2^{40}$ instead of walking away with nothing:**
$$
E(X) = \sum_{n=1}^{40} \frac{1}{2^n}\cdot 2^n + \sum_{n=41}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^n}\cdot 2^{40} = 40 + 1 = 41.
$$
This is only a \$$1$ increase over Scenario A. **Key insight:** the infinite expectation in the original paradox is driven entirely by an infinite tail of extremely rare, extremely large payoffs. Truncating this tail — as is realistic in the real world — dramatically reduces the expected value.

# 4.4 Indicator R.V.s and the Fundamental Bridge

## Recap: Indicator Random Variables

The **indicator r.v.** $I_A$ (or $I(A)$) for an event $A$ is defined as:
$$
I_A = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } A \text{ occurs}, \ 0, & \text{if } A \text{ does not occur}. \end{cases}
$$
$I_A$ is a **Bernoulli random variable**, where "success" means $A$ occurs and "failure" means $A$ does not occur.

### Properties of Indicator R.V.s

> **Theorem 4.4.1 (Indicator r.v. properties).** Let $A$ and $B$ be events. Then:
> 
> 1. $(I_A)^k = I_A$ for any positive integer $k$.
> 2. $I_{A^c} = 1 - I_A$.
> 3. $I_{A \cap B} = I_A I_B$.
> 4. $I_{A \cup B} = I_A + I_B - I_A I_B$.

**Proof.**

1. Since $0^k = 0$ and $1^k = 1$ for any positive integer $k$.
2. $1 - I_A$ equals 1 when $A$ does not occur and 0 when $A$ occurs — matching $I_{A^c}$.
3. $I_A I_B$ equals 1 exactly when both $I_A = 1$ and $I_B = 1$, i.e., when $A \cap B$ occurs.
4. Using De Morgan's law and Properties 2–3:
$$
I_{A\cup B} = 1 - I_{A^c \cap B^c} = 1 - I_{A^c}I_{B^c} = 1 - (1-I_A)(1-I_B) = I_A + I_B - I_A I_B. \qquad \blacksquare
$$
## The Fundamental Bridge

Indicator r.v.s create a direct link between **probability** and **expectation**.

> **Theorem 4.4.2 (Fundamental bridge between probability and expectation).** There is a one-to-one correspondence between events and indicator r.v.s, and:
> $$
> P(A) = E(I_A)
> $$

**Proof.** Each event $A$ determines a unique indicator r.v. $I_A$, and conversely $A$ can be recovered from $I_A$ via $A = \{ s \in S : I_A(s) = 1 \}$. Since $I_A \sim \text{Bern}(p)$ with $p = P(A)$, we have $E(I_A) = P(A)$. $\blacksquare$

**Significance:** the fundamental bridge lets us express _any_ probability as an expectation, and — used together with linearity — lets us compute expectations of complicated r.v.s by breaking them into indicators.

## Application: Boole's/Bonferroni's Inequality and Inclusion-Exclusion

> **Example 4.4.3 (Boole, Bonferroni, and inclusion-exclusion).** Let $A_1, \ldots, A_n$ be events.

**Boole's/Bonferroni's inequality.** Observe:
$$
I(A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_n) \leq I(A_1) + \cdots + I(A_n),
$$
since if the left side is 0 the inequality is immediate, and if the left side is 1, at least one term on the right must also be 1. Taking expectations of both sides (using linearity and the fundamental bridge):
$$
P(A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_n) \leq P(A_1) + \cdots + P(A_n).
$$
**Inclusion-exclusion (general $n$).** Using indicator properties:
$$
1 - I(A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_n) = I(A_1^c \cap \cdots \cap A_n^c) = (1-I(A_1))\cdots(1-I(A_n))
$$
$$
= 1 - \sum_i I(A_i) + \sum_{i<j} I(A_i)I(A_j) - \cdots + (-1)^n I(A_1)\cdots I(A_n).
$$
Taking the expectation of both sides and applying the fundamental bridge proves the **inclusion-exclusion theorem**. $\blacksquare$

_(For $n=2$, this follows directly from taking expectations in Property 4 of Theorem 4.4.1.)_

## Strategy: Indicators + Linearity for Complicated Counting R.V.s

The fundamental bridge is especially powerful in reverse: express a complicated r.v. (whose distribution is unknown or intractable) as a **sum of indicators**, find each indicator's expectation via the fundamental bridge, then sum via linearity. This strategy was already used implicitly for the Binomial and Hypergeometric expectations.

**General heuristic:** for an r.v. counting the number of [nouns] (people, places, or things), define one indicator per potential [noun].

### Example 4.4.4 — Matching Problem (Revisited)

**Setup:** A well-shuffled deck of $n$ cards labeled $1$ through $n$. A card is a _match_ if its position equals its label. Let $X$ = number of matches. Find $E(X)$.

**Why not a named distribution?** $X$ takes integer values in $[0, n]$, suggesting Binomial or Hypergeometric — but neither fits, since $X$ can never equal $n-1$ (if $n-1$ cards match, the last one must too).

**Solution via indicators:** Write $X = I_1 + I_2 + \cdots + I_n$, where:
$$
I_j = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{the } j\text{th card is a match}, \ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}
$$
By the fundamental bridge:
$$
E(I_j) = P(A_j) = \frac{1}{n} \quad \text{for all } j.
$$
By linearity:
$$
E(X) = E(I_1) + \cdots + E(I_n) = n \cdot \frac{1}{n} = 1.
$$
**The expected number of matches is always 1, regardless of $n$.** Even though the $I_j$ are dependent in a complicated way (making $X$'s distribution neither Binomial nor Hypergeometric), linearity still applies without issue.

### Example 4.4.5 — Distinct Birthdays and Birthday Matches

**Part 1: Expected number of distinct birthdays.**

Let $X$ = number of distinct birthdays among $n$ people. Write $X = I_1 + \cdots + I_{365}$, where:
$$
I_j = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{day } j \text{ is represented}, \ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}
$$
One indicator per _day of the year_, since $X$ counts represented days. By the fundamental bridge:
$$
E(I_j) = P(\text{day } j \text{ represented}) = 1 - P(\text{no one born on day } j) = 1 - \left(\frac{364}{365}\right)^n.
$$
By linearity:
$$
E(X) = 365\left[1 - \left(\frac{364}{365}\right)^n\right].
$$
**Part 2: Expected number of birthday matches.**

Let $Y$ = number of pairs of people sharing a birthday. Label people $1, \ldots, n$ and order the $\binom{n}{2}$ pairs. Write:
$$
Y = J_1 + J_2 + \cdots + J_{\binom{n}{2}},
$$
where $J_i$ is the indicator that the $i$th pair shares a birthday — one indicator per _pair of people_. Since any two people share a birthday with probability $1/365$:
$$
E(Y) = \binom{n}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{365}.
$$
**Symmetry note:** both examples exploit symmetry — every indicator in the sum has the _same_ expected value, letting us multiply a single indicator's expectation by the number of terms rather than computing each separately.

## Symmetry from Equally Likely Permutations

### Example 4.4.6 — Putnam Problem (Local Maxima)

**Definition:** A permutation $a_1, \ldots, a_n$ of $1, \ldots, n$ has a **local maximum** at position $j$ if $a_j > a_{j-1}$ and $a_j > a_{j+1}$ (with boundary conventions at $j=1$ and $j=n$). Example: $4,2,5,3,6,1$ has local maxima at positions $1, 3, 5$.

**Question (Putnam 2006):** For $n \geq 2$, what is the average number of local maxima over all $n!$ equally likely permutations?

**Solution:** Let $I_1, \ldots, I_n$ be indicators, with $I_j = 1$ if there's a local maximum at position $j$.

- For $1 < j < n$: $E(I_j) = 1/3$, since a local max at $j$ is equivalent to $a_j$ being the largest of ${a_{j-1}, a_j, a_{j+1}}$, and all orderings of these three values are equally likely.
- For $j = 1$ or $j = n$: $E(I_j) = 1/2$, since there is only one neighbor to compare against.

By linearity:
$$
E\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n} I_j\right) = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} + (n-2)\cdot \frac{1}{3} = \frac{n+1}{3}.
$$
## The Negative Hypergeometric Distribution

This section completes the four-way classification of sampling schemes:

| |With replacement|Without replacement|
|---|---|---|
|**Fixed number of trials**|Binomial|Hypergeometric|
|**Fixed number of successes**|Negative Binomial|Negative Hypergeometric|

> **Example 4.4.7 (Negative Hypergeometric).** An urn has $w$ white balls and $b$ black balls, drawn one by one without replacement until $r$ white balls are obtained. The number of black balls drawn before the $r$th white ball is $\text{NHGeom}(w,b,r)$ (assume $r \leq w$).

**Examples:**

- Dealing a shuffled deck until the first ace: the number of non-aces dealt is $\text{NHGeom}(4, 48, 1)$.
- A student trying courses at random until finding 4 good ones (out of $g$ good, $b$ bad): the number of bad courses tried is $\text{NHGeom}(g, b, 4)$.

### PMF

Interpreting $X = k$ as: there are many ways to do this, here we provide 2 ways. The first approach is to first choose the desired set of $r+k$ balls (i.e. $r$ white and $k$ black balls), then count the possible arrangements:
$$
P(X=k) = \frac{\binom{w}{r} \cdot \binom{b}{k} \cdot r \cdot (r + k - 1)!}{\binom{w + b}{r + k} \cdot (r + k)!}
$$
**Alternative (simpler) derivation:** imagine drawing until the urn is empty (valid since continuing past the $r$th white ball doesn't affect $X$). Line up all $w+b$ balls in a uniformly random order. Then $X=k$ means: among the first $r+k-1$ positions there are exactly $r-1$ white balls, position $r+k$ is white, and the remaining $w-r$ white balls are among the last $w+b-r-k$ positions. By the naive definition of probability, the problems boils down to number of ways to arrange $r-1$ white balls are placed in among the first $r+k-1$ and $w - r$ white balls among the last $w+b - r - k$ balls, total ways are $\binom{w+b}{w}$:
$$
P(X=k) = \frac{\dbinom{r+k-1}{r-1}\dbinom{w+b-r-k}{w-r}}{\dbinom{w+b}{w}}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \ldots, b.
$$
### Expectation via Indicators

Direct computation from the definition of expectation gives complicated sums, but:
$$
E(X) = \frac{rb}{w+1} \quad \text{for } X \sim \text{NHGeom}(w,b,r).
$$
**Proof (case $r=1$).** Continue drawing until the urn is empty. Label black balls $1, \ldots, b$. Let $I_j$ be the indicator that black ball $j$ is drawn _before any white balls_. Considering only black ball $j$ and the $w$ white balls (ignoring other black balls), all relative orderings are equally likely by symmetry, so:
$$
P(I_j = 1) = \frac{1}{w+1}.
$$
By linearity:
$$
E\left(\sum_{j=1}^{b} I_j\right) = \sum_{j=1}^{b} E(I_j) = \frac{b}{w+1}.
$$
**Sanity check:** This is increasing in $b$, decreasing in $w$, and correct in the extremes:

- $b = 0$: no black balls, expectation is $0$. ✓
- $w = 0$: all black balls drawn before a (nonexistent) white ball, i.e., all $b$ are drawn. ✓

Also, $b/(w+1)$ is slightly **smaller** than $b/w$ (the mean of $\text{Geom}(w/(w+b))$, the _with-replacement_ analogue) — sensible, since sampling _without_ replacement should give a shorter expected waiting time. (Analogy: searching for a lost item without replacement — i.e., not re-checking already-ruled-out locations — is more efficient.)

**General $r$:** Write $X = X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_r$, where $X_1$ = black balls before the 1st white ball, $X_2$ = black balls between the 1st and 2nd white balls, etc. By the same argument as above, $E(X_j) = b/(w+1)$ for each $j$. By linearity:
$$
E(X) = \frac{rb}{w+1}.
$$
## Expectation via the Survival Function

An alternative formula for the expectation of a nonnegative integer-valued r.v., using **tail probabilities** rather than the PMF directly.

> **Theorem 4.4.8 (Expectation via survival function).** Let $X$ be a nonnegative integer-valued r.v. with CDF $F$, and let $G(x) = 1 - F(x) = P(X > x)$ be the **survival function**. Then:
> $$
> E(X) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} G(n)
> $$

**Proof (bounded case).** Suppose $X \leq b$ for some nonnegative integer $b$. Write $X$ as a sum of indicators:
$$
X = I_1 + I_2 + \cdots + I_b, \qquad I_n = I(X \geq n).
$$
(E.g., if $X = 7$, then $I_1$ through $I_7$ equal 1 and the rest equal 0.)

By linearity and the fundamental bridge, and noting ${X \geq k} = {X > k-1}$:
$$
E(X) = \sum_{k=1}^{b} E(I_k) = \sum_{k=1}^{b} P(X \geq k) = \sum_{n=0}^{b-1} P(X > n) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} G(n). \qquad \blacksquare
$$
### Example 4.4.9 — Geometric Expectation, Revisited

Let $X \sim \text{Geom}(p)$, $q = 1-p$. By the Geometric story, ${X > n}$ is the event that the first $n+1$ trials are all failures, so $P(X>n) = q^{n+1}$. By Theorem 4.4.8:
$$
E(X) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} P(X>n) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} q^{n+1} = \frac{q}{1-q} = \frac{q}{p},
$$
confirming the earlier derivation of the Geometric mean.

# 4.5 Law of the Unconscious Statistician (LOTUS)

## Motivation

As seen in the St. Petersburg paradox, $E(g(X)) \neq g(E(X))$ in general when $g$ is not linear. So how do we correctly compute $E(g(X))$?

One approach: find the distribution of $g(X)$ first, then apply the definition of expectation directly to $g(X)$.

**Surprising alternative:** it is possible to compute $E(g(X))$ directly from the distribution of $X$ — **without ever finding the distribution of $g(X)$**. This is the **Law of the Unconscious Statistician (LOTUS)**.

## The Theorem

> **Theorem 4.5.1 (LOTUS).** If $X$ is a discrete r.v. and $g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, then:
> $$
> E(g(X)) = \sum_{x} g(x) P(X=x)
> $$
> where the sum is over all possible values of $X$.

**Interpretation:** $E(g(X))$ can be computed knowing _only_ the PMF of $X$ — the PMF of $g(X)$ is never needed.

**Origin of the name:** going from $E(X) = \sum_x x P(X=x)$ to $E(g(X)) = \sum_x g(x) P(X=x)$ looks like a purely mechanical substitution of $x$ for $g(x)$ — something one could do "unconsciously." It may seem too easy to be legitimate, but LOTUS confirms it works.

## Building Intuition: A Special Case

Let $X$ have support $0, 1, 2, \ldots$ with probabilities $p_0, p_1, p_2, \ldots$, i.e., $P(X=n) = p_n$. Then $X^3$ has support $0^3, 1^3, 2^3, \ldots$ with the _same_ probabilities $p_0, p_1, p_2, \ldots$ attached to the corresponding cubed values. So:
$$
E(X) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} n, p_n, \qquad E(X^3) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} n^3 p_n.
$$
As LOTUS suggests, going from $E(X)$ to $E(X^3)$ just requires changing $n$ to $n^3$ in front of $p_n$. This case was easy because $g(x) = x^3$ is one-to-one — but LOTUS holds far more generally.

## Proof of LOTUS (General Case)

The key idea mirrors the proof of linearity: write $E(g(X))$ in **ungrouped** form as a sum over individual sample-space pebbles $s$:

$$
E(g(X)) = \sum_{s} g(X(s)) P({s}),
$$

then regroup the pebbles into super-pebbles according to the value $X$ assigns them. Within the super-pebble ${X = x}$, $g(X)$ always equals $g(x)$ (a constant), so:

$$
E(g(X)) = \sum_{s} g(X(s)) P({s}) = \sum_{x} \sum_{s: X(s)=x} g(X(s)) P({s}) = \sum_{x} g(x) \sum_{s: X(s)=x} P({s}) = \sum_{x} g(x) P(X=x).
$$

In the last step, $\sum_{s: X(s)=x} P({s})$ is recognized as the total weight of the super-pebble ${X=x}$, i.e., $P(X=x)$. $\blacksquare$

# 4.6 Variance

## Motivation

LOTUS's most important application is computing **variance**. Like expectation, variance is a single-number summary of a distribution:

- **Expectation** — the center of mass.
- **Variance** — how _spread out_ the distribution is.

## Definition

> **Definition 4.6.1 (Variance and standard deviation).** The variance of an r.v. $X$ is:
> $$
> \text{Var}(X) = E\big[(X - EX)^2\big]
> $$
> The **standard deviation (SD)** is the square root of the variance:
> $$
> \text{SD}(X) = \sqrt{\text{Var}(X)}
> $$

**Notational caution:** $E(X-EX)^2$ means the expectation of the random variable $(X-EX)^2$ — **not** $\big(E(X-EX)\big)^2$, which equals $0$ by linearity (since $E(X - EX) = EX - EX = 0$).

### Why Square the Deviations?

Variance measures the average distance of $X$ from its mean, but:

- The plain average deviation $E(X - EX)$ **always equals 0** by linearity — positive and negative deviations cancel out.
- **Squaring** the deviations ensures both positive and negative deviations contribute positively to the measure of spread.

**Units issue:** since variance is an average _squared_ distance, its units are the square of $X$'s units (e.g., dollars$^2$ if $X$ is in dollars). Taking the square root (the standard deviation) restores the original units.

### Why Not Use $E|X - EX|$?

This alternative would also fix the cancellation problem while preserving units, but is far less popular than $E(X-EX)^2$, primarily because:

- The absolute value function is **not differentiable at 0**.
- The squaring function is differentiable everywhere, and is central to many fundamental results (e.g., the Pythagorean theorem).

## A More Convenient Formula

> **Theorem 4.6.2.** For any r.v. $X$:
> $$
> \text{Var}(X) = E(X^2) - (EX)^2
> $$

**Proof.** Let $\mu = EX$. Expanding $(X-\mu)^2$ and applying linearity:

$$
E(X-\mu)^2 = E(X^2 - 2\mu X + \mu^2) = E(X^2) - 2\mu, EX + \mu^2 = E(X^2) - \mu^2. \qquad \blacksquare
$$

_(This is the formula used most often in practice.)_

## Properties of Variance

1. **Shift invariance:** $\text{Var}(X + c) = \text{Var}(X)$ for any constant $c$. _Intuition:_ shifting a distribution left or right moves its center of mass but not its spread. Below is the algebraic proof of this property,
	$$
	\begin{align}
	Var(X+c) & = E(X+c)^{2} - (E(X+c))^{2} \\
	 & = E(X^{2} + c^{2} - 2cX) - (EX + c)^{2} \\
	 & = EX^{2} + c^{2} - 2cEX - (EX)^{2} - c^{2} + 2cEX \\
	 & = EX^{2} - (EX)^{2}
	\end{align}
	$$
2. **Scaling:** $\text{Var}(cX) = c^2 \text{Var}(X)$ for any constant $c$.
    
3. **Additivity under independence:** If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, then:
	$$
	\text{Var}(X+Y) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y).
	$$
	_(Proved in detail in Chapter 7.)_ This is **not** true in general for dependent $X, Y$. Extreme example: if $X$ always equals $Y$,
	$$
	\text{Var}(X+Y) = \text{Var}(2X) = 4 \cdot \text{Var}(X) > 2 \cdot \text{Var}(X) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y)
	$$
	whenever $\text{Var}(X) > 0$.
    
4. **Nonnegativity:** $\text{Var}(X) \geq 0$, with equality **if and only if** $P(X = a) = 1$ for some constant $a$. In words: the _only_ random variables with zero variance are constants (degenerate r.v.s); every other r.v. has strictly positive variance.
	
	**Proof of Property 4.** $\text{Var}(X)$ is the expectation of the nonnegative r.v. $(X-EX)^2$, so $\text{Var}(X) \geq 0$.
	
	- If $P(X=a)=1$: then $E(X) = a$ and $E(X^2) = a^2$, so $\text{Var}(X) = a^2 - a^2 = 0$.
	- Conversely, if $\text{Var}(X) = 0$: then $E(X-EX)^2 = 0$, which forces $(X-EX)^2 = 0$ with probability 1 — i.e., $X$ equals its mean with probability 1. $\blacksquare$

> **h 4.6.3 (Variance is not linear).** Unlike expectation, variance is **not linear**:
> 
> - Constants come out **squared**: $\text{Var}(cX) = c^2\text{Var}(X)$.
> - The variance of a sum need **not** equal the sum of variances when the summands are dependent.

## Example 4.6.4 — Geometric and Negative Binomial Variance

**Using LOTUS to find $\text{Var}(X)$ for $X \sim \text{Geom}(p)$:**

We already know $E(X) = q/p$. By LOTUS:
$$
E(X^2) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} k^2 P(X=k) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} k^2 p q^k = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k^2 p q^k.
$$
**Strategy:** differentiate the geometric series repeatedly, as was done for $E(X)$.

Start with:
$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} q^k = \frac{1}{1-q}.
$$
Differentiate once with respect to $q$ (starting the sum at $k=1$ since the $k=0$ term vanishes):
$$
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k q^{k-1} = \frac{1}{(1-q)^2}.
$$
Multiply both sides by $q$ (to "replenish" a factor of $q$ before differentiating again — otherwise a second derivative gives $k(k-1)$ instead of $k^2$):
$$
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k q^k = \frac{q}{(1-q)^2}.
$$
Differentiate once more:
$$
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k^2 q^{k-1} = \frac{1+q}{(1-q)^3}.
$$
So:
$$
E(X^2) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k^2 p q^k = pq \cdot \frac{1+q}{(1-q)^3} = \frac{q(1+q)}{p^2}.
$$

**Combining via Theorem 4.6.2:**
$$
\text{Var}(X) = E(X^2) - (EX)^2 = \frac{q(1+q)}{p^2} - \left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^2 = \frac{q}{p^2}.
$$
**First Success distribution:** since $Y \sim \text{FS}(p) = X + 1$ (a constant shift), and shifting doesn't affect variance:
$$
\text{Var}(Y) = \frac{q}{p^2}.
$$
**Negative Binomial:** Since $X \sim \text{NBin}(r,p)$ can be written as a sum of $r$ **i.i.d.** $\text{Geom}(p)$ r.v.s (Theorem 4.3.10), and variance is additive for **independent** r.v.s:
$$
\text{Var}(X) = r \cdot \frac{q}{p^2}.
$$

## Using Indicators Instead of LOTUS

LOTUS is a general-purpose tool for $E(g(X))$, but often leads to messy sums — it should be a **last resort**. For variance, indicator r.v.s frequently offer a cleaner path.

### Example 4.6.5 — Binomial Variance

Let $X \sim \text{Bin}(n,p)$. Write $X = I_1 + I_2 + \cdots + I_n$, where $I_j$ is the indicator of the $j$th trial being a success.

**Variance of each indicator:**
$$
\text{Var}(I_j) = E(I_j^2) - (E(I_j))^2 = p - p^2 = p(1-p),
$$
using $I_j^2 = I_j$ (Property 1 of Theorem 4.4.1), so $E(I_j^2) = E(I_j) = p$.

**Summing via independence:** since the $I_j$ are independent, their variances add:
$$
\text{Var}(X) = \text{Var}(I_1) + \cdots + \text{Var}(I_n) = np(1-p).
$$

### Alternative Derivation: Counting Pairs of Successes

$E(X^2)$ can also be found by relating it to $E\big[\binom{X}{2}\big]$, the expected number of pairs of successful trials — computed via an indicator for each _pair_ of trials:
$$
E\left[\binom{X}{2}\right] = \binom{n}{2} p^2.
$$
Let's compute $E[\binom{X}{2}]$, below we provide two methods,
**Method 1:** Since $\binom{X}{2} = \frac{X(X-1)}{2}$:
$$
\begin{align}
E[\binom{X}{2}] & = E\left( \frac{X(X-1)}{2} \right) \\
 & = \frac{1}{2} (E(X^2) - E(X)) \\
 & = \frac{1}{2} (E(X^2) - np)
\end{align}
$$
**Method 2:** Using LOTUS,
$$
\begin{align}
E[\binom{X}{2}] & = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{k}{2} P(X=k) \\
 & = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{k(k-1)}{2} P(X=k) \\
 & = \frac{1}{2}\left( \sum_{k=0}^{n} k^{2} P(X=k) - \sum_{k=0}^{n} kP(X=k) \right) \\
 & = \frac{1}{2}(EX^{2} - EX) \\
 & = \frac{1}{2} (EX^{2} - np)
\end{align}
$$
From the above results we now calculate the value of $EX^{2}$
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{1}{2}(EX^{2} - np) & = \binom{n}{2} p^{2} \\
\implies EX^{2} & = n(n-1)p^{2} + np
\end{align}
$$
which again yields:
$$
\text{Var}(X) = E(X^2) - (EX)^2 = \big(n(n-1)p^2 + np\big) - (np)^2 = np(1-p).
$$
_(This pairs-of-trials strategy is used again to find the variance of the Hypergeometric distribution — see Exercise 48.)_

# 4.7 Poisson

The **Poisson distribution** is the last discrete distribution introduced in this chapter, and is extremely popular for modeling discrete (count) data.

## Definition and PMF

> **Definition 4.7.1 (Poisson distribution).** An r.v. $X$ has the **Poisson distribution** with parameter $\lambda > 0$ if its PMF is:
> $$
> P(X=k) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots
> $$
> We write $X \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda)$.

**Validity check:** using the Taylor series $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{\lambda^k}{k!} = e^\lambda$:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!} = e^{-\lambda}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!} = e^{-\lambda} \cdot e^\lambda = 1.
$$

## Example 4.7.2 — Poisson Expectation and Variance

Let $X \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda)$. We show that **both the mean and the variance equal $\lambda$**.

### Mean

$$
E(X) = e^{-\lambda} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} k\frac{\lambda^k}{k!} = e^{-\lambda}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k \frac{\lambda^k}{k!} = \lambda e^{-\lambda} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{\lambda^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} = \lambda e^{-\lambda} e^{\lambda} = \lambda.
$$

**Steps:** the $k=0$ term is dropped (it's zero); one factor of $\lambda$ is factored out of the sum; what remains is exactly the Taylor series for $e^\lambda$.

### Variance

By LOTUS:

$$
E(X^2) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} k^2 P(X=k) = e^{-\lambda}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} k^2 \frac{\lambda^k}{k!}.
$$

The derivation parallels the Geometric variance calculation: differentiate the series $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{\lambda^k}{k!} = e^\lambda$ with respect to $\lambda$, and "replenish" as needed.

**First derivative:**

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k\frac{\lambda^{k-1}}{k!} = e^{\lambda} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k \frac{\lambda^k}{k!} = \lambda e^{\lambda}.
$$

**Second derivative** (differentiate again, then replenish):

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k^2 \frac{\lambda^{k-1}}{k!} = e^{\lambda} + \lambda e^{\lambda} = e^{\lambda}(1+\lambda) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k^2 \frac{\lambda^k}{k!} = e^{\lambda}\lambda(1+\lambda).
$$

Therefore:

$$
E(X^2) = e^{-\lambda} \cdot e^{\lambda}\lambda(1+\lambda) = \lambda(1+\lambda),
$$

$$
\text{Var}(X) = E(X^2) - (EX)^2 = \lambda(1+\lambda) - \lambda^2 = \lambda.
$$

**Conclusion:** for $X \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda)$, both $E(X)$ and $\text{Var}(X)$ equal $\lambda$.

## The Poisson Story

The Poisson distribution typically arises when **counting successes over a region or time interval**, where there is a **large number of trials**, each with a **small probability of success**. Example scenarios approximately Poisson-distributed:

- **Emails received in an hour** — many potential senders, but each is individually unlikely to email in that hour. (Equivalently: subdivide the hour into $3.6\times 10^6$ milliseconds, each with a tiny chance of an email arriving.)
- **Chocolate chips in a cookie** — subdivide the cookie into small cubes; each cube has a small chance of containing a chip, but there are many cubes.
- **Earthquakes per year in a region** — at any given moment/location the probability is small, but there are many possible times/locations.

The parameter $\lambda$ is the **rate of occurrence** of these rare events (e.g., $\lambda=20$ emails/hour, $\lambda=10$ chips/cookie, $\lambda=2$ earthquakes/year).

## The Poisson Paradigm (Law of Rare Events)

> **Approximation 4.7.3 (Poisson paradigm).** Let $A_1, \ldots, A_n$ be events with $p_j = P(A_j)$, where $n$ is large, the $p_j$ are small, and the $A_j$ are independent or weakly dependent. Let
> $$
> X = \sum_{j=1}^{n} I(A_j)
> $$
> count how many of the $A_j$ occur. Then $X$ is approximately $\text{Pois}(\lambda)$, with
> $$
> \lambda = \sum_{j=1}^{n} p_j
> $$

**On rigor:** formally proving this requires precise notions of "weak dependence" and "good approximation" (of which there are several). One remarkable result: if the $A_j$ are independent, $N \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda)$, and $B$ is any set of nonnegative integers, then:

$$
|P(X \in B) - P(N \in B)| \leq \min\left(1, \frac{1}{\lambda}\right) \sum_{j=1}^{n} p_j^2.
$$

This bounds the approximation error and clarifies that "small $p_j$" is measured **relative to $\lambda$** — i.e., $\sum_j p_j^2$ should be small compared to $\lambda$. _(Proved via the advanced Stein-Chen method.)_

**Why "law of rare events"?** "Rare" refers to the individual $p_j$ being small — **not** to $\lambda$ itself being small. In the email example, the low per-person probability is offset by the large number of potential senders.

**Approximate, not exact:** a Poisson r.v. has no upper bound, but the count of how many of $A_1,\ldots,A_n$ occur is capped at $n$ (e.g., a cookie can only hold so many chips). Still, the approximation is typically excellent. The conditions are flexible: trials can have **different** success probabilities, and need not be fully independent (just not strongly dependent) — making the Poisson paradigm applicable to a wide range of count-data scenarios.

## Example 4.7.4 — Balls in Boxes (Occupancy Problems)

**Setup:** $k$ distinguishable balls placed randomly into $n$ distinguishable boxes, all $n^k$ arrangements equally likely.

**(a) Expected number of empty boxes.**

Let $I_j$ be the indicator that box $j$ is empty. Then:

$$
E(I_j) = P(I_j=1) = \left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)^k.
$$

By linearity:

$$
E\left(\sum_{j=1}^n I_j\right) = n\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^k.
$$

**(b) Probability at least one box is empty.**

For $k < n$, the probability is $1$ (trivially, some box must be empty). In general, let $A_j$ = event box $j$ is empty. By inclusion-exclusion:

$$
P(A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_n) = \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} (-1)^{j+1}\binom{n}{j}\left(1-\frac{j}{n}\right)^k.
$$

**(c) Numerical case: $n=1000$, $k=5806$.**

The expected number of empty boxes is $\approx 3$. Since there are many boxes each with a small chance of being empty, the number of empty boxes $X$ is approximately $\text{Pois}(3)$. Using $e^3 \approx 20$:

$$
P(X \geq 1) = 1 - P(X=0) \approx 1 - e^{-3} \approx 1 - \frac{1}{20} = 0.95.
$$

## Poisson Approximation for the Birthday Problem

The Poisson paradigm greatly simplifies analyzing the birthday problem (Chapter 1) and its variants, which are otherwise hard to solve exactly.

### Example 4.7.5 — Birthday Problem, Poisson Approximation

With $m$ people, each pair has probability $p=1/365$ of sharing a birthday, and there are $\binom{m}{2}$ pairs. By the Poisson paradigm, the number of matches $X$ is approximately $\text{Pois}(\lambda)$ with:

$$
\lambda = \binom{m}{2}\frac{1}{365}.
$$

So $P(X \geq 1) = 1 - P(X=0) \approx 1 - e^{-\lambda}$.

**Check with $m=23$:** $\lambda = 253/365$, giving $1 - e^{-\lambda} \approx 0.500002$ — matching the classical result that 23 people are needed for a 50-50 chance of a shared birthday. Although $m=23$ is small, the relevant quantity driving the approximation is $\binom{m}{2}$ (the number of "trials," i.e., pairs), which is already reasonably large — so the approximation performs well.

### Example 4.7.6 — Near-Birthday Problem

**Question:** how many people are needed for a 50-50 chance that two share birthdays within one day of each other (same day or adjacent)?

This has no simple exact solution, but the Poisson paradigm still applies. Any two people have birthdays within one day of each other with probability $3/365$ (the second person must be born on the same day, the day before, or the day after). With $\binom{m}{2}$ pairs:

$$
\lambda = \binom{m}{2}\frac{3}{365}.
$$

A similar calculation shows $m = 14$ or more is needed. Remarkably, **$m=14$ is also the exact answer**.

## Example 4.7.7 — Birth-Minute and Birth-Hour Matches

**Setup:** $1600$ sophomores at a college, usual birthday-problem assumptions.

### (a) Same day, hour, and minute

This is the birthday problem with $c = 365 \cdot 24 \cdot 60 = 525{,}600$ categories instead of $365$. With $n=1600$, create an indicator for each pair; by linearity, the expected number of matching pairs is:

$$
\lambda_1 = \binom{n}{2} \frac{1}{c}.
$$

Poisson approximation gives:

$$
P(\text{at least one match}) \approx 1 - e^{-\lambda_1} \approx 0.9122.
$$

_(This closely matches the exact computed value of $0.9125$.)_

### (b) Four sophomores sharing the same day and hour

Now there are $b = 365 \times 24 = 8760$ categories. Two different Poisson approximations are compared:

**Method 1 — indicator per quadruplet of sophomores.**

$$
\lambda_2 = \binom{n}{4}\frac{1}{b^3}.
$$

$$
P(\text{at least one match}) \approx 1 - e^{-\lambda_2} \approx 0.333.
$$

**Method 2 — indicator per day-hour.**

Let $I_j$ = indicator that at least 4 people were born in the $j$th day-hour, $1 \le j \le b$. With $p = 1/b$, $q = 1-p$:

$$
E(I_j) = 1 - q^n - np q^{n-1} - \binom{n}{2}p^2q^{n-2} - \binom{n}{3}p^3q^{n-3}.
$$

(i.e., 1 minus the probability of 0, 1, 2, or 3 people in that day-hour.)

The expected number of "crowded" day-hours is $\lambda_3 = b \cdot E(I_1)$, giving:

$$
P(\text{at least one match}) \approx 1 - e^{-\lambda_3} \approx 0.295.
$$

**Comparing accuracy:** the true value (computed exactly) is $0.296$ — so **Method 2 is more accurate**.

**Why?** Method 1's indicators (per quadruplet) are more strongly dependent: knowing that sophomores ${1,2,3,4}$ share a birth day-hour substantially raises the chance that ${1,2,3,5}$ also do (they already share 3 members). In contrast, Method 2's indicators (per day-hour) are much closer to independent — knowing one specific day-hour has $\geq 4$ births tells us almost nothing about another day-hour. Since the Poisson paradigm relies on weak dependence, Method 2's weaker dependence yields a better approximation.

# 4.8 Connections Between Poisson and Binomial

The Poisson and Binomial distributions are closely linked — the relationship parallels that between the Binomial and Hypergeometric (Chapter 3):

- **Poisson $\to$ Binomial:** obtained by **conditioning**.
- **Binomial $\to$ Poisson:** obtained by taking a **limit**.

Both directions rely on the fact that **the sum of independent Poissons is Poisson** (analogous to the sum of independent Binomials being Binomial).

## Sum of Independent Poissons

> **Theorem 4.8.1 (Sum of independent Poissons).** If $X \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda_1)$, $Y \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda_2)$, and $X \perp Y$, then:
> $$
> X + Y \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)
> $$

**Proof.** Condition on $X$ and apply the law of total probability:

$$
P(X+Y=k) = \sum_{j=0}^{k} P(X+Y=k \mid X=j)P(X=j) = \sum_{j=0}^{k} P(Y=k-j)P(X=j)
$$

$$
= \sum_{j=0}^{k} \frac{e^{-\lambda_2}\lambda_2^{k-j}}{(k-j)!}\cdot \frac{e^{-\lambda_1}\lambda_1^j}{j!} = \frac{e^{-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)}}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\lambda_1^j \lambda_2^{k-j} = \frac{e^{-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)}(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)^k}{k!},
$$

where the last step uses the Binomial Theorem. This is exactly the $\text{Pois}(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$ PMF. $\blacksquare$

**Intuition (Poisson story):** if two independent types of events occur at rates $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$, the combined event rate is $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2$.

## Poisson Given a Sum of Poissons (Poisson $\to$ Binomial)

> **Theorem 4.8.2 (Poisson given a sum of Poissons).** If $X \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda_1)$, $Y \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda_2)$, $X \perp Y$, then the conditional distribution of $X$ given $X+Y=n$ is:
> $$
> \text{Bin}\left(n, \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}\right)
> $$

**Proof.** As with the analogous Binomial/Hypergeometric result, apply Bayes' rule:

$$
\begin{align}
P(X=k \mid X+Y=n) & = \frac{P(X+Y=n \mid X=k)P(X=k)}{P(X+Y=n)}  \\
& = \frac{P(Y=n-k)P(X=k)}{P(X+Y=n)}.
\end{align}
$$

Substituting the PMFs of $X$, $Y$, and $X+Y \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$ (Theorem 4.8.1):

$$
\begin{align}
P(X=k \mid X+Y=n) & = \frac{\dfrac{e^{-\lambda_2}\lambda_2^{n-k}}{(n-k)!}\cdot \dfrac{e^{-\lambda_1}\lambda_1^k}{k!}}{\dfrac{e^{-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)}(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)^n}{n!}} \\
 &  = \binom{n}{k}\frac{\lambda_1^k \lambda_2^{n-k}}{(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)^n} \\
 &  = \binom{n}{k}\left(\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}\right)^k \left(\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}\right)^{n-k}
\end{align}
$$

which is precisely the $\text{Bin}\left(n,\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}\right)$ PMF. $\blacksquare$

## Poisson Approximation to the Binomial (Binomial $\to$ Poisson)

Conversely, taking $n \to \infty$ and $p \to 0$ with $np$ fixed in a $\text{Bin}(n,p)$ distribution produces a Poisson distribution.

> **Theorem 4.8.3 (Poisson approximation to Binomial).** If $X \sim \text{Bin}(n,p)$ and $n \to \infty$, $p \to 0$ such that $\lambda = np$ remains fixed (or converges to a constant $\lambda$), then the PMF of $X$ converges to the $\text{Pois}(\lambda)$ PMF.

This is a special case of the **Poisson paradigm** where the $A_j$ are independent with equal probabilities, so $\sum_{j=1}^{n} I(A_j)$ is exactly Binomial — allowing a direct limiting proof.

**Proof.** For $0 \leq k \leq n$:

$$
\begin{align}
P(X=k) & = \binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k} \\
 & = \frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{n^k}\cdot \frac{\lambda^k}{k!}\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^n \left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{-k}
\end{align}
$$

Taking $n \to \infty$ with $k$ fixed:

$$
\begin{align}
\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{n^k} &  \to 1, \\
\qquad \left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^n  & \to e^{-\lambda}, \\
\qquad \left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{-k}  & \to 1
\end{align}
$$

where the middle limit follows from the compound interest formula (Math Appendix, Section A.2.5). Hence:

$$
P(X=k) \to \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!},
$$

the $\text{Pois}(\lambda)$ PMF. $\blacksquare$

**Practical takeaway:** if $n$ is large, $p$ is small, and $np$ is moderate, $\text{Bin}(n,p)$ can be well-approximated by $\text{Pois}(np)$. What matters most is that $p$ be small; for $X \sim \text{Bin}(n,p)$ and $N \sim \text{Pois}(np)$, the error in approximating $P(X \in B) \approx P(N \in B)$ is at most $\min(p, np^2)$.

## Example 4.8.4 — Visitors to a Website

**Setup:** $n = 10^6$ people independently decide whether to visit a website each day, each with probability $p = 2\times 10^{-6}$. Approximate $P(X \geq 3)$ where $X \sim \text{Bin}(n,p)$.

**Why approximate?** Exact computation with $n$ this large and $p$ this small risks numerical/computational issues.

**Solution:** Since $n$ is large, $p$ is small, and $\lambda = np = 2$ is moderate, use $\text{Pois}(2)$:
$$
\begin{align}
P(X \geq 3) & = 1 - P(X<3) \approx 1 - e^{-2} - e^{-2}\cdot 2 - e^{-2}\cdot \frac{2^2}{2!}  \\
& = 1 - 5e^{-2} \\
& \approx 0.3233.
\end{align}
$$
This turns out to be extremely accurate.

# 4.9 Using Probability and Expectation to Prove Existence

A striking application: probability and expectation can be used to **prove existence** of objects with desired properties, without explicitly constructing them. This is the **probabilistic method**, built on two key ideas.

## The Two Core Principles

> **The possibility principle.** Let $A$ be the event that a randomly chosen object in a collection has a certain property. If $P(A) > 0$, then there exists an object with the property.

> **The good score principle.** Let $X$ be the score of a randomly chosen object. If $E(X) \geq c$, then there is an object with a score of at least $c$.

**Why the possibility principle holds:** by contrapositive — if no object has the property, then $P(A) = 0$.

**Why the good score principle holds:** by contrapositive — if all objects score below $c$, then a weighted average (which is what $E(X)$ is) of numbers below $c$ must itself be below $c$.

**Key limitation:** the probabilistic method proves _existence_ — it gives no method for explicitly finding the object.

## Example 4.9.1 — Overlapping Committees

**Setup:** 100 people assigned to 15 committees of size 20, with each person serving on exactly 3 committees. Show there exist 2 committees sharing at least 3 people.

**Why not brute force?** One would need to enumerate every possible committee assignment and check every pair of committees — infeasible directly.

**Solution via probabilistic method:** choose two committees at random and let $X$ = number of people on both. Write:

$$
X = I_1 + I_2 + \cdots + I_{100},
$$

where $I_j = 1$ if person $j$ is on both chosen committees. By symmetry, all indicators have the same expectation, so $E(X) = 100, E(I_1)$.

**Finding $E(I_1)$:** by the fundamental bridge, $E(I_1)$ is the probability that a specific person (Bob) is on both randomly chosen committees $A$ and $B$. Think of Bob's 3 committees as 3 "tagged" elk among a population of 15; choosing $A$ and $B$ is sampling 2 elk without replacement. Using the $\text{HGeom}(3,12,2)$ PMF, the probability both sampled committees are "tagged" (contain Bob) is:

$$
\frac{\binom{3}{2}\binom{12}{0}}{\binom{15}{2}} = \frac{1}{35}.
$$

Therefore:

$$
E(X) = 100 \cdot \frac{1}{35} = \frac{20}{7}.
$$

**Applying the good score principle:** $20/7 \approx 2.857$ is just short of the target of 3. But since the overlap between two committees must be an **integer**, an average overlap of at least $20/7$ implies there exists a pair with overlap **at least 3** (since $20/7 > 2$, some pair must have overlap $\geq 3$). $\blacksquare$

## 4.9.1 Communicating Over a Noisy Channel

A major application of the probabilistic method: **information theory** — the study of reliable communication over noisy channels (e.g., a phone call where you might be misheard).

### Setup: Codes and Rates

> **Definition 4.9.2 (Codes and rates).** Given positive integers $k, n$, a **code** is a function $c$ assigning each message $x \in {0,1}^k$ a **codeword** $c(x) \in {0,1}^n$. The **rate** of the code is $k/n$ (input bits per output bit). A decoder attempts to recover $x$ from a possibly corrupted version of $c(x)$.

**Example — repetition code:** repeat $x$ some odd number $m$ of times; decode each bit by majority vote. This can make the failure probability small, but requires large $m$, giving a very low rate $1/m$.

### Shannon's Remarkable Result

Claude Shannon showed that even over a very noisy channel, there exist codes achieving **reliable communication at a rate bounded away from 0**, regardless of how small we require the failure probability to be. His proof technique was itself remarkable: he analyzed the performance of a **completely random code** — averaging over all possible random codes and showing the average performance was close to ideal.

_(The text includes a quote from Richard Hamming describing Shannon's habit of "attacking back" in chess as reflective of the boldness behind this proof technique — averaging over all random codes to find that the average is close to ideal.)_

### Key Definitions

> **Definition 4.9.3 (Hamming distance).** For binary vectors $v, w$ of the same length, the Hamming distance is the number of positions where they differ:
> $$
> d(v,w) = \sum_i |v_i - w_i|
> $$

> **Definition 4.9.4 (Binary entropy function).** For $0 < p < 1$:
> $$
> H(p) = -p\log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2(1-p)
> $$
> with $H(0) = H(1) = 0$.

**Interpretation:** $H(p)$ measures the information gained from observing a $\text{Bern}(p)$ r.v. $H(1/2) = 1$: a fair coin flip gives 1 bit of information. $H(1) = 0$: a coin that always lands Heads gives no information (the outcome is already known).

**Channel intuition:** for a channel flipping each bit independently with probability $p$, smaller $p$ is not always "better" in the naive sense that matters here — $p = 1/2$ is actually the **worst case** (a "useless channel," since the output becomes independent of the input). Analogy: a movie review from someone who always disagrees with you is more informative than one who agrees with you exactly half the time.

### Shannon's Theorem

> **Theorem 4.9.5 (Shannon).** Consider a channel flipping each transmitted bit independently with probability $p$, $0 < p < 1/2$. For any $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a code with rate at least $1 - H(p) - \varepsilon$ that can be decoded with probability of error less than $\varepsilon$.

**Proof sketch.**

1. **Setup:** Assume $1 - H(p) - \varepsilon > 0$. Choose large $n$, set $k = \lceil n(1-H(p)-\varepsilon)\rceil + 1$, and pick $p' \in (p, 1/2)$ with $|H(p') - H(p)| < \varepsilon/2$ (possible since $H$ is continuous).
    
2. **Random code construction:** For each $x \in {0,1}^k$, choose $C(x)$ to be a uniformly random vector in ${0,1}^n$ (independently across $x$). The rate $k/n$ exceeds $1 - H(p) - \varepsilon$ by construction.
    
3. **Decoding rule:** Given received message $Y$, decode to the unique $z$ satisfying $d(C(z), Y) \leq np'$, if such a unique $z$ exists; otherwise declare failure.
    
4. **Two failure modes to bound:**
    

- **(a)** $d(C(x), Y) > np'$ (the true codeword is "too far" from what was received).
- **(b)** Some impostor $z \neq x$ satisfies $d(C(z), Y) \leq np'$.

**Bounding (a):** $d(C(x),Y) = B_1 + \cdots + B_n \sim \text{Bin}(n,p)$, where $B_i$ indicates the $i$th bit being flipped. By the Law of Large Numbers (Chapter 10), $d(C(x),Y)/n \to p$, so it becomes very unlikely to exceed $p' > p$:
$$
P(d(C(x),Y) > np') \to 0 \text{ as } n\to\infty,
$$
so $n$ can be chosen large enough that this probability is $< \varepsilon/4$.

**Bounding (b):** For $z \neq x$, $d(C(z),Y) \sim \text{Bin}(n, 1/2)$ (since $C(z)$'s bits are i.i.d. $\text{Bern}(1/2)$, independent of $Y$). By **Boole's inequality**:
$$
P(d(C(z),Y) \leq np' \text{ for some } z\neq x) \leq (2^k - 1) P(B \leq np'), \quad B \sim \text{Bin}(n,1/2).
$$
Using standard bounding techniques (bounding a sum of $m$ terms by $m$ times the largest term, and bounding binomial coefficients), this reduces to an expression of order $2^{-n/2} \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$ — so $n$ can be chosen to make this probability $< \varepsilon/4$ as well.

5. **Combining:** for a random code $C$ and fixed $x$, $P(F(C,x)) < \varepsilon/2$, where $F(c,x)$ denotes decoding failure using code $c$ on input $x$.
    
6. **From "works for fixed $x$" to "works for all $x$":** Let $X$ be a uniformly random message, independent of $C$. By the Law of Total Probability (LOTP):
$$
P(F(C,X)) = \sum_x P(F(C,x)) P(X=x) < \varepsilon/2.
$$
Conditioning on $C$ instead:
$$
\sum_c P(F(c,X))P(C=c) = P(F(C,X)) < \varepsilon/2,
$$
so **there exists a specific code $c$** with $P(F(c,X)) < \varepsilon/2$ — i.e., averaged over a random input, $c$ fails with probability less than $\varepsilon/2$.
    
7. **Expurgation — guaranteeing performance for every $x$, not just on average:** Remove the worst 50% of inputs $x$ (those with highest failure probability under $c$). For the remaining $x$, $P(F(c,x)) < \varepsilon$ (otherwise more than half of all $x$ would have failure probability more than double the average — related to **Markov's inequality**, Chapter 10). Relabeling the surviving $2^{k-1}$ messages with vectors in ${0,1}^{k-1}$ yields a new code $c': {0,1}^{k-1} \to {0,1}^n$ with:
    

- Rate $(k-1)/n \geq 1 - H(p) - \varepsilon$,
- Failure probability $< \varepsilon$ for **every** input message. $\blacksquare$

### The Channel Capacity

There is also a **converse** to Shannon's theorem: requiring a rate of at least $1 - H(p) + \varepsilon$ makes it _impossible_ to drive the error probability to 0. This is why $1 - H(p)$ is called the **capacity** of the channel.

Shannon extended these results to much more general channels. These are **existence** results — they establish theoretical limits without specifying which codes to use. Subsequent decades of research have focused on constructing practical codes that approach the Shannon bound with efficient encoding/decoding.
