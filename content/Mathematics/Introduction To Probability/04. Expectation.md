# 4.1 Definition of Expectation

The probability distribution of a random variable ($r.v.$) provides full information regarding the probability of falling into any set (e.g., exceeding $1000$, equaling $5$, or lying in the interval $[0, 7]$). Because managing full distributions can be unwieldy, the distribution is frequently summarized by a single number representing its "average" value: the **mean** or **expected value**.

- **Mean (Expected Value):** The most common measure of the center of a random variable's distribution.
    
- **Variability:** Measures such as variance and standard deviation quantify how "spread out" a distribution is. Both are formally defined in terms of expected values.

### Foundations: Arithmetic vs. Weighted Mean

Expectation generalizes the weighted mean of a collection of numbers by utilizing probabilities as weights.

- **Arithmetic Mean:** For a list of numbers $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$, the arithmetic mean is:
$$\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^n x_j$$
- **Weighted Mean:** Given non-negative weights $p_1, p_2, \dots, p_n$ such that $\sum_{j=1}^n p_j = 1$:
$$\text{weighted-mean}(x) = \sum_{j=1}^n x_j p_j$$
    _(The unweighted arithmetic mean is the special case where $p_j = \frac{1}{n}$ for all $j$.)_


### Definition: Expectation of a Discrete Random Variable

The **expected value** (also known as the **expectation** or **mean**) of a discrete random variable $X$ with distinct possible values $x_1, x_2, \dots$ is defined as:
$$E(X) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty x_j P(X = x_j)$$
- If the support of $X$ is finite, the infinite series is replaced by a finite sum.
    
- Summing directly over the support of $X$:
$$E(X) = \sum_{x} x P(X = x)$$
    _(Values $x$ outside the support contribute $0$ since $x P(X = x) = 0$.)_

##### Condition for Existence
$E(X)$ is undefined if the absolute series diverges:
$$\sum_{j=1}^\infty \vert{}x_j\vert{} P(X = x_j) = \infty$$
If absolute convergence does not hold, the series either diverges or evaluates to different values depending on the order in which the outcomes $x_j$ are listed.

### Illustrative Examples & Interpretations

##### 1. Fair Six-Sided Die
Let $X$ denote the outcome of rolling a fair six-sided die, where $X \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$ with equal probability $\frac{1}{6}$:

  

$$E(X) = \frac{1}{6}(1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6) = \frac{21}{6} = 3.5$$

- **Non-Attainment:** A random variable may never equal its mean ($X$ can never take the value $3.5$). Similarly, an average of $1.8$ children per household in a demographic region does not imply any single household has $1.8$ children.

##### 2. Bernoulli Random Variable
Let $X \sim \text{Bern}(p)$ and $q = 1 - p$, where $X \in \{0, 1\}$:
$$E(X) = 1 \cdot p + 0 \cdot q = p$$
- **Physical Interpretation (Center of Mass):** $E(X) = p$ represents the compromise between $0$ and $1$. For a seesaw with pebble masses $q$ placed at position $0$ and $p$ placed at position $1$, the fulcrum must be placed at $p$ to balance.
    
- **Frequentist Interpretation:** Over a large sequence of independent Bernoulli trials with $1$ representing success and $0$ representing failure, the proportion of successes approaches $p$. The arithmetic average of a collection of $0$s and $1$s is precisely the proportion of $1$s.

##### 3. Empirical Simulation Interpretation
Let $X$ take values $a_1, a_2, a_3$ with probabilities $p_1, p_2, p_3$. In a simulation of $n$ independent draws (for large $n$), outcomes appear approximately $p_1 n$, $p_2 n$, and $p_3 n$ times, respectively.

The arithmetic mean of these simulation draws approximates:
$$\frac{(p_1 n) a_1 + (p_2 n) a_2 + (p_3 n) a_3}{n} = p_1 a_1 + p_2 a_2 + p_3 a_3 = E(X)$$

### Distributional Properties

##### Proposition 4.1.2
If $X$ and $Y$ are discrete random variables with identical distributions, then:
$$E(X) = E(Y)$$
_(provided the expectation exists)._
**Proof:** The definition of expectation depends solely on the probability mass function (PMF) of the random variable.

##### Falsehood of the Converse
The converse of Proposition 4.1.2 does **not** hold. The expected value is a single-number summary representing the center of mass; it does not fully specify the distribution, nor does it determine the spread or the probability that the random variable is positive.

### Critical Operational Warnings & Notation

##### Warning 4.1.3: Replacing a Random Variable by Its Expectation
$E(X)$ is a deterministic constant (a number), whereas $X$ is a function (a random variable).

- Replacing $X$ by $E(X)$ without justification is invalid both mathematically and statistically because it completely disregards the variability of $X$.
    
- Replacement is only valid in the degenerate case where $X$ is a constant.

##### Notation 4.1.4: Common Shorthand
Expectation expressions are commonly abbreviated without outer parentheses:

- $EX \equiv E(X)$
    
- $EX^2 \equiv E(X^2)$
    
- $EX^n \equiv E(X^n)$

##### Warning 4.1.5: Order of Operations
Unless explicit parentheses indicate otherwise, powers and transformations are applied **prior** to evaluating the expectation:

- $EX^2$ signifies the expectation of the squared variable $X^2$, i.e., $E(X^2)$, which is distinct from $(EX)^2$.
    
- $E(X - 1)^4$ signifies $E\left((X - 1)^4\right)$, not $(E(X - 1))^4$.

# 4.2 Linearity of Expectation

Linearity is the most fundamental operational property of expected values: the expectation of a sum of random variables equals the sum of their individual expectations, regardless of whether the variables are independent or dependent.

### Theorem 4.2.1: Linearity of Expectation

For any random variables $X$ and $Y$, and any real constant $c$:
$$
\begin{align}
E(X + Y) & = E(X) + E(Y) \\
E(cX) & = cE(X)
\end{align}
$$
- **Homogeneity:** Constant factors factor out directly from an expectation ($E(cX) = cE(X)$).
    
- **Independence Not Required:** The relation $E(X + Y) = E(X) + E(Y)$ holds universally, even under strong dependence between $X$ and $Y$.
    - _Extreme Dependence Case:_ If $X = Y$ everywhere, then $X + Y = 2X$. By linearity:
$$E(X + Y) = E(2X) = 2E(X) = E(X) + E(Y)$$
- **Domain of Validity:** While valid for all types of random variables, the formal proof below focuses on discrete random variables.

##### Mathematical Proof: Grouped vs. Ungrouped Averages
The theoretical proof rests on the arithmetic equivalence between calculating an average in an **ungrouped** manner versus a **grouped** manner.

##### Arithmetic Analogy
Given a data list $(1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 3, 3, 5)$:

- **Ungrouped calculation (equal weights $\frac{1}{8}$ per item):**
$$\frac{1}{8}(1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 3 + 3 + 5) = 2$$
- **Grouped calculation (weights corresponding to relative frequencies):**
$$\frac{5}{8} \cdot 1 + \frac{2}{8} \cdot 3 + \frac{1}{8} \cdot 5 = 2$$

##### Formal Application to Random Variables
A random variable $X$ maps sample space outcomes $s \in S$ to values in $\mathbb{R}$.

- **Grouped Definition (Standard Expectation):**
    Groups outcomes assigning the same value $x$ into "super-pebbles" with probability mass $P(X = x)$:
$$E(X) = \sum_{x} x P(X = x)$$
    _Limitation for proofs:_ When combining two random variables $X$ and $Y$, their partition of the sample space differs, yielding different groupings and weights ($P(X = x)$ vs. $P(Y = y)$).

![](Pasted%20image%2020260912134316.png)
- **Ungrouped Definition (Sample Space Level):**
    Evaluates the weighted sum over the elemental outcomes $s$ directly, where each outcome has weight $P(\{s\})$:
$$
\begin{align}
E(X) & = \sum_{s} X(s) P(\{s\}) \\
E(Y) & = \sum_{s} Y(s) P(\{s\})
\end{align}
$$
##### Derivation
Because both $X$ and $Y$ share the exact same elemental weights $P(\{s\})$ on the underlying sample space:
$$E(X) + E(Y) = \sum_{s} X(s) P(\{s\}) + \sum_{s} Y(s) P(\{s\}) = \sum_{s} (X + Y)(s) P(\{s\}) = E(X + Y)$$
### Simulation Intuition & The Law of Large Numbers

Consider performing $n$ independent repetitions of an experiment (where $n$ is very large) and recording the realized values:

| **Repetition** | **Realized X** | **Realized Y** | **Realized X+Y** |
| -------------- | -------------- | -------------- | ---------------- |
| **1**          | $3$            | $4$            | $7$              |
| **2**          | $2$            | $2$            | $4$              |
| **3**          | $6$            | $8$            | $14$             |
| **4**          | $10$           | $23$           | $33$             |
| **5**          | $1$            | $-3$           | $-2$             |
| **6**          | $1$            | $0$            | $1$              |
| **7**          | $5$            | $9$            | $14$             |
| **8**          | $4$            | $1$            | $5$              |
| $\vdots$       | $\vdots$       | $\vdots$       | $\vdots$         |
Summing the columns yields an algebraic identity:
$$\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i + \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n y_i = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i + y_i)$$
- By the **Law of Large Numbers**, as $n \to \infty$:
$$\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i \to E(X), \quad \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n y_i \to E(Y), \quad \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i + y_i) \to E(X + Y)$$
- **Invariance to Dependence:** Shuffling or re-pairing elements in the $Y$ column alters the joint dependency pattern between $X$ and $Y$ (e.g., positive correlation), but leaves individual column sums and means unchanged. Hence, linearity holds unconditionally.

### Applications and Distribution Expectations

##### Example 4.2.2: Binomial Distribution Expectation
Let $X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$.

- **Method 1: Direct Definition and Algebraic Manipulation**
$$E(X) = \sum_{k=0}^n k P(X = k) = \sum_{k=0}^n k \binom{n}{k} p^k q^{n-k}$$
    Using the combinatorial identity $k \binom{n}{k} = n \binom{n-1}{k-1}$:
$$\sum_{k=1}^n n \binom{n-1}{k-1} p^k q^{n-k} = np \sum_{k=1}^n \binom{n-1}{k-1} p^{k-1} q^{n-k}$$
    Substituting the index $j = k - 1$:
$$E(X) = np \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \binom{n-1}{j} p^j q^{n-1-j} = np(1) = np$$
- **Method 2: Indicator Variables and Linearity**
     Represent $X$ as a sum of $n$ independent Bernoulli trials:
$$X = I_1 + I_2 + \dots + I_n$$
    where each $I_j \sim \text{Bern}(p)$, so $E(I_j) = 1 \cdot p + 0 \cdot q = p$. By linearity:
$$E(X) = \sum_{j=1}^n E(I_j) = \sum_{j=1}^n p = np$$

##### Example 4.2.3: Hypergeometric Distribution Expectation
Let $X \sim \text{HGeom}(w, b, n)$, denoting the number of white balls drawn in a sample of size $n$ without replacement from an urn containing $w$ white balls and $b$ black balls.

- Decompose $X$ into Bernoulli indicator variables:
$$X = I_1 + I_2 + \dots + I_n$$
    where $I_j = 1$ if the $j$-th drawn ball is white, and $0$ otherwise.
- By unconditional symmetry, the $j$-th draw is equally likely to be any ball in the urn:
$$I_j \sim \text{Bern}(p), \quad \text{where } p = \frac{w}{w + b} \implies E(I_j) = \frac{w}{w + b}$$
- Even though the draws are **dependent** (sampling without replacement lowers the conditional probability of subsequent draws), linearity of expectation remains valid:
$$E(X) = \sum_{j=1}^n E(I_j) = n \left( \frac{w}{w + b} \right) = \frac{nw}{w + b}$$

### Proposition 4.2.4: Monotonicity of Expectation

Let $X$ and $Y$ be random variables such that $X \geq Y$ with probability $1$. Then:
$$E(X) \geq E(Y)$$
with equality holding if and only if $X = Y$ with probability $1$.
##### Proof (Discrete Case)
1. Define the non-negative random variable $Z = X - Y \geq 0$ (with probability $1$).
2. The expectation $E(Z)$ is a sum of non-negative terms:
$$E(Z) = \sum_z z P(Z = z) \geq 0$$
3. Applying linearity of expectation:
$$E(X) - E(Y) = E(X - Y) = E(Z) \geq 0 \implies E(X) \geq E(Y)$$
4. If $E(X) = E(Y)$, then $E(Z) = 0$. Since all terms in the summation defining $E(Z)$ are non-negative, any strictly positive term $z > 0$ with $P(Z = z) > 0$ would force $E(Z) > 0$. Thus:
$$P(Z = 0) = 1 \implies P(X = Y) = 1$$
