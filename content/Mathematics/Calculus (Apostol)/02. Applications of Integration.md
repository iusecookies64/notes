## 2.2 Area of A Region b/w Two Graphs Expressed as an Integral

### Theorem 2.1:
Assume $f$ and $g$ are integrable and satisfy $f \leq g$ on $[a,b]$. Then the region $S$ between their graphs is measurable and its area is given by the integral
$$a(S) = \int_a^b [g(x) - f(x)] \, dx$$

#### Proof:
First assume that $0 \leq f \leq g$ on $[a,b]$, then we have the graph as

> [!NOTE] Figure/Diagram Placeholder
> **Graph of Region Between Two Non-negative Functions:** A 2D Cartesian coordinate plot showing an interval $[a,b]$ on the x-axis. Two continuous curves are displayed: the upper curve labeled $g(x)$ and the lower curve labeled $f(x)$, satisfying $0 \leq f(x) \leq g(x)$ for all $x \in [a,b]$. Vertical dashed lines mark the boundaries at $x = a$ and $x = b$. The region between the two curves is shaded with vertical/diagonal hatching. The region beneath $f(x)$ down to the x-axis remains unshaded.

Consider the following two ordinate sets
$$F = \{(x,y) \mid a \leq x \leq b, \, 0 \leq y \leq f\}$$
$$G = \{(x,y) \mid a \leq x \leq b, \, 0 \leq y \leq g\}$$

The area b/w the graphs of $f$ and $g$ is given by $G-F$ and hence by axiom 3 of the area we have that $G-F$ is measurable and
$$a(G-F) = a(G) - a(F)$$
$$= \int_a^b g(x) \, dx - \int_a^b f(x) \, dx$$
$$= \int_a^b (g(x) - f(x)) \, dx$$

$\Rightarrow$ When we have $f(x) \leq g(x)$ and no lower limit condition on non-negativity i.e they can be wherever on the graph on $[a,b]$.

$\Rightarrow$ In this case also we can find some real $c$ such that
$$0 \leq f(x) + c \leq g(x) + c$$

$\Rightarrow$ There always exists such '$c$' as the functions $f$ and $g$ are bounded.

$\Rightarrow$ Let $T$ be the area b/w $f(x)+c$ and $g(x)+c$.
$$\Rightarrow a(T) = \int_a^b (g(x) + c - (f(x)+c)) \, dx$$
$$= \int_a^b [g(x) - f(x)] \, dx$$

$\Rightarrow$ Now, if $S$ is the area b/w $g(x)$ & $f(x)$, then $S$ is congruent to $T$
$$\Rightarrow a(S) = a(T) = \int_a^b [g(x) - f(x)] \, dx$$
$\Rightarrow$ Hence, proved.

---

**Note:** If in the interval $[a,b]$ we have that $f \leq g$ sometimes and $g \leq f$ other times, then we can break the interval in such a way that when $f \leq g$ we take $\int [g(x)-f(x)]$ and when $g \leq f$, then we take $\int [f(x)-g(x)]$ and since each of these are disjoint we can add these areas to get the final area.

$\Rightarrow$ In this case when $f \leq g$ or $g \leq f$ on some finite subintervals of $[a,b]$, the area b/w the graphs becomes
$$a(S) = \int_a^b |g(x) - f(x)| \, dx$$

---

### Example:
Compute the area b/w the graphs of $f(x) = x$ & $g(x) = \frac{x^3}{4}$ on $[-1,2]$

**Solution:**
$f(x)$ and $g(x)$ meet at 3 points $x = -2, 0, 2$, from $[-2,0]$ $g(x) \geq f(x)$ & from $[0,2]$ we have $g(x) \leq f(x)$, hence we get the area b/w $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ as
$$a(S) = \int_{-1}^0 \left(\frac{x^3}{4} - x\right) dx + \int_0^2 \left(x - \frac{x^3}{4}\right) dx$$
$$= \left. \left( \frac{x^4}{16} - \frac{x^2}{2} \right) \right|_{-1}^0 + \left. \left( \frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{x^4}{16} \right) \right|_0^2$$
$$= -\left( \frac{1}{16} - \frac{1}{2} \right) + \frac{4}{2} - \frac{16}{16}$$
$$= 1 + \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{16} = \frac{16+8-1}{16}$$
$$= \frac{23}{16}$$

### Example:
Prove that the area of a circle of radius $r$ is $r^2$ times the area of a unit circle.
$$\Rightarrow A(r) = r^2 A(1)$$

**Proof:**
We will consider circles with center at the origin, the upper circle then has the equation $y(x) = \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}$, and the lower half of the circle has the equation $f(x) = -\sqrt{r^2 - x^2}$ on the interval $[-r, r]$.
$$\Rightarrow A(r) = \int_{-r}^r \left(\sqrt{r^2 - x^2} - \left(-\sqrt{r^2 - x^2}\right)\right) dx$$
$$= 2 \int_{-r}^r \sqrt{r^2 - x^2} \, dx = 2r \int_{-1}^1 \sqrt{r^2 - (rx)^2} \, dx$$
$$= 2r^2 \int_{-1}^1 \sqrt{1 - x^2} \, dx.$$

Now,
$$A(1) = \int_{-1}^1 \left(\sqrt{1 - x^2} - \left(-\sqrt{1 - x^2}\right)\right) dx$$
$$= 2 \int_{-1}^1 \sqrt{1 - x^2} \, dx$$

$$\Rightarrow A(r) = r^2 A(1)$$

**Note:** The area of a unit circle is defined to be the number "$\pi$". Hence making the area of a circle of radius '$r$' = $\pi r^2$.

### Area of Ordinate Set Under Similarity Transformation

Let $S$ be a set of points in a plane. If we create a new set by multiplying each coordinates of each point by $k$ ($k > 0$) then the new set denoted by $kS$ is said to be similar to $S$.

$\Rightarrow$ The process through which we convert $S$ to $kS$ is called Similarity Transformation.

$\Rightarrow$ If $k > 1$ then the transformation is called stretching.

$\Rightarrow$ If $0 < k < 1$, then the transformation is called shrinking or contraction.

#### Example:
Prove that the area of $kS$ is $k^2$ times area of $S$ where $S$ is the ordinate set of a non-negative function which is integrable on $[a,b]$.

**Proof:**
Let $f(x)$ be the function whose ordinate set is $S$.
$$\Rightarrow a(S) = \int_a^b f(x) \, dx.$$

$\Rightarrow$ Now consider $g(x)$ be the function of the transformed ordinate set $kS$.
$$\Rightarrow \text{If } (x,y) \in g, \text{ then } \left(\frac{x}{k}, \frac{y}{k}\right) \in f$$
$$\Rightarrow \frac{y}{k} = f\left(\frac{x}{k}\right) \Rightarrow y = k f\left(\frac{x}{k}\right)$$
$$\Rightarrow g(x) = k f\left(\frac{x}{k}\right)$$
$$\Rightarrow \int_{ka}^{kb} g(x) \, dx = k^2 \int_a^b f(x) \, dx$$
$$\Rightarrow a(kS) = k^2 a(S)$$

**Note:** More Generally if we multiply the $x$ coordinates with $n$ and $y$'s with $m$, then with same method we can prove that
$$a(kS) = mn \, a(S)$$

> [!WARNING] Mathematical Error in Notes
> The note states $a(kS) = mn \, a(S)$. However, when the $x$-coordinates are scaled by $n$ and the $y$-coordinates are scaled by $m$, the transformation is an affine scaling rather than a similarity transformation by a single scalar factor $k$. Denoting the transformed set by $S'$, the correct relation is $a(S') = mn \, a(S)$.

### Theorem 2.2:
For $a > 0, b > 0$ and $n$ a positive integer, we have
$$\int_a^b x^{1/n} \, dx = \frac{b^{1 + 1/n} - a^{1 + 1/n}}{1 + 1/n}$$

#### Proof:
Firstly, we will find the integral of $\int_0^b x^{1/n} \, dx$, for that look at the graph of $x^{1/n}$ in $[0,b]$

> [!NOTE] Figure/Diagram Placeholder
> **Graph of $y = x^{1/n}$ and Bounding Box:** Cartesian axes showing the curve $y = x^{1/n}$ passing from the origin $(0,0)$ to the point $(b, b^{1/n})$. A bounding rectangle of width $b$ and height $b^{1/n}$ is indicated with dashed lines from $(0, b^{1/n})$ to $(b, b^{1/n})$ and down to $(b, 0)$. The region between the curve and the y-axis (representing the area under $g(y) = y^n$) is shaded with diagonal hatching.

The shaded region is the area under the curve of the graph $g(y) = y^n$ with base $b^{1/n}$.
$$\Rightarrow \int_0^{b^{1/n}} y^n \, dy = \left. \frac{y^{n+1}}{n+1} \right|_0^{b^{1/n}} = \frac{b^{\frac{n+1}{n}}}{n+1}$$

$\Rightarrow$ Also the area of the whole rectangle is $b \cdot b^{1/n} = b^{1 + 1/n}$

$\Rightarrow$ If $Q$ is the ordinate set of $x^{1/n}$ then, we have
$$a(Q) = \int_0^b x^{1/n} \, dx$$

$\Rightarrow$ But also we see that,
$$a(Q) + a(G) = b^{1 + 1/n}$$
$$\Rightarrow a(Q) = b^{1 + 1/n} - \frac{b^{\frac{n+1}{n}}}{n+1} = b^{1 + 1/n} \left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)$$

$$\Rightarrow \int_0^b x^{1/n} \, dx = \frac{b^{1 + 1/n}}{1 + \frac{1}{n}}$$

$$\Rightarrow \int_a^b x^{1/n} \, dx = \int_0^b x^{1/n} \, dx - \int_0^a x^{1/n} \, dx = \frac{b^{1 + 1/n} - a^{1 + 1/n}}{1 + 1/n}$$

### 18
Let $n \geq 3$ be a positive integer. Let $A_n^{in}$ and $A_n^{out}$ denote the areas of regular $n$-sided polygons inscribed and circumscribed about a circular disk of radius $r > 0$. Prove that

**sol:**

## 2.4 The Trigonometric Functions

$\rightarrow$ First we will provide the axiomatic defn of the sine and cosine functions and then we will explain why these come using geometry.

$\rightarrow$ **Fundamental Properties of the Sine and Cosine**

(i) **Domain of Definition.** The sine and cosine functions are defined everywhere on the real line.

(ii) **Special Values:** We have
$$\cos 0 = \sin \frac{\pi}{2} = 1, \quad \cos \pi = -1$$

(iii) **Cosine of a difference,** for all $x$ and $y$, we have
$$\cos(x-y) = \cos x \cos y + \sin x \sin y$$

(iv) **Fundamental Inequalities.** For $0 < x < \frac{\pi}{2}$, we have
$$0 < \cos x < \frac{\sin x}{x} < \frac{1}{\cos x}$$

---

### Theorem 2.3:
If two functions $\sin$ and $\cos$ satisfy properties 1 through 4, then they also satisfy the following properties:

**(a) Pythagorean Identity:**
$$\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$$
**Proof:** If we put $y = x$ in property (iii) we get this.

**(b) Special Values:**
$$\sin 0 = \cos \frac{\pi}{2} = \sin \pi = 0$$
**Proof:** If I put $x = y = 0$ in prop (iii) we get
$$\cos(0) = \cos^2(0) + \sin^2(0)$$
$$\Rightarrow \sin^2 0 = 0 \Rightarrow \sin(0) = 0$$

Again putting $x = y = \pi$ we get
$$\cos(0) = \cos^2 \pi + \sin^2 \pi$$
$$\Rightarrow \sin \pi = 0$$

**(c) Even and Odd Properties:**
The cosine is an even function and sine is odd.
**Proof:** Putting $y = 0$ in prop (iii)
$$\Rightarrow \cos(-x) = \cos(0)\cos(x) + \sin 0 \sin x$$
$$\Rightarrow \cos(-x) = \cos x, \quad \forall x \in \mathbb{R}.$$

$\Rightarrow$ For the next part we will use the following.
$$\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \cos \frac{\pi}{2} \cos x + \sin \frac{\pi}{2} \sin x$$
$$\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \sin x$$

$$\Rightarrow \sin(-x) = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + x\right) = \cos\left(\pi - \left(\frac{\pi}{2}-x\right)\right)$$
$$= \cos \pi \sin x + \sin \pi \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-x\right)$$
$$= -\sin x$$
$$\Rightarrow \sin(-x) = \sin x = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+x\right)$$

> [!WARNING] Mathematical Error in Notes
> In the final boxed equality above, the notes write $\sin(-x) = \sin x = \cos(\frac{\pi}{2}+x)$. The middle expression is missing a minus sign; since sine is an odd function, the correct relation is $\sin(-x) = -\sin x = \cos(\frac{\pi}{2}+x)$.

**(d) For all $x$, we have**
(a) $\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + x\right) = \cos x$
(b) $\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + x\right) = -\sin x$

**Proof:**
(b) we already proved as part of previous proof.
(a) In previous proof we already proved that
$$\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-x\right) = \sin x$$
$\Rightarrow$ Putting $x = \frac{\pi}{2}+x$
$$\Rightarrow \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+x\right) = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \left(\frac{\pi}{2}+x\right)\right) = \cos(-x)$$
$$\Rightarrow \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+x\right) = \cos x$$

**(e) Periodicity:**
For all $x$, we have
$$\sin(2\pi + x) = \sin x \quad \& \quad \cos(2\pi + x) = \cos x$$

**Proof:**
$$\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + \left(\frac{3\pi}{2}+x\right)\right) = \cos\left(\frac{3\pi}{2}+x\right) = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + (\pi+x)\right) = -\sin(\pi+x) = -\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + \frac{\pi}{2}+x\right) = -\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+x\right) = \sin x$$
$$\Rightarrow \sin(2\pi + x) = \sin x$$

$\Rightarrow$ Similarly we can also prove that
$$\cos(2\pi + x) = -\sin\left(\frac{3\pi}{2}+x\right) = -\cos(\pi+x) = \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+x\right) = \cos x$$
$$\Rightarrow \cos(2\pi + x) = \cos x$$

**(f) Addition Formulae:**
$$\cos(x+y) = \cos x \cos y - \sin x \sin y$$
$$\sin(x+y) = \sin x \cos y + \cos x \sin y$$

**Proof:**
$$\cos(x+y) = \cos(x - (-y))$$
$$= \cos x \cos(-y) + \sin x \sin(-y)$$
$$= \cos x \cos y - \sin x \sin y.$$

Similarly,
$$\sin(x+y) = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-(x+y)\right)$$
$$= \cos\left(\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-x\right) + y\right)$$
$$= \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-x\right)\cos y - \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-x\right)\sin y$$
$$= \sin x \cos y + \cos x \sin y.$$
$\Rightarrow$ Hence Proved.

> [!WARNING] Mathematical Error in Notes
> In the derivation of $\sin(x+y)$, the notes write $\frac{\pi}{2}-(x+y) = (\frac{\pi}{2}-x) + y$, which is algebraically incorrect (it should be $(\frac{\pi}{2}-x) - y$). Consequently, applying the cosine difference formula to $\cos((\frac{\pi}{2}-x) - y)$ gives $\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}-x)\cos y + \sin(\frac{\pi}{2}-x)\sin y = \sin x \cos y + \cos x \sin y$. The notes wrote a minus sign in the expansion but then changed it back to a plus sign in the final line.

**(g) Difference Formulas:**
For all $a$ and $b$, we have
$$\sin a - \sin b = 2 \sin \frac{a-b}{2} \cos \frac{a+b}{2}$$
$$\cos a - \cos b = -2 \sin \frac{a-b}{2} \sin \frac{a+b}{2}$$

**Proof:**
$$\frac{a-b}{2} + \frac{a+b}{2} = a$$
$$\frac{a-b}{2} - \frac{a+b}{2} = -b$$
$$\sin a - \sin b = \sin a + \sin(-b)$$
$$= \sin\left(\frac{a-b}{2} + \frac{a+b}{2}\right) + \sin\left(\left(\frac{a-b}{2}\right) - \left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)\right)$$
$$= \sin\left(\frac{a-b}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right) + \sin\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{a-b}{2}\right) + \sin\left(\frac{a-b}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right) - \sin\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{a-b}{2}\right)$$
$$= 2 \sin\left(\frac{a-b}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)$$

$\Rightarrow$ Similarly we can prove for $\cos a - \cos b$.

**(h) Monotonicity:**
In the interval $[0, \frac{\pi}{2}]$, the sine is strictly increasing and cos is strictly decreasing.

**Proof:**
assume that $0 \leq b < a < \frac{\pi}{2}$
$$\Rightarrow \frac{a-b}{2} \quad \& \quad \frac{a+b}{2} \in \left[0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$$
From property (iv) we see that cosine (or) sine of both $\frac{a-b}{2}$ & $\frac{a+b}{2}$ are $> 0$.
$$\Rightarrow \sin a - \sin b > 0 \quad \& \quad \cos a - \cos b < 0$$
$\Rightarrow$ Hence sine is increasing and cosine is decreasing.

## 2.5 Integration Formulas For The Sine and Cosine

$\Rightarrow$ The monotonicity combined with the periodic nature of sine and cosine make them integrable for every interval of real numbers.

### Theorem 2.4:
If $0 < a \leq \frac{\pi}{2}$ and $n \geq 1$, then we have
$$\frac{a}{n} \sum_{k=1}^n \cos \frac{ka}{n} < \sin a < \frac{a}{n} \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \cos \frac{ka}{n}$$

#### Proof:
First we will convert the sums $\sum_{k=1}^n \cos \frac{ka}{n} \, \& \, \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}$ to something more mangable.

$\Rightarrow$ For this consider the sum
$$2\sin \frac{x}{2} \sum_{k=1}^n \cos kx = \sum_{k=1}^n 2\sin \frac{x}{2} \cos kx$$
$$= \sum_{k=1}^n \left(\sin\left(k+\frac{1}{2}\right)x - \sin\left(k-\frac{1}{2}\right)x\right)$$

$\Rightarrow$ Above is a telescoping series with the sum equal to
$$= \sin\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)x - \sin \frac{x}{2}$$
$$\Rightarrow \sum_{k=1}^n \cos kx = \frac{\sin\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)x - \sin \frac{x}{2}}{2\sin \frac{x}{2}}$$

$\Rightarrow$ This is assuming that $\frac{x}{2} \neq m\pi$ for some $m \in \mathbb{Z} \Rightarrow x \neq 2m\pi$.

$\Rightarrow$ If we put $n-1$ in place of $n$ and add 1 on both sides we get
$$\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \cos kx = \frac{\sin\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)x + \sin \frac{x}{2}}{2\sin \frac{x}{2}}$$

$\Rightarrow$ Now, if we take $x = \frac{a}{n}$, the inequalities convert into more managable expression.

$\Rightarrow$ Note that taking $x = \frac{a}{n}$ is valid as $\frac{a}{n} \neq 2m\pi$.
$$\Rightarrow \frac{a}{n} \left(\frac{\sin\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{a}{n} - \sin \frac{a}{2n}}{2\sin \frac{a}{2n}}\right) < \sin a < \frac{a}{n} \left(\frac{\sin\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{a}{n} + \sin \frac{a}{2n}}{2\sin \frac{a}{2n}}\right)$$
$$\Rightarrow \sin\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{a}{n} - \sin \frac{a}{2n} < \frac{\sin \frac{a}{2n}}{\frac{a}{2n}} \sin a < \sin\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{a}{n} + \sin \frac{a}{2n}$$

$\Rightarrow$ To simplify we will take $\frac{a}{2n} = \theta$, so finally the inequality that we need to solve is
$$\sin(2n+1)\theta - \sin\theta < \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} \sin 2n\theta < \sin(2n-1)\theta + \sin\theta$$

$\Rightarrow$ **Left Hand Inequality Proof:**
$$\sin(2n+1)\theta = \sin 2n\theta \cos\theta + \cos 2n\theta \sin\theta$$
$$< \sin 2n\theta \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} + \sin\theta$$
$$\Rightarrow \sin(2n+1)\theta - \sin\theta < \sin 2n\theta \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}$$
$\Rightarrow$ For above we used the fact that
$$a \in \left(0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] \Rightarrow 2n\theta \in \left(0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$$
$$\Rightarrow \theta \in \left(0, \frac{\pi}{4n} < \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$$
$$\Rightarrow \text{Hence, } \cos\theta < \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} \quad \& \quad 0 \leq \cos 2n\theta \leq 1$$

$\Rightarrow$ **Right Hand Inequality Proof:**
$$\sin(2n-1)\theta + \sin\theta = \sin 2n\theta \cos\theta - \cos 2n\theta \sin\theta + \sin\theta$$
$$= \sin 2n\theta \cos\theta + \sin\theta(1 - \cos 2n\theta)$$
$$= \sin 2n\theta \cos\theta + \sin\theta (2\sin^2 n\theta)$$
$$= \sin 2n\theta \left(\cos\theta + \frac{\sin\theta \sin n\theta}{\cos n\theta}\right)$$
$$= \sin 2n\theta \left(\frac{\cos(n-1)\theta}{\cos n\theta}\right)$$

$\Rightarrow$ So, now we need to prove that
$$\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} < \frac{\cos(n-1)\theta}{\cos n\theta} \quad \text{to complete the proof.}$$
$$\cos n\theta = \cos(n-1)\theta \cos\theta - \sin(n-1)\theta \sin\theta$$
Now, $\sin(n-1)\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ both are $> 0$
$$\Rightarrow \cos n\theta < \cos(n-1)\theta \cos\theta$$
$$\Rightarrow \frac{1}{\cos\theta} < \frac{\cos(n-1)\theta}{\cos n\theta}$$
$$\Rightarrow \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} < \frac{1}{\cos\theta} < \frac{\cos(n-1)\theta}{\cos n\theta}$$
$\Rightarrow$ Hence Proved.

### Theorem 2.5:
If two functions sine and $\cos$ satisfy the 4 fundamental properties, then for every real '$a$' we have
$$\int_0^a \cos x \, dx = \sin a$$
$$\int_0^a \sin x \, dx = 1 - \cos a$$

#### Proof:
**When $0 < a \leq \frac{\pi}{2}$:**
$\Rightarrow$ Well in this case we have already proved that, for all $n \geq 1$
$$\frac{a}{n} \sum_{k=1}^n \cos \frac{ka}{n} < \sin a < \frac{a}{n} \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \cos \frac{ka}{n}$$
$\Rightarrow$ This simply proves that
$$\int_0^a \cos x \, dx = \sin a$$

**When $-\frac{\pi}{2} \leq a < 0$:**
$\Rightarrow$ In this case $-a \in (0, \frac{\pi}{2}]$, this means that
$$\Rightarrow I = \int_0^{-a} \cos x \, dx = \sin(-a) = -\sin a$$
$$\Rightarrow I = \int_0^{-a} \cos x \, dx = (-1) \int_0^a \cos(-x) \, dx = -\int_0^a \cos x \, dx$$
$\Rightarrow$ Hence, we have $\int_0^a \cos x \, dx = \sin a$ in this case also.

**When $\frac{\pi}{2} \leq a \leq \frac{3\pi}{2}$:**
$$\Rightarrow \int_0^a \cos x \, dx = \int_0^{\pi/2} \cos x \, dx + \int_{\pi/2}^a \cos x \, dx$$
$$= 1 + \int_{\frac{\pi}{2}-\pi}^{a-\pi} \cos(x+\pi) \, dx$$
$$= 1 + \int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{a-\pi} (\cos(x)\cos\pi - \sin x \sin\pi) \, dx$$
$$= 1 - \int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{a-\pi} \cos x \, dx$$
$$= 1 - \int_0^{a-\pi} \cos x \, dx + \int_0^{-\pi/2} \cos x \, dx$$
$$= 1 - \sin(a-\pi) - 1$$
$$= -(\sin a \cos\pi - \cos a \sin\pi)$$
$$= \sin a$$

$\Rightarrow$ Hence, we proved that $\int_0^a \cos x \, dx = \sin a$ is true for $-\frac{\pi}{2} \leq a \leq \frac{3\pi}{2}$.

$\Rightarrow$ This interval is of length $2\pi$, this means that $\int_0^a \cos x \, dx = \sin a$ is true for all $a \in \mathbb{R}$.

$\Rightarrow$ Now, we will prove this for $\sin$.
$$\Rightarrow \int_0^a \sin x \, dx = \int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{a-\frac{\pi}{2}} \sin\left(x+\frac{\pi}{2}\right) dx$$
$$= \int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{a-\frac{\pi}{2}} \cos x \, dx = \int_0^{a-\frac{\pi}{2}} \cos x \, dx - \int_0^{-\frac{\pi}{2}} \cos x \, dx$$
$$= \sin\left(a - \frac{\pi}{2}\right) - \sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)$$
$$= 1 - \cos a.$$

$$\Rightarrow \int_0^a \sin x \, dx = 1 - \cos a, \quad \forall a \in \mathbb{R}$$
$$\Rightarrow \int_0^a \cos x \, dx = \sin x, \quad \forall a \in \mathbb{R}$$

> [!WARNING] Mathematical Error in Notes
> In the boxed summary formula $\int_0^a \cos x \, dx = \sin x$, the right-hand side is erroneously written as $\sin x$ instead of $\sin a$. The dummy variable of integration is $x$ and the upper limit is $a$.

---

**Note:** In general we can write the integrals as follows.
$$\int_a^b \cos x \, dx = \left. \sin x \right|_a^b = \sin b - \sin a$$
$$\text{And}, \int_a^b \sin x \, dx = \left. (1 - \cos x) \right|_a^b = \left. (-\cos x) \right|_a^b = -(\cos b - \cos a)$$

**Note:** Integral of multiple angle of $\cos$ and $\sin$.
$$\int_a^b \cos cx \, dx = \frac{1}{c} \int_{ac}^{bc} \cos x \, dx = \frac{1}{c} (\sin cb - \sin ca)$$
$$\Rightarrow \text{Similarly, we have that } \int_a^b \sin cx \, dx = \frac{1}{c} \int_{ca}^{cb} \sin x \, dx = -\frac{(\cos cb - \cos ca)}{c}$$

---

### Example:
Find the integral of $\sin^2 x$ and $\cos^2 x$.

**solution:**
$$\int_a^b \sin^2 x \, dx = \int_a^b \frac{(1 - \cos 2x)}{2} \, dx$$
$$= \frac{1}{2}(b-a) - \frac{1}{2} \int_a^b \cos 2x \, dx$$
$$= \left(\frac{b-a}{2}\right) - \frac{(\sin 2b - \sin 2a)}{4}$$
Similarly, $\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x$ and the rest will be done in a similar manner.

## 2.6 A Geometric Description of The Sine And Cosine Functions

$\Rightarrow$ Consider a circle with center at origin and radius '$r$'.
$\Rightarrow$ The right most point on $x$-axis is $A(r,0)$.

> [!NOTE] Figure/Diagram Placeholder
> **Geometric Definition of Angle on a Circle:** A circle centered at origin $O$ with radius $r$. Point $A(r,0)$ lies on the positive x-axis. A point $P$ is placed on the circumference in the upper-left quadrant. Line segments $OA$ and $OP$ are drawn. An arc with a directional arrow shows counterclockwise rotation from line segment $OA$ to $OP$, labeled with angle $x$.

$\Rightarrow$ Let $P$ be any point on the circle then line segments $OA$ and $OP$ determine a geometrical configuration called an Angle.

$\Rightarrow$ When $P$ is above real line then we take the smaller region and if $P$ is below real line then we take the larger angle. It is represented by $\angle AOP$.

$\Rightarrow$ In other words $\angle AOP$ starts from $A$ and then moves in counterclockwise direction to reach $P$.

---

### Measure of Angle (Radians)

$\rightarrow$ The measure of $\angle AOP$ is the length of arc $A\text{ to }P$ on a circle of radius 1. This is radians measure.

$\rightarrow$ If the circle has radius '$r$' then to make sure that measure is dimensionless the angle is
$$\angle AOP = \frac{\text{Arc length } AP}{r}$$

### Radians in terms of Area:

$\Rightarrow$ Since we have not yet defined the arc length and its calculation, we will define it using area of sector $AOP$.
$$\angle AOP = \frac{2 \times \text{Area of } AOP}{r^2}$$

$\Rightarrow$ From school days I know that
$$\text{arc length} = \frac{\theta}{2\pi} \cdot 2\pi r = \theta r$$
$$\text{sector area} = \frac{\theta}{2\pi} \cdot \pi r^2 = \frac{\theta r^2}{2}$$
$$\Rightarrow \angle AOP = \theta = \frac{\text{arc length}}{r} = \frac{2 \cdot \text{area}}{r^2}$$

---

### Some Common Measures of Angle

> [!NOTE] Figure/Diagram Placeholder
> **Unit Circle with Quadrantal Points:** A unit circle centered at $O$. Dashed horizontal and vertical diameters divide the circle into four quadrants. The four axis intersection points are labeled: $A$ on the positive x-axis, $P_1$ on the positive y-axis, $P_2$ on the negative x-axis, and $P_3$ on the negative y-axis.

$\rightarrow$ Above is a unit circle and we will look at its different angles and their measures, because we know that area of unit circle is $\pi$.
$$\angle AOP_1 = 2 \times \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{2}$$
$$\angle AOP_2 = 2 \times \frac{\pi}{2} = \pi$$
$$\angle AOP_3 = 2 \times \frac{3\pi}{4} = \frac{3\pi}{2}$$
$$\Rightarrow \text{Angle of full circle} = 2\pi$$

### Geometric Definition of Cosine and Sine

Let $x$ be any real number such that $0 < x < 2\pi$. Let $P$ be a point on the below unit circle such that the area of the sector $AOP$ is $\frac{x}{2}$. Then we have, if $(a,b)$ denote the co-ordinates of $P$, then
$$\cos x = a \quad \& \quad \sin x = b.$$

> [!NOTE] Figure/Diagram Placeholder
> **Trigonometric Coordinates on the Unit Circle:** A unit circle centered at $O$. Point $A$ is on the positive x-axis and point $P(a,b)$ is on the circle in the first quadrant. Rays $OA$ and $OP$ subtend an angle labeled $x$, indicated by a curved arrow. The sector bounded by $OA$, $OP$, and arc $AP$ corresponds to area $x/2$.

$\Rightarrow$ This defines sine and cosine in the interval $(0, 2\pi)$, we extend this by defining periodicity of these functions
$$\sin(2\pi + x) = \sin x$$
$$\cos(2\pi + x) = \cos x.$$

---

### Geometric Interpretations of The 4 Fundamental Properties

#### Property 1:
With the extended defn we see that we can get any real $x$ to its equivalent $x'$ such that $x' \in (0, 2\pi)$.

#### Property 2:
The way in which we defined sine and cosine, it is very easy to see that
$$\sin \frac{\pi}{2} = \cos 0 = 1, \quad \& \quad \cos \pi = -1$$

#### Property 3:
This property can be proved using some geometry.

> [!NOTE] Figure/Diagram Placeholder
> **Geometric Setup for Cosine Difference Formula:** A unit circle with origin $O$. Two points are placed on the circle: $P(\cos x, \sin x)$ at angle $x$ and $Q(\cos y, \sin y)$ at angle $y$. Radial segments $OP$ and $OQ$ are drawn, with angle $\angle POQ = y - x$. Chord $QP$ is drawn. A right triangle $\Delta QBP$ is formed by dropping a perpendicular from $Q$ onto line $OP$ at foot $B$, showing base length $BP = 1 - \cos(y-x)$ and altitude $QB = \sin(y-x)$.

$\rightarrow$ From the above figure we have that the distance between $QP$ using distance formulae is
$$d^2 = (\cos x - \cos y)^2 + (\sin x - \sin y)^2$$

$\rightarrow$ If we look at $\Delta^{le} POQ$, the $\angle POQ = y - x$, hence length of
$$QB = \sin(y-x) \quad \text{and} \quad OB = \cos(y-x)$$
$$\Rightarrow \text{length } BP = 1 - \cos(y-x)$$
$$\Rightarrow d^2 = QB^2 + BP^2$$
$$= \sin^2(y-x) + (1 - \cos(y-x))^2$$

$\Rightarrow$ Equating both of these and solving for $\cos(y-x)$ proves property 3.

**Note:** Solving for $\cos(y-x)$ will require the use of Pythagorean Property for the $\sin \& \cos$.
The way we have defined sine and cosine we can easily see why pythagorean property holds.

#### Property 4:
$\Rightarrow$ Again we will use geometry and properties of area to prove this.

> [!NOTE] Figure/Diagram Placeholder
> **Geometric Proof of Fundamental Inequalities:** A circular sector $AOP$ of radius 1 and central angle $x$ centered at $O$, with $A$ on the horizontal axis. Perpendicular $PB$ is dropped from $P$ to the horizontal axis $OA$, giving triangle $\Delta PBO$ with base $\cos x$ and height $\sin x$. At $A$, a vertical tangent line meets the extension of ray $OP$ at point $Q$, giving triangle $\Delta QAO$ with base $OA = 1$ and height $AQ = h = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}$. The sector $AOP$ lies nested between $\Delta PBO$ and $\Delta QAO$.

$$\text{Area of } \Delta^{le} PBO = \frac{1}{2} \cos x \cdot \sin x$$
$$\text{Area of } \Delta^{le} QAO = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} \cdot 1$$
$$\text{Area of sector } AOP = \frac{x}{2}$$
$$\Rightarrow \frac{\cos x \cdot \sin x}{2} < \frac{x}{2} < \frac{\sin x}{2\cos x}$$
$$\Rightarrow \cos x < \frac{x}{\sin x} < \frac{1}{\cos x}$$
$$\Rightarrow \cos x < \frac{\sin x}{x} < \frac{1}{\cos x}$$

**Note:** Analytical treatment of these functions make no use of geometry, we will see this later.

### 10
Prove that if $A$ and $B$ are given real numbers, there exists $C$ and $\alpha$, with $C \geq 0$, such that
$$C \cos(x+\alpha) = A \sin x + B \cos x$$

**sol:**
If $A = 0$, then $C = B$ and $\alpha = 0$.
If $B = 0$, then $C = -A$ and $\alpha = \frac{\pi}{2}$.
If $A \neq 0$ and $B \neq 0$, then we have
$$A \sin x + B \cos x = B\left(\cos x + \frac{A}{B}\sin x\right)$$
$\Rightarrow$ Taking $\frac{A}{B} = \tan\beta$ and we get
$$A \sin x + B \cos x = \frac{B}{\cos\beta} \cos(x-\beta)$$
$$\Rightarrow C = \frac{B}{\cos\beta}, \quad \alpha = -\beta.$$
$\Rightarrow$ Hence, such $C \, \& \, \alpha$ always exist.

> [!WARNING] Mathematical Error in Notes
> The problem requires $C \geq 0$. In the notes' special cases, if $A > 0$ with $B = 0$, setting $C = -A$ produces a negative value of $C$. Similarly, for the general case, $C = B/\cos\beta$ can be negative if $B$ and $\cos\beta$ have opposite signs. The standard construction valid for all real $A$ and $B$ with $C \geq 0$ is $C = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2}$, $\cos\alpha = B/C$, and $\sin\alpha = -A/C$ (when $C > 0$).

### 11
Find all real $x$ such that

**sol:**
$$\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \cos^2 \frac{\pi}{4} - \sin^2 \frac{\pi}{4}$$
$$\Rightarrow \cos \frac{\pi}{4} = \sin \frac{\pi}{4}$$
$\Rightarrow$ Similarly, we can prove that
$$\cos\left(\pi + \frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \sin\left(\pi + \frac{\pi}{4}\right)$$

$\Rightarrow A(0)$ and $A(1)$ is true as shown above.
$\Rightarrow$ Assume $A(n)$ is also true
$$\Rightarrow \sin\left(n\pi + \frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \cos\left(n\pi + \frac{\pi}{4}\right)$$

$$A(n+1): \sin\left((n+1)\pi + \frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \sin\left(\pi + \left(n\pi + \frac{\pi}{4}\right)\right)$$

$$= -\sin\left(n\pi + \frac{\pi}{4}\right)$$

$$= \cos\pi \cos\left(n\pi + \frac{\pi}{4}\right) - \sin\pi \sin\left(n\pi + \frac{\pi}{4}\right)$$
$$= -\cos\left(n\pi + \frac{\pi}{4}\right)$$

$\Rightarrow$ Hence, $A(n+1)$ is also true, this proves that the assertion is true for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

**Note:** Induction was used to prove the above result. In a similar way we should have used induction to prove that the results shown in (1) are also true.

### 15
Prove (or) Disprove each of the following statements.

(a) For all $x \neq 0$, we have
$$\sin 2x \neq 2\sin x$$

**sol:**
for all $x = n\pi$, we have
$$\sin(2n\pi) = 2\sin(n\pi) = 0$$

(b) For every $x$, there is a $y$ such that $\cos(x+y) = \cos x + \cos y$

**sol:**
$$\cos x \cos y - \sin x \sin y = \cos x + \cos y$$
$$\Rightarrow \cos x \cos y - \cos x - \cos y = \sin x \sin y.$$
Let $A = \cos x$ & $B = \cos y$
$$\Rightarrow \sin x = \sqrt{1-A^2} \quad \& \quad \sin y = \sqrt{1-B^2}$$
$\Rightarrow$ Squaring on both sides we get:
$$A^2 + B^2 - 2A^2 B - 2AB^2 + 2AB = (1-A^2)(1-B^2) = 1 - A^2 - B^2 + A^2 B^2$$
$$\Rightarrow 2(A^2 + B^2 - A^2 B - AB^2 + AB) = 1$$
$$\Rightarrow B^2(1-A) + B(A - A^2) + \left(A^2 - \frac{1}{2}\right) = 0$$
$$\Rightarrow B = \frac{-A(1-A) \pm \sqrt{A^2(1-A)^2 - 4(1-A)\left(A^2 - \frac{1}{2}\right)}}{2(1-A)}$$
$$= -\frac{A}{2} \pm \frac{\sqrt{A^2(1-A) - 4\left(A^2 - \frac{1}{2}\right)}}{2\sqrt{1-A}}$$
$$= -\frac{A}{2} \pm \frac{\sqrt{A^2 - A^3 - 4A^2 + 2}}{2\sqrt{1-A}}$$
$$= -\frac{A}{2} \pm \frac{\sqrt{-A^3 - 3A^2 + 2}}{2\sqrt{1-A}}$$

Now, $A = \cos x \Rightarrow |A| \leq 1$.
If we put $A = \frac{3}{4}$, we get that

## 2.8 Polar Coordinates

$\rightarrow$ The cartesian coordinate system divides the 2D plane into rectangles and uses rectangular coordinates to locate any point.

$\rightarrow$ The polar coordinate system uses the line joining the point and origin.

> [!NOTE] Figure/Diagram Placeholder
> **Polar Coordinates Representation:** A 2D Cartesian plane showing the horizontal $x$-axis and vertical $y$-axis intersecting at the origin $O$. A point $P(x, y)$ in the first quadrant is connected to the origin $O$ by a straight line segment of length $r$. The counterclockwise angle measured from the positive $x$-axis to the segment $OP$ is labeled $\theta$.

$\rightarrow$ The point $P$ in polar coordinates is represented as $(r, \theta)$ and we assume that $r > 0$.

### Rectangular To Polar Conversion

$\rightarrow$ If $x$ and $y$ are the coordinates of $P$ in rectangular (cartesian) coordinate system, then
$$r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \quad \& \quad \theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)$$

> [!WARNING] Mathematical Error in Notes
> The expression $\theta = \tan^{-1}(y/x)$ only gives the correct polar angle for points in the right half-plane ($x > 0$). When $x < 0$, an angle adjustment of $+\pi$ (or $-\pi$) is necessary: $\theta = \tan^{-1}(y/x) + \pi$. For $x = 0$, $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$ if $y > 0$ and $\theta = -\frac{\pi}{2}$ if $y < 0$. In standard mathematical notation, this is uniquely given by the two-argument function $\operatorname{atan2}(y, x)$.

### Polar To Rectangular Conversion

$\rightarrow$ If '$r$' and '$\theta$' are the coordinates of $P$ (polar), then we can convert that to rectangular coordinates as follows.
$$x = r \cos \theta \quad \& \quad y = r \sin \theta$$

Note: Unlike the cartesian coordinate system where a pair $(x, y)$ uniquely identifies the position of a point, we have that in polar coordinates that $(r, \theta) = (r, \theta + 2n\pi), n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

$\Rightarrow$ That is there are infinitely many ways to represent the same point.

### Axes Of Polar Coordinates

$\rightarrow$ The axes in cartesian system is made up of $2 \perp^{\text{lr}}$ lines and the grid is seen as a set of lines $\parallel$ to x axis and another $\parallel$ to y-axis.

$\rightarrow$ In polar coordinate we have concentric circles associated with '$r$' and radial lines associated with '$\theta$'.

> [!NOTE] Figure/Diagram Placeholder
> **Polar Grid System:** A polar grid with concentric circles centered at the pole with radii $r = 1$ and $r = 2$, intersected by radial lines radiating outward at angles $\theta = 0, 2\pi$, $\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$, $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$, $\theta = \frac{3\pi}{4}$, $\theta = \pi$, $\theta = \frac{5\pi}{4}$, $\theta = \frac{3\pi}{2}$, and $\theta = \frac{7\pi}{4}$.

Note: The angle $\theta$ starts at the positive axis and increases in the anti-clockwise direction and decreases in the clockwise dir.

Note: It is not necessary that we restrict $r > 0$, we can choose it so that we may get $r \in \mathbb{R}$.

Note: The origin has polar coordinate equal to $(0, \theta)$ where $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$.

### Polar Graphs and Equations

$\rightarrow$ If $f$ is a non-negative function defined on the interval $[a, b]$, the set of all points $(r, \theta)$ satisfy-ing the equation $r = f(\theta)$ is called the graph of $f$ in polar coordinates.

$\rightarrow$ The equation $r = f(x)$ is called the polar equation of this graph.

> [!WARNING] Mathematical Error in Notes
> The notes write $r = f(x)$ instead of $r = f(\theta)$. In polar coordinates, the radius $r$ is a function of the angular coordinate $\theta$.

is called the radial set of $f$ on $[a, b]$.

Theorem 2.6: Let $R$ denote the radial set of a non-negative function $f$ over an interval $[a, b]$, where $0 \le b - a \le 2\pi$, and assume that $R$ is measurable. If $f^2$ is integrable on $[a, b]$, then the area of $R$ is given by the integral
$$a(R) = \frac{1}{2} \int_a^b f^2(\theta) \, d\theta$$

Proof: Let $s \ \& \ t$ be step functions defined on $[a, b]$ such that
$$s \le f \le t, \quad \forall \theta \in [a, b]$$

$\Rightarrow$ Since all of $s, f, t$ are non-ve, we have
$$s^2 \le f^2 \le t^2, \quad \forall \theta \in [a, b]$$

$\Rightarrow$ Also, given that $f^2$ is integrable on $[a, b]$, hence we have that $I = \int_a^b f^2(\theta) \, d\theta$ is the only real number such that
$$\int_a^b s^2(\theta) \, d\theta \le I \le \int_a^b t^2(\theta) \, d\theta$$
$$\Rightarrow \frac{1}{2} \int_a^b s^2(\theta) \, d\theta \le \frac{I}{2} \le \frac{1}{2} \int_a^b t^2(\theta) \, d\theta$$

$\Rightarrow$ Now, since $R$ is the radial set of $f$ and let $S$ and $T$ be the radial set of $s$ and $t$ we have
$$S \subseteq R \subseteq T$$

$\Rightarrow$ The above relation is the result of monotone property of area.
$$\Rightarrow a(S) \le a(R) \le a(T)$$

$\Rightarrow$ The above is possible since we are given that $R$ is measurable.

$\Rightarrow$ In fact $a(R)$ would be the only number satisfying the above result for all possible $s$ and $t$.

$\Rightarrow$ Also, we proved that
$$a(S) = \frac{1}{2} \int_a^b s^2(\theta) \, d\theta$$
$$a(T) = \frac{1}{2} \int_a^b t^2(\theta) \, d\theta$$
$$\Rightarrow \frac{1}{2}\int_a^b s^2(\theta) \, d\theta \le a(R) \le \frac{1}{2}\int_a^b t^2(\theta) \, d\theta$$
$$\Rightarrow a(R) = \frac{1}{2}\int_a^b f^2(\theta) \, d\theta$$

## 2.11 Application of Integration To The Calculation of Volume

$\rightarrow$ Just like area, we consider a set of points in 3d space a measurable set if there exist a set function $v$ that assigns this set a real number.

$\rightarrow$ The set function $v$ in this case is called the Volume Function.

$\rightarrow$ The set of all measurable sets in a 3d space is denoted by the symbol $\mathcal{A}$.

$\rightarrow$ Each set $S$ in $\mathcal{A}$ is called a solid.

### Cavalieri's Solid

$\rightarrow$ Cavalieri's Solid: Suppose $S$ is a solid and $L$ is a given line. If a plane $F$ is perpendicular to $L$, the intersection $F \cap S$ is called a cross-section $\perp^{\text{lr}}$ to $L$.

If every cross-section $\perp^{\text{lr}}$ to $L$ is a measurable set in its own plane, we call $S$ a cavalieri solid.

Note: We can imagine a Cavalieri Solid as a being a stack made of thin sheets of material.

### Rectangular Parallelepiped:

It is any set which is congruent to a set of the form
$$\{(x, y, z), 0 \le x \le a, 0 \le y \le b, 0 \le z \le c\}$$
where $a, b, c$ are any non-negative numbers.

### Axiomatic Definition of Volume

We assume there exists a class $\mathcal{A}$ of solids and a set function $v$, whose domain is $\mathcal{A}$, with the following properties:

(i) Non-negative Property: For each set $S$ in $\mathcal{A}$ we have $v(S) \ge 0$.

(ii) Difference Property: If $S$ and $T$ are in $\mathcal{A}$ with $S \subseteq T$, then $T - S$ is in $\mathcal{A}$, and we have $v(T - S) = v(T) - v(S)$.

(iii) Additive Property: If $S \ \& \ T$ are in $\mathcal{A}$, then $S \cup T$ is in $\mathcal{A}$ & $S \cap T$ is also in $\mathcal{A}$, with the relation
$$v(S \cup T) = v(S) + v(T) - v(S \cap T)$$

(iv) Cavalieri's Property: If $S$ and $T$ are two cavalieri solids in $\mathcal{A}$ with $a(S \cap F) \le a(T \cap F)$ for every $F \perp^{\text{lr}}$ to a given line, then
$$v(S) \le v(T)$$

(v) Choice of Scale: Every box $B$ is in $\mathcal{A}$. If the edges of $B$ have lengths $a, b, c$, then
$$v(B) = abc$$

(vi) Every convex set is in $\mathcal{A}$.

### Convex Set:

A set of points is called convex if, for every pair of points $P$ and $Q$ in the set, the line joining $P \ \& \ Q$ is also in the set.

$\Rightarrow$ Hence, the (vi) axiom ensures that all elementary solids are in $\mathcal{A}$.

Note: Using axiom (iii) It can be proven that the volume of an empty set is 0, If $S = T$, then we have
$$v(S - T) = v(S) - v(T) = 0 \Rightarrow v(\phi) = 0$$

> [!WARNING] Mathematical Error in Notes
> The notes state "Using axiom (iii) It can be proven that the volume of an empty set is 0", but the identity used, $v(S - T) = v(S) - v(T)$, comes directly from Axiom (ii) (the Difference Property), not Axiom (iii) (the Additive Property).

### Monotone Property:

Axiom (iii) also implies that volume function is monotonous
$$v(S) \le v(T) \quad \text{if } S \subseteq T$$

$\Rightarrow$ This can be easily proved using
$$v(T - S) \ge 0 \Rightarrow v(T) - v(S) \ge 0 \Rightarrow v(T) \ge v(S), \quad \text{if } S \subseteq T$$

> [!WARNING] Mathematical Error in Notes
> The notes claim that "Axiom (iii) also implies that volume function is monotonous", but the proof provided uses $v(T - S) \ge 0$ (Axiom (i), Non-negativity) and $v(T - S) = v(T) - v(S)$ (Axiom (ii), Difference Property). Axiom (iii) is not required for this proof.

### Area of Plane Set:

Using the monotone property we can also prove that volume of a plane set is 0.

$\Rightarrow$ Let $S$ be a plane set (bounded), and then there exists a square which $S$ is subset of, let it be $S'$.

$\rightarrow$ Let $B$ be a box of height $c$ and same base as $S'$.
$$\Rightarrow S \subseteq S' \subseteq B$$
$$\Rightarrow v(S) \le v(S') \le v(B) = a^2 c$$
where $a^2 = \text{area of } S'$.

$\Rightarrow$ Now $B$ can have height equal to any $c > 0$.

$\Rightarrow v(S) \ge 0$, if $v(S) > 0$, then $B$ cannot have a height $c < \frac{v(S)}{a^2}$.

$\Rightarrow$ (Or) more formally we can define $c = \frac{1}{n} \quad \forall n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$.
$$\Rightarrow 0 \le v(S) \le \frac{a^2}{n}, \quad \forall n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$$
$$\therefore v(S) = 0$$

Note: If two solids have the same area for every plane $\perp^{\text{lr}}$ to a line i.e $a(S \cap F) = a(T \cap F)$, then we can prove using axiom (iv) that their volumes are equal.
$$\Rightarrow a(S \cap F) \le a(T \cap F) \Rightarrow v(S) \le v(T)$$
$$\text{Also, } a(T \cap F) \le a(S \cap F) \Rightarrow v(T) \le v(S)$$
$$\Rightarrow v(S) = v(T)$$

where $B$ is a bounded plane set.

### Theorem 2.7: Cavalieri's Principle for Volumes

Theorem 2.7: Let $R$ be a Cavalieri Solid in $\mathcal{A}$ with a Cross-Sectional area function $a_R$ which is integrable on an interval $[a, b]$ and zero outside $[a, b]$. Then the volume of $R$ is equal to the integral of cross-section-al area function,
$$v(R) = \int_a^b a_R(u) \, du$$

Proof: Consider a solid whose cross-sectional area function is a step function, if we consider each interval on the solid to be a right cylinder where the step function is constant then the volume of solid will be sum volume of each right cylinder, in that case the volume will be
$$v(S) = \sum_{k=1}^n s_k (x_k - x_{k-1}) = \int_a^b s(x) \, dx$$

$\rightarrow$ Let $s$ and $t$ be step functions such that $s(x) \le a_R(x) \le t(x) \ \forall x \in [a, b]$, since $a_R$ is integrable we have $I = \int_a^b a_R(x) \, dx$ as the only number such that
$$\int_a^b s(x) \, dx \le I \le \int_a^b t(x) \, dx$$
for all $s$ and $t$.

$\rightarrow$ Now consider solids $S$ and $T$ such that their cross-sectional area functions are $s$ and $t$.
$$\Rightarrow v(S) = \int_a^b s(x) \, dx \quad \& \quad v(T) = \int_a^b t(x) \, dx$$

$\Rightarrow$ Also, since $s(x) \le a_R(x) \le t(x)$ we have that $v(S) \le v(R) \le v(T)$ using axiom (iv).
$$\Rightarrow \int_a^b s(x) \, dx \le v(R) \le \int_a^b t(x) \, dx$$

$\Rightarrow$ Hence, we have that
$$v(R) = \int_a^b a_R(x) \, dx$$

### Volume of a Solid of Revolution

$\rightarrow$ If $f$ is a non-negative integrable function in the interval $[a, b]$, then if we rotate the ordinate set of $f$ about the $x$ axis, it sweeps out a solid of revolution.

$\rightarrow$ At any point $x \in [a, b]$, the cross section of this solid $\perp^{\text{lr}}$ to $x$ axis is a circle with radius $f(x)$, hence the area is $\pi f^2(x)$.

$\rightarrow$ If $f^2(x)$ is integrable on $[a, b]$ then using theorem 2.7 we have that the volume of this solid of revolution is
$$v(F) = \int_a^b \pi f^2(x) \, dx$$

$\rightarrow$ More generally if $f$ and $g$ are two non-negative integrable functions on $[a, b]$ such that $f(x) \le g(x)$. Then if we rotate the area b/w the curves $f$ and $g$ about $x$ axis the we get a solid of revolution whose volume is
$$\int_a^b \pi (g^2(x) - f^2(x)) \, dx$$
if $g^2(x) - f^2(x)$ is integrable on $[a, b]$.

### Volume of Sphere:

A sphere of radius '$r$' can be obtained by revolving the ordinate set of $f = \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}$ about the $x$-axis. Hence the volume of sphere is
$$= \int_{-r}^r \pi (r^2 - x^2) \, dx = 2\pi \int_0^r (r^2 - x^2) \, dx$$
$$= 2\pi \left(r^2 x - \frac{x^3}{3}\right)_0^r = 2\pi \cdot \frac{2r^3}{3} = \frac{4\pi r^3}{3}$$

Hence, the volume of sphere is $\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$.

## 2.13 Application of Integration To The Concept of Work

$\Rightarrow$ Work is the measure of the amount of energy expended by a force in moving a particle from one point to another.

$\Rightarrow$ In this discussion we will only consider motion along straight and will also assume that the force acts along that same line.

> [!WARNING] Mathematical Error in Notes
> The notes omit the noun "line" in the phrase "consider motion along straight and will also assume...". It should properly read "consider motion along a straight line".

$\Rightarrow$ We also assume in this case that work done by the force is a function of its position i.e if the particle is at point $x$, then the force acting on the particle is $f(x)$.

### Work Done by Constant Force

Work done by a constant force $f(x) = c$ in moving a particle from $x = a$ to $x = b$ is
$$W_a^b(f) = c(b - a)$$

$\Rightarrow$ Note here there is no rule that $a < b$, we can have $b < a$ as well.

Note: If the function acts along the positive direction of x axis, then it is considered $> 0$ and if it acts along -ve direction of x axis, then it is considered -ve.

> [!WARNING] Mathematical Error in Notes
> The note states "if the function acts along the positive direction of x axis...", which loosely refers to the function rather than the physical force vector. It should properly state "if the force acts along the positive direction of the $x$-axis...".

### Fundamental Properties of Work

Let $W_a^b(f)$ be the work done by a force function $f$ in moving a particle from $a$ to $b$. Then work has the following properties:

(i) **Additive:** If $a < c < b$, then
$$W_a^b(f) = W_a^c(f) + W_c^b(f)$$

(ii) **Monotone Property:** If $f \le g$ on $[a, b]$, then
$$W_a^b(f) \le W_a^b(g)$$

(iii) **Elementary Formula:** If $f$ is constant, say $f(x) = c$ for all $x$ in the open interval $(a, b)$, then
$$W_a^b(f) = c(b - a)$$

Note: The additive property can be extended to any finite number of intervals using induction.

$\Rightarrow$ This gives us that if
$$a = x_0 < x_1 < x_2 \dots < x_n = b$$
then
$$W_a^b(f) = \sum_{k=1}^n W_{x_{k-1}}^{x_k}(f)$$

### Work Done By Step Function Force

$\Rightarrow$ If $f$ is a force function which is constant on every open interval of partition $P = \{x_0, x_1, \dots, x_n\}$ of the interval $[a, b]$, then work done by force in moving a particle from $a$ to $b$ is
$$W_a^b(f) = \sum_{k=1}^n W_{x_{k-1}}^{x_k}(f)$$
$$\Rightarrow W_a^b(f) = \sum_{k=1}^n f_k (x_k - x_{k-1})$$
$$\Rightarrow \boxed{W_a^b(f) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx}$$

### Work Done by Integrable Force Function

#### Theorem 2.8
Suppose work has been defined for a class of force function in such a way that it satisfies properties 1, 2 and 3. Then the work done by an integrable force function $f$ in moving a particle from $a$ to $b$ is equal to the integral of $f$,
$$W_a^b(f) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx$$

**Proof:**
Given that $f$ is integrable on $[a, b]$ we have for every step functions $s$ and $t$ on $[a, b]$ such that $s \le f \le t$, then
$$\int_a^b s(x)\,dx \le \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \le \int_a^b t(x)\,dx$$

$\Rightarrow$ Now, if we imagine $s$ and $t$ as force functions, then using the monotonicity of the work, we have
$$W_a^b(s) \le W_a^b(f) \le W_a^b(t)$$
$$\Rightarrow \int_a^b s(x)\,dx \le W_a^b(f) \le \int_a^b t(x)\,dx$$

$\Rightarrow$ This inequality is true for every step functions $s$ and $t$,
$$\Rightarrow \boxed{W_a^b(f) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx}$$

### Work Required to Stretch a Spring

**Hooke's Law:** It states that the force required to stretch/compress a spring by a distance of $x$ beyond its natural length is proportional to $x$
$$\Rightarrow f(x) = cx$$

$\Rightarrow$ Work done by this force can be calculated by imagining the x axis along the axis of the spring, then

> [!NOTE] Figure/Diagram Placeholder
> **Spring attached to a wall (Hooke's Law):** A horizontal spring attached to a rigid wall on the left, with the equilibrium position labeled as $0$ at the right end of the relaxed spring. A dashed line shows an extension to position $b$ along the $x$-axis, with a dimension arrow indicating distance $b$.

$$W_0^b(f) = \int_0^b f(x)\,dx = c\int_0^b x\,dx$$
$$\boxed{W_0^b(f) = c \frac{b^2}{2}}$$

### Unit of Work

$\rightarrow$ If force is measured in pounds and distance in feet, we measure work in foot pound.

$\rightarrow$ If force in dyne and distance in cm, then work is measured in dyne centimeter.

$\rightarrow$ If force is measured in newtons and distance in meters, then work is measured in newton-meters.
$$1\text{ dyne-centimeter} = 1\text{ erg}$$
$$1\text{ newton-meter} = 1\text{ joule}$$

## 2.15 Average Value of a Function

### Arithmetic Mean
Arithmetic Mean of $n$ real numbers $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$ is defined by
$$\bar{a} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n a_i}{n}$$

### Root Mean Square
Root Mean Square of $n$ real numbers $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$ is defined by
$$\bar{a} = \sqrt{\frac{\sum a_i^2}{n}}$$

### Definition of Average Value of a Function
If $f$ is integrable on $[a, b]$, we define $A(f)$, the average value of $f$ on $[a, b]$, by the formula:
$$A(f) = \frac{1}{b - a} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx$$

**Interpretation:**
If $f$ is non-negative the formula has a simple geometric interpretation. Written in the form $(b - a)(A(f)) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx$, it states that the rectangle of altitude $A(f)$ and base $(b - a)$ has the same area as the ordinate set of $f(x)$ on the same interval.

> [!NOTE] Figure/Diagram Placeholder
> **Geometric Interpretation of Average Value:** Cartesian coordinate axes showing a non-negative continuous curve $f(x)$ on the interval $[a, b]$. A horizontal dashed line at height $A(f)$ forms a rectangle on $[a, b]$ with base $(b - a)$ and height $A(f)$, illustrating that the area of the rectangle equals the area under the curve.

### Average Value of Step Function
If we look at the avg value of a step function over the interval $[a, b]$ we see the relation b/w the avg value of a function and arithmetic mean.
$$A(f) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{s_k (x_k - x_{k-1})}{b - a}$$

> [!WARNING] Mathematical Error in Notes
> The notes write $A(f) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{s_k (x_k - x_{k-1})}{b - a}$, omitting the factor $\frac{1}{b - a}$ in front of the integral. The correct formula is $A(s) = \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b s(x)\,dx = \sum_{k=1}^n s_k \frac{x_k - x_{k-1}}{b - a}$.

Lets say that $f$ is any function and let $s$ be a step function defined on $[a, b]$ as follows:
$$s = \{ f(x_k), \quad x_{k-1} < x < x_k \}$$
where $x_k = a + \frac{k(b - a)}{n}$, i.e $s$ is a step function defined by dividing the interval $[a, b]$ into equal subintervals and in all open subintervals taking the value of the function.
$$\Rightarrow A(s) = \int_a^b \frac{s(x)\,dx}{(b - a)} = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{f(x_k)\frac{(b - a)}{n}}{(b - a)}$$
$$\Rightarrow \boxed{A(s) = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^n f(x_k)}{n}}$$

$\Rightarrow$ The integral of the function divided by $(b - a)$ is simple the limit of above expression as $n \to \infty$.

### Weighted Mean
Sometimes weighted mean is used where each data point $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$ is assigned a weight $w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n$ not all 0.
Then weighted mean is defined:
$$\bar{a} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^n w_k a_k}{\sum_{k=1}^n w_k}$$

$\rightarrow$ When the weights are all equal this turns into the ordinary arithmetic mean formula.

### Weighted Means For Integrable Function
$\rightarrow$ The concept of weighted mean can be extended to integrable functions as follows:
$$A(f) = \frac{\int_a^b w(x) \cdot f(x)\,dx}{\int_a^b w(x)\,dx}$$
where $w(x)$ is a non-negative function & $\int_a^b w(x)\,dx \neq 0$.

### First & Second Moments (Center of Mass and Moment of Inertia)

> [!WARNING] Mathematical Error in Notes
> The heading in the notes is written as "First & Second Momentum" instead of the standard terminology "First & Second Moments".

$\rightarrow$ This is application of weighted mean used in physics.

$\rightarrow$ Suppose a rod of varying mass is placed at x axis such that the density of rod at point $x$ is given by $p(x)$ (Greek letter $\rho$ (rho)).

$\rightarrow$ Then the mass of the whole length $L$ is given by
$$m(x) = \int_0^L \rho(x)\,dx$$

> [!WARNING] Mathematical Error in Notes
> The notes write $m(x) = \int_0^L \rho(x)\,dx$. The total mass of the rod on $[0, L]$ is a constant scalar quantity $m$ (or $M$), not a function of the spatial coordinate $x$.

$\rightarrow$ **First Moment:** The first moment of rod about 0 or the center of mass of the rod is given by
$$\bar{x} = \frac{\int_0^L x \rho(x)\,dx}{\int_0^L \rho(x)\,dx}$$

$\rightarrow$ First Moment is the weighted average of $f(x) = x$ with weights as $\rho(x)$.

$\rightarrow$ **Mistake acknowledged in notes:** The first moment of rod is given by $\int_0^L x \rho(x)\,dx$ and the formula given above is of center of mass only.

$\rightarrow$ **Second Moment:** The second moment is the weighted average of function $f(x) = x^2$ with the weight as $\rho(x)$ i.e $\int_0^L x^2 \rho(x)\,dx$.

> [!WARNING] Mathematical Error in Notes
> The notes state that the second moment is the "weighted average ... i.e $\int_0^L x^2 \rho(x)\,dx$". The weighted average of $x^2$ is $\frac{\int_0^L x^2 \rho(x)\,dx}{\int_0^L \rho(x)\,dx}$. The integral $\int_0^L x^2 \rho(x)\,dx$ is the second moment (moment of inertia) itself, equal to the weighted average multiplied by the total mass.

$\rightarrow$ The second moment is also called the moment of inertia.

$\rightarrow$ **Radius of Gyration:** It is defined as follows:
$$\mu^2 = \frac{\int_0^L x^2 \rho(x)\,dx}{\int_0^L \rho(x)\,dx}$$

## 2.17 Indefinite Integral

Let $f$ be a integrable function on the interval $[a, b]$, then for all $x \in [a, b]$ we can fix $a$ & $f$ and see the integral of $f$ as a function of $f$ i.e
$$A(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt$$

> [!WARNING] Mathematical Error in Notes
> The notes state "...see the integral of $f$ as a function of $f$ i.e $A(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt$". The indefinite integral $A(x)$ is a function of the upper limit $x$, not of $f$.

$\rightarrow$ The function $A(x)$ is called an indefinite integral of $f$.

Note: We say an indefinite integral instead of the indefinite integral as it depends on the value of '$a$'. A different value of '$a$' might give us a completely different $A(x)$.

### Difference of Indefinite Integrals
Let $A(x)$ and $F(x)$ be two indefinite integrals of a function $f$ with lower limits '$a$' and '$c$', then we have
$$A(x) - F(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt - \int_c^x f(t)\,dt$$
$$\Rightarrow A(x) - F(x) = \int_a^c f(t)\,dt$$

$\Rightarrow$ Assuming that $f$ is integrable on the interval $[a, c]$ we have that $A(x) - F(x)$ is independent of $x$ and is a fixed constant value.

$\Rightarrow$ Now suppose that $a$ & $b$ both fall into an interval where $f$ is integrable, then we can write
$$\int_a^b f(x) = \int_c^b f(x) - \int_c^a f(x)$$
$$\boxed{\int_a^b f(x) = F(b) - F(a)}$$

$\Rightarrow$ Hence, we can write the integral as a difference of indefinite integrals.

$\Rightarrow$ Here, $c$ can be any fixed number also from the interval where $f$ is integrable.

$\Rightarrow$ A different choice of '$c$' will merely change the indefinite integral $F(x)$ by a constant and this still won't affect the difference $F(b) - F(a)$.

$\Rightarrow$ Hence we say that $\int_a^b f = F(b) - F(a)$ is independent of the choice of '$c$'.

Note: There is a special symbol to denote $F(b) - F(a)$ and that is $F(x)\big|_a^b$
$$\Rightarrow \boxed{\int_a^b f(t)\,dt = F(x)\Big|_a^b = F(b) - F(a)}$$

#### Example
Find indefinite integral of $x^n$ for all non-negative integers $n$ and then also represent $\int_a^b t^n\,dt$ as difference of indefinite integral.

**Sol:**
$$\int_0^x t^n\,dt = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}$$
$$\int_a^b t^n\,dt = \int_0^b t^n\,dt - \int_0^a t^n\,dt$$
$$\Rightarrow \boxed{\int_a^b t^n\,dt = \frac{b^{n+1}}{n+1} - \frac{a^{n+1}}{n+1}}$$

### Geometric Relation b/w $f$ and its Indefinite Integral
$\Rightarrow$ The relation b/w $f$ and its indefinite integral is that the indefinite integral gives the area under the curve if $f$ is non-negative and gives signed area in general case of $f$.

### Concave And Convex Functions
$\Rightarrow$ Sometimes some special property of $f$ gives rise to special properties in $A(x)$, for example if $f$ is non-negative, then $A(x)$ is an increasing function.

$\Rightarrow$ If $a \le x \le y \le b$ & $f$ is non-negative on $[a, b]$, then we have that
$$A(y) - A(x) = \int_a^y f - \int_a^x f$$
$$= \int_x^y f \ge 0$$
$$\Rightarrow \boxed{A(y) \ge A(x)}$$

#### Definition of a Convex Function
A function $g$ is said to be convex on the interval $[a, b]$ if, for all $x$ and $y$ in $[a, b]$ and for every $\alpha$ satisfying $0 < \alpha < 1$, we have
$$g(z) \le \alpha g(y) + (1 - \alpha) g(x),$$
where $z = \alpha y + (1 - \alpha)x$.

We say $g$ is concave on $[a, b]$ if the reverse inequality holds,
$$g(z) \ge \alpha(g(y)) + (1 - \alpha) g(x),$$
where $z = \alpha y + (1 - \alpha)x$.

**Interpretation:**
Consider the points $(a, g(a))$ & $(b, g(b))$ on the graph of $g$ at points $a$ & $b$.
$\Rightarrow$ The equation of line joining these is given by
$$y = mx + c, \text{ where } m = \frac{g(b) - g(a)}{b - a}$$
$$\& \quad c = -ma + g(a)$$

Now, $z = \alpha(b) + (1 - \alpha)a = a + (b - a)\alpha$
$\Rightarrow z$ is any point in the interval $[a, b]$
$\Rightarrow$ If we substitute $x = z$ on the equation of line we will get that
$$y = m(a + (b - a)\alpha) - ma + g(a)$$
$$= (g(b) - g(a))\alpha + g(a)$$
$$= \alpha g(b) + (1 - \alpha) g(x)$$

> [!WARNING] Mathematical Error in Notes
> In the expression $y = (g(b) - g(a))\alpha + g(a) = \alpha g(b) + (1 - \alpha)g(x)$, the notes write $g(x)$ instead of $g(a)$ in the second term. The correct formula is $\alpha g(b) + (1 - \alpha)g(a)$, which the notes then correctly use in the subsequent coordinate expression $(z, \alpha(g(b)) + (1 - \alpha)g(a))$.

$\Rightarrow$ Hence the point $(z, \alpha(g(b)) + (1 - \alpha)g(a))$ is the point where the line $x = z$ intersects the line joining the points $(a, g(a))$ & $(b, g(b))$.

**Conclusion:** Hence the definition more technically states that the line joining $g(x)$ & $g(y)$ is above the graph of $g$ in b/w $x$ & $y$.

> [!NOTE] Figure/Diagram Placeholder
> **Geometric interpretation of convexity:** Cartesian axes showing a convex (concave up) curve $y = g(x)$ spanning between points $x$ and $y$. A straight secant line (chord) joins $(x, g(x))$ and $(y, g(y))$. For an intermediate coordinate $z$, a vertical dashed line shows that the point on the curve $(z, g(z))$ lies strictly below the point on the secant chord $(z, \alpha g(y) + (1 - \alpha)g(x))$.

Similarly, the other condition means that the line joining $g(x)$ & $g(y)$ is below the curve b/w $x$ & $y$.

> [!NOTE] Figure/Diagram Placeholder
> **Geometric interpretation of concavity:** Cartesian axes showing a concave (concave down) curve $y = g(x)$ between $x$ and $y$. A straight secant line connects $(x, g(x))$ and $(y, g(y))$. For an intermediate point $z$, a dashed vertical line demonstrates that the point on the curve $(z, g(z))$ lies strictly above the point on the secant chord $(z, \alpha g(y) + (1 - \alpha)g(x))$.

#### Theorem 2.9
Let $A(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt$. Then $A$ is convex on the interval where $f$ is increasing and concave on the interval where $f$ is decreasing.

**Sol:**
Assume that $f$ is increasing on $[a, b]$ and $x < y$, and let $z = \alpha y + (1 - \alpha)x$ for some $\alpha \in (0, 1)$.

$\Rightarrow$ We need to prove that
$$A(z) \le \alpha A(y) + (1 - \alpha) A(x).$$

$\Rightarrow$ We can write $A(z) = \alpha(A(z)) + (1 - \alpha)A(z)$
$$\Rightarrow \alpha A(z) + (1 - \alpha)A(z) \le \alpha A(y) + (1 - \alpha) A(x)$$
$$\Rightarrow (1 - \alpha)(A(z) - A(x)) \le \alpha(A(y) - A(z))$$

$\Rightarrow$ So this is the new inequality that we need to prove.

$\Rightarrow$ Since $f$ is increasing, we have that
$$f(t) \le f(z), \quad x \le t \le z$$
$$\Rightarrow \int_x^z f(t)\,dt \le f(z)(z - x)$$

$\Rightarrow$ Similarly, we have that
$$f(z) \le f(t), \quad z \le t \le y$$
$$\Rightarrow f(z)(y - z) \le \int_z^y f(t)\,dt$$

$\Rightarrow$ Now we will use these new inequalities as follows:
$$(1 - \alpha)\int_x^z f(t)\,dt \le (1 - \alpha)f(z)(z - x)$$
$$\Rightarrow (1 - \alpha)(z - x) = \alpha(y - z)$$
$$\Rightarrow (1 - \alpha)\int_x^z f \le \alpha f(z)(y - z) \le \alpha \int_z^y f$$
$$\Rightarrow \boxed{(1 - \alpha)\int_x^z f \le \alpha \int_z^y f}$$
$$\Rightarrow (1 - \alpha)(A(z) - A(x)) \le \alpha(A(y) - A(z))$$
$\Rightarrow$ Hence, Proved for the case when $f$ is increasing.

$\Rightarrow$ When $f$ is decreasing we have that $-f$ is increasing, so proceeding in the same way we get
$$(1 - \alpha)\int_x^z -f \le \alpha \int_z^y -f$$
$$\Rightarrow (1 - \alpha)\int_x^z f \ge \alpha \int_z^y f$$
$$\Rightarrow (1 - \alpha)(A(z) - A(x)) \ge \alpha(A(y) - A(z))$$
$$\Rightarrow \boxed{A(z) \ge \alpha A(y) + (1 - \alpha) A(x)}$$
$\Rightarrow$ Hence in this case when $f$ is decreasing, we see that $A(x)$ is concave function.

**Example:**
The cosine function decreases in the interval $[0, \pi]$, and the integral of cosine is sine, hence it is concave in the interval $[0, \pi]$.

### Example 3
Given an even function $f$, defined everywhere, periodic with period 2, and integrable on every interval. Let $g(x) = \int_0^x f(t)\,dt$, and let $A = g(1)$.

$$g(1) = \int_0^1 f(t)\,dt = A$$

$$\Rightarrow \int_{-1}^1 f(t)\,dt = \int_{-1}^{-1+2} f(t)\,dt = g(2)$$
$$\Rightarrow \text{But } f \text{ is odd} \implies \int_{-1}^1 f(t)\,dt = 0$$
$$\Rightarrow \boxed{g(2) = 0}$$

$$\Rightarrow g(2n) = \int_0^{2n} f(t)\,dt$$
$$= \int_0^2 f + \int_2^4 f + \dots + \int_{2n-2}^{2n} f$$
$$= n \, g(2) = 0$$

#### (b)
$$g(x+2) = \int_0^{x+2} f(t)\,dt$$
$$= \int_0^x f + \int_x^{x+2} f$$
$$= g(x) + g(2) = g(x)$$
$$\Rightarrow \boxed{g(x+2) = g(x)}$$

$$g(-x) = \int_0^{-x} f(t)\,dt = -\int_0^x f(-t)\,dt$$
$$= \int_0^x f(t)\,dt = g(x)$$
$$\Rightarrow \boxed{g(-x) = g(x)}$$

### Exercise 20
Given two functions $f$ and $g$, integrable on every interval and having the following properties:
$\rightarrow$ $f$ is odd, $g$ is even,
$\rightarrow$ $f(5) = 7$, $f(0) = 0$,
$\rightarrow$ $g(x) = f(x+5)$,
$\rightarrow$ $f(x) = \int_0^x g(t)\,dt$, $\forall x \in \mathbb{R}$

Prove that:
(a) $f(x-5) = -g(x)$ $\forall x \in \mathbb{R}$
(b) $\int_0^5 f(t)\,dt = 7$
(c) $\int_0^x f(t)\,dt = g(0) - g(x)$

**Sol:**
#### (a)
Given that $g(x) = f(x+5)$
$$\Rightarrow g(-x) = g(x) = f(-x+5) = -f(x-5)$$
$$\Rightarrow f(x-5) = -g(x)$$

#### (b)
$$\int_0^5 f(t)\,dt = \int_{-5}^0 f(t+5)\,dt$$
$$= \int_{-5}^0 g(t)\,dt = -\int_0^{-5} g(t)\,dt$$
$$= \int_0^5 g(t)\,dt = f(5)$$
$$\Rightarrow \int_0^5 f(t)\,dt = f(5) = 7$$

#### (c)
$$\int_0^x f(t)\,dt = \int_5^{x+5} f(t-5)\,dt$$
$$= \int_5^x -g(t)\,dt + \int_x^{x+5} -g(t)\,dt$$
$$= \int_0^5 g(t)\,dt - \int_0^x g(t)\,dt - \int_x^{x+5} g(t)\,dt$$
$$= f(5) - f(x+5)$$
$$= g(0) - g(x)$$
$$\Rightarrow \boxed{\int_0^x f(t)\,dt = g(0) - g(x)}$$

### Exercise 21
Solve the following equation for $x$:
$$\int_1^x \left(1 + 2t - \frac{2}{5} t^3\right)dt = \frac{2}{5} \int_{1-\sqrt{3}}^x (1 - t^3)\,dt$$

**Sol:**
$$\text{LHS} = \int_1^x \left(1 + 2t - \frac{2}{5} t^3\right)dt$$
$$= \left( t + t^2 - \frac{t^4}{10} \right)_1^x$$
$$= x + x^2 - \frac{x^4}{10} - \left( 1 + 1 - \frac{1}{10} \right)$$
$$= x + x^2 - \frac{x^4}{10} - \frac{19}{10}$$

$$\text{RHS} = \frac{2}{5} \int_{1-\sqrt{3}}^x (1 - t^3)\,dt$$
$$= \frac{2}{5} \left( t - \frac{t^4}{4} \right)_{1-\sqrt{3}}^x$$
$$= \frac{2}{5} \left[ \left( x - \frac{x^4}{4} \right) - \left( 1 - \sqrt{3} - \frac{(1 - \sqrt{3})^4}{4} \right) \right]$$
$$= \frac{2}{5} \left[ \left( x - \frac{x^4}{4} \right) - \left( 1 - \sqrt{3} - \frac{\cancel{4}(4 + 3 - 4\sqrt{3})}{\cancel{4}} \right) \right]$$
$$= \frac{2}{5} \left[ \left( x - \frac{x^4}{4} \right) - (1 - \sqrt{3} - (7 - 4\sqrt{3})) \right]$$
$$= \frac{2}{5} \left( x - \frac{x^4}{4} \right) + \frac{12}{5} - \frac{6}{5}\sqrt{3}$$

$\text{LHS} = \text{RHS}$ we need to solve now:
$$x + x^2 - \frac{x^4}{10} - \frac{19}{10} = \frac{2x}{5} - \frac{x^4}{10} + \frac{12}{5} - \frac{6\sqrt{3}}{5}$$
$$\Rightarrow \frac{3x}{5} + x^2 + \frac{6\sqrt{3}}{5} - \frac{12}{5} - \frac{19}{10} = 0$$
$$\Rightarrow 10x^2 + 6x + 12\sqrt{3} - 43 = 0$$
$$\Rightarrow x = \frac{-6 \pm \sqrt{36 - 40(12\sqrt{3} - 43)}}{20}$$

$\Rightarrow$ Since $x > 0$, we need to choose the bigger root of the equation.

> [!WARNING] Mathematical Error in Notes
> The problem statement asks for all real values of $x$ satisfying the equation without restricting $x > 0$. Since polynomial integrands are defined and integrable everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$, both roots are valid solutions:
> $$x = \frac{-6 \pm \sqrt{1756 - 480\sqrt{3}}}{20} = \frac{-3 \pm \sqrt{439 - 120\sqrt{3}}}{10}$$
> Numerically, the two roots are $x \approx 1.22$ and $x \approx -1.82$.
