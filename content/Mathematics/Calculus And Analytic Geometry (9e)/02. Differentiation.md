# 2.1 The Derivative of a Function

##### Definition
The derivative of the function $f$ with respect to the variable $x$ is the function $f'$ whose value at $x$ is
$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}
$$
provided that the limit exists.

>[!note]
>1. The domain of $f'$ is the set of all points in domain of $f$ for which the derivative exists. $\implies D(f') \subseteq D(f)$
>2. If $f'(x)$ exists, then we say that the function $f$ is **differentiable** at $x$.

##### Differentiable Functions are Continuous

##### Theorem 1
If $f$ has a derivative at $x = c$, then $f$ is continuous at $x = c$.

**Proof:**
We need to prove that if $f'(c)$ exists, then $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)$, which is equivalent to $\lim_{h \to 0} f(c+h) = f(c)$.
$$
\lim_{h \to 0} f(c+h) = \lim_{h \to 0} \left( f(c) + \frac{f(c+h) - f(c)}{h} \cdot h \right)
$$
$$
= \lim_{h \to 0} f(c) + \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(c+h) - f(c)}{h} \cdot \lim_{h \to 0} h
$$
$$
= f(c) + f'(c) \cdot 0 = f(c)
$$
$$
\boxed{\lim_{h \to 0} f(c+h) = f(c)}
$$
hence $f$ is continuous at $x = c$.

**Another Way:**
$$
\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(c+h) - f(c)}{h} = f'(c)
$$
$$
\implies \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(c+h) - f(c)}{h} \cdot \lim_{h \to 0} h = f'(c) \cdot 0
$$
$$
\implies \lim_{h \to 0} f(c+h) - f(c) = 0 \implies \lim_{h \to 0} f(c+h) = f(c)
$$

### Differentiable on an Interval: One-Sided Derivatives

A function $y = f(x)$ is differentiable on an open interval (finite or infinite) if it has a derivative at each point of the interval. It is differentiable on a closed interval $[a,b]$ if it is differentiable on the interior $(a,b)$ and if the limits
$$
\lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} \quad \text{(Right-Hand derivative at } a\text{)}
$$
$$
\lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{f(b+h) - f(b)}{h} \quad \text{(Left-Hand derivative at } b\text{)}
$$
exist at the endpoints.

>[!note]
>We see that derivative is nothing but a special limit, and as the limit of functions goes, we have already seen that for two-sided limit to exist, the one-sided limits must exist and must be equal.
>
>Hence, in the same way, for two-sided derivative to exist, both one-sided derivatives must exist and must be equal.

##### Example
Discuss differentiability of $y = |x|$.

**Solution:** The function is differentiable at all points in the intervals $(-\infty, 0)$ & $(0, \infty)$.

At $0$, we have left-hand derivative as:
$$
\lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{f(0+h) - f(0)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{-h}{h} = -1
$$
Similarly, the right-hand derivative is $1$, hence the function $y = |x|$ is not differentiable at $x = 0$.

### The Intermediate Value Property of Derivatives

##### Theorem 2
If $a$ and $b$ are any two points in an interval on which $f$ is differentiable, then $f'$ takes on every value between $f'(a)$ and $f'(b)$.

##### Alternative Statement
If $f$ is differentiable on the closed interval $[a,b]$. If $y_{0}$ is any value between $f'(a)$ & $f'(b)$, then there exists at least one $c \in (a,b)$ such that $f'(c) = y_{0}$.

##### Proof: Optimization Proof

We will prove this for the case when $f'(a) < y_{0} < f'(b)$ and for other cases it can be proved in similar manner.

Consider the function $g(x) = f(x) - y_{0}x$, since $f$ is differentiable in range $[a,b]$ and so is $yx$, we have that $g(x)$ is differentiable on $[a,b]$.
$$
\implies g'(x) = f'(x) - y
$$
Now if we look at the value of $g'(x)$ at endpoints we see that:
$$
g'(a) = f'(a) - y < 0, \quad g'(b) = f'(b) - y > 0
$$
Now, using the extreme value theorem on $g$ (since $g$ is continuous on $[a,b]$) we know that it will take a minimum value somewhere.

1. It can't be at $a$ as $g'(a) < 0$, which means that $g$ decreases for some interval after $a$.
2. It cannot be at $b$ as $g'(b) > 0$, which means that $g$ decreases for some interval before $b$.

Hence, $g$ takes a minimum value at some interior point $c$.

Now, since $g$ is differentiable on $[a,b] \implies g$ is differentiable at $c$.

Then by Fermat's theorem (Interior Extremum Theorem) we have that $g'(c) = 0$,
$$
\implies f'(c) - y = 0 \implies \boxed{f'(c) = y}
$$
![](Pasted%20image%2020260921124708.png)
##### Moving Secant Proof

In this proof we use the fact that even though the slope of tangent is not continuous, but slopes of secants are continuous, and then we use the fact that $f$ is differentiable along with MVT.

Since $f$ is differentiable on $[a,b]$, $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$.

Let $S(x) = \dfrac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \quad \forall x \in [a, b-h]$.

Now $S$ is also continuous on $[a, b-h]$, moreover we can choose the value of $h$ very small so that $S(a) \approx f'(a)$ & $S(b-h) \approx f'(b)$.

Hence we will get that $y$ is between $S(a)$ & $S(b-h)$. But since $S$ is continuous, there will be some point $x_0$ such that $S(x_0) = y$ using intermediate value theorem.
$$
\implies S(x_0) = \frac{f(x_0+h) - f(x_0)}{h} = y
$$
Now, $f$ is differentiable on the interval $[x_0, x_0+h]$, then using MVT we get that at some point in this range, the average value of the function is equal to instantaneous rate of change.
$$
\implies f'(c) = \frac{f(x_0+h) - f(x_0)}{h} = y
$$
$$
\implies \boxed{f'(c) = y}
$$

>[!note] Common Misconception
>A common misconception that people get from this theorem is that since $f$ is differentiable on $[a,b]$ and $f'(x)$ takes all the values in the range $f'(a)$ to $f'(b)$, the $f'(x)$ must be continuous on $[a,b]$ (something like reverse IVT).
>
>But this is wrong. $f$ is differentiable on $[a,b]$ means that $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, but does not mean that $f'$ is also continuous on $[a,b]$; it just means that $f'$ exists for all $x$ in $[a,b]$.
>
>If $f'$ is not continuous on $[a,b]$, then why does it take all values in the range $f'(a)$, $f'(b)$? Well, that is because if $f'$ is discontinuous on $[a,b]$ then it won't have jump discontinuity, so it won't skip any value even if it doesn't converge at limits.

##### Example
This function shows us how the function differentiable on an interval can have derivative that is not continuous.
$$
f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 \sin(1/x), & x \neq 0 \\ 0, & x = 0 \end{cases}
$$
![](Pasted%20image%2020260921125019.png)
**Solution:** The function is differentiable at all points $\neq 0$ as we can see from definition.

At $x = 0$, we have:
$$
f'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h^2 \sin(1/h) - 0}{h} = 0
$$
Hence the function is differentiable at $x = 0$ as well, this means that the function is differentiable $\forall x \in \mathbb{R}$.

We will now find the definition of $f'(x)$, $\forall x \neq 0$ using product and chain rule.
$$
f'(x) = 2x \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) + x^2 \cos\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\left(-\frac{1}{x^2}\right)
$$
$$
f'(x) = \begin{cases} 2x\sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) - \cos\left(\frac{1}{x}\right), & x \neq 0 \\ 0, & x = 0 \end{cases}
$$
Clearly, $\lim_{x \to 0} f'(x)$ does not exist as $\cos(1/x)$ doesn't converge to any value even though $\lim_{x \to 0} 2x\sin(1/x) = 0$.

But, we see that this is oscillating discontinuity and not jump discontinuity.

##### Exercise 58
**(i)** Let $f(x)$ be a function satisfying $|f(x)| \leq x^2$ for $-1 \leq x \leq 1$. Show that $f$ is differentiable at $x = 0$ & find $f'(0)$.

**Solution:** Given $|f(x)| \leq x^{2}$,
$$
\begin{align}
\implies -x^2  & \leq f(x) \leq x^2  \\
\text{ if } \quad x > 0  & \implies -x \leq \dfrac{f(x)}{x} \leq x \\
 & (or) \\
\text{if} \quad x < 0  & \implies x \leq \dfrac{f(x)}{x} \leq -x
\end{align}
$$
This means that $\dfrac{f(x)}{x}$ is always between $-x$ & $x$, hence using the sandwich theorem we have,
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \dfrac{f(x)}{x} = 0
$$
Now, $f'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(h) - f(0)}{h}$, $f(0) = 0$ since $x^2 = 0$ at $x = 0$
$$
\implies f'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(h)}{h} = 0
$$
Hence, $f$ is differentiable at $x = 0$ & $f'(0) = 0$

#### Weierstrass Nowhere Differentiable Function

For a long time people believed that if a function is continuous everywhere, then it must be differentiable at most of the points in its domain.

But Weierstrass created a function that says otherwise.
$$
f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a^n \cos(b^n \pi x)
$$
In this, $0 < a < 1$ makes sure that the sum converges & the function is continuous, but $b > 1$ makes the frequency higher as $n$ increases, making the function wiggle extremely fast.

This function is continuous everywhere, but differentiable nowhere.

# 2.2 Differentiation Rules

### Powers, Multiples, Sums and Differences

##### Rule 1: Derivative of a Constant
If $c$ is a constant, then $\dfrac{d}{dx}(c) = 0$

**Proof:** This can be proved very easily using the derivative definition.
$$
f'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}(c) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(c+h) - f(c)}{h}
$$
$$
\implies \frac{d}{dx}(c) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{c - c}{h} = 0
$$
##### Rule 2: Power Rule for Positive Integers
If $n$ is a positive integer, then $\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^n) = nx^{n-1}$

**Proof:** Again using the definition on $f(x) = x^n$ we get
$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^n - x^n}{h}
$$
$$
= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x^n + {}^nC_1 x^{n-1}h + {}^nC_2 x^{n-2}h^2 + \cdots + h^n - x^n}{h}
$$
$$
= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h\left({}^nC_1 x^{n-1} + {}^nC_2 x^{n-2}h + \cdots + h^{n-1}\right)}{h} = nx^{n-1}
$$
##### Rule 3: The Constant Multiple Rule
If $u$ is a differentiable function of $x$, and $c$ is a constant, then
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(cu) = c\frac{du}{dx}
$$
**Proof:**
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(cu(x)) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{c(u(x+h)) - cu(x)}{h}
$$
$$
= c\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{u(x+h) - u(x)}{h} = c\frac{du}{dx}
$$
##### Rule 4: The Sum Rule
If $u$ and $v$ are differentiable functions of $x$, then their sum $u+v$ is also differentiable at every point where $u$ and $v$ are both differentiable. At such points,
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(u+v) = \frac{du}{dx} + \frac{dv}{dx}
$$
**Proof:**
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(u+v) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{u(x+h) + v(x+h) - (u(x) + v(x))}{h}
$$
$$
= \lim_{h \to 0} \left( \frac{u(x+h) - u(x)}{h} + \frac{v(x+h) - v(x)}{h} \right)
$$
$$
= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{u(x+h) - u(x)}{h} + \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{v(x+h) - v(x)}{h}
$$
$$
= \frac{du}{dx} + \frac{dv}{dx}
$$
>[!note]
> 1. Using constant rule with this we can prove this for difference of two functions also.
> $$
> \frac{d}{dx}(u-v) = \frac{du}{dx} - \frac{dv}{dx}
> $$
> 2. Using induction we can prove that this is true for sum of any $n$ differentiable functions.
> $$
> \frac{d}{dx}(u_1+u_2+\cdots+u_n) = \frac{du_1}{dx} + \frac{du_2}{dx} + \cdots + \frac{du_n}{dx}
> $$
##### Rule 5: The Product Rule
If $u$ and $v$ are differentiable at $x$, then so is their product $uv$, and
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(uv) = u\frac{dv}{dx} + v\frac{du}{dx}
$$
**Proof:**
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(uv) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{u(x+h)v(x+h) - u(x)v(x)}{h}
$$
$$
= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{u(x+h)v(x+h) - u(x+h)v(x) + v(x)u(x+h) - v(x)u(x)}{h}
$$
$$
= \lim_{h \to 0} \left[ u(x+h)\left(\frac{v(x+h)-v(x)}{h}\right) + v(x)\left(\frac{u(x+h)-u(x)}{h}\right) \right]
$$
$$
= \lim_{h \to 0} u(x+h) \cdot \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{v(x+h)-v(x)}{h} + v(x) \cdot \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{u(x+h)-u(x)}{h}
$$
Now, since $u$ is differentiable at $x$, it is continuous at $x$, and hence
$$
\lim_{h \to 0} u(x+h) = u(x)
$$
$$
\implies \frac{d}{dx}(uv) = u(x)\frac{dv}{dx} + v(x)\frac{du}{dx}
$$
##### Rule 6: The Quotient Rule
If $u$ and $v$ are differentiable at $x$ and $v(x) \neq 0$, then the quotient $u/v$ is also differentiable at $x$, and
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{u}{v}\right) = \frac{v\frac{du}{dx} - u\frac{dv}{dx}}{v^2}
$$
**Proof:**
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{u}{v}\right) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\frac{u(x+h)}{v(x+h)} - \frac{u(x)}{v(x)}}{h}
$$
$$
= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{u(x+h)v(x) - v(x+h)u(x)}{v(x+h)v(x)h}
$$
$$
= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{u(x+h)v(x) - v(x)u(x) + v(x)u(x) - v(x+h)u(x)}{v(x+h)v(x)h}
$$
$$
= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{v(x)(u(x+h)-u(x)) - u(x)(v(x+h)-v(x))}{v(x+h)(v(x))h}
$$
$$
= \frac{v(x)\lim_{h\to 0}\left(\frac{u(x+h)-u(x)}{h}\right) - u(x)\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{v(x+h)-v(x)}{h}}{v(x) \cdot \lim_{h\to 0} v(x+h)}
$$
Again $v(x)$ is differentiable at $x \implies v(x)$ is continuous at $x \implies \lim_{h\to 0} v(x+h) = v(x)$
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dx}(uv) = \frac{v\frac{du}{dx} - u\frac{dv}{dx}}{v^2}}
$$
#### Rule 7: Power Rule for Negative Integers

If $n$ is a negative integer and $x \neq 0$, then
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(x^n) = nx^{n-1}
$$
**Proof:** Let $m = -n \implies m > 0$ & $m$ is positive integer.
$$
\frac{d}{dx}x^n = \frac{d}{dx}x^{-m} = \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{1}{x^m}\right) = \frac{x^m\frac{d}{dx}(1) - 1\frac{d}{dx}x^m}{x^{2m}}
$$
$$
= \frac{0 - mx^{m-1}}{x^{2m}} = -mx^{m-1-2m} = -mx^{-m-1}
$$
But $-m = n$, hence we get
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1}}
$$

### Second and Higher Order Derivatives

The derivative $y' = \dfrac{dy}{dx}$ is the first order derivative of $y$ with respect to $x$. This derivative may itself be a differentiable function of $x$; if so, its derivative
$$
y'' = \frac{dy'}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right) = \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}
$$
is called the second order derivative of $y$ with respect to $x$.

If $y''$ is also differentiable then its derivative
$$
y''' = \frac{dy''}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\right) = \frac{d^3y}{dx^3}
$$
is called the third order derivative of $y$ with respect to $x$.

In this way we can continue, and in general we write
$$
y^{(n)} = \frac{d}{dx}y^{(n-1)}
$$
denoting the $n$th order derivative of $y$ with respect to $x$, for any positive integer $n$.

##### Example
Find the first 4 derivatives of $y = x^3 - 3x^2 + 2$

**Solution:** $y' = 3x^2 - 6x$, $y'' = 6x - 6$, $y''' = 6$, $y^{(4)} = 0$

$\implies y^{(5)}, y^{(6)}, \ldots$ also exist, but they are all 0.

##### Exercise 54: Generalizing the Product Rule
The Product Rule gives the formula
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(uv) = u\frac{dv}{dx} + v\frac{du}{dx}
$$
for the derivative of the product $uv$ of two differentiable functions of $x$.

**(a)** What is the analogous formula for the derivative of the product $uvw$ of three differentiable functions of $x$?

**(b)** What is the formula for the derivative of a product $u_1 u_2 u_3 \cdots u_n$ of a finite number $n$ of differentiable functions of $x$?

**Solution:**

**(a)** We will take
$$
\frac{d}{dx}((uv)w) = uv\frac{dw}{dx} + w\frac{d(uv)}{dx}
$$
$$
= uv\frac{dw}{dx} + w\left(u\frac{dv}{dx} + v\frac{du}{dx}\right) = uv\frac{dw}{dx} + vw\frac{du}{dx} + wu\frac{dv}{dx}
$$
**(b)** If the induction hypothesis is $A(n): \dfrac{d}{dx}(u_1 u_2 \cdots u_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\prod_{j\neq i} u_j\right)\dfrac{du_i}{dx}$, then the assertion is true for $n = 2, 3$.

Let the assertion be true for some arbitrary $n$, then we have
$$
A(n+1): \frac{d}{dx}(u_1 u_2 \cdots u_{n+1}) = u_1\cdots u_n \frac{du_{n+1}}{dx} + u_{n+1}\frac{d}{dx}(u_1u_2\cdots u_n)
$$
Now, we can use the fact that the assertion is true for $n$
$$
\implies \frac{d}{dx}(u_1u_2\cdots u_{n+1}) = (u_1u_2\cdots u_n)\frac{du_{n+1}}{dx} + u_{n+1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\prod_{j\neq i}u_j\right)\frac{du_i}{dx}\right)
$$
$$
\implies \frac{d}{dx}(u_1u_2\cdots u_{n+1}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\left(\prod_{j\neq i}u_j\right)\frac{du_i}{dx}
$$
Hence, the assertion is true for $n+1$, this means that by mathematical induction the assertion is true for all $n \geq 2$.

##### Exercise 55: Rational Powers

**(a)** Find $\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^{3/2})$ by writing $x^{3/2} = x \cdot x^{1/2}$ and using the product rule.

**(b)** Do the same to find $\dfrac{d}{dx}x^{5/2}$, $\dfrac{d}{dx}x^{7/2}$. What pattern do you see?

**Solution:**

**(a)**
$$
\frac{d}{dx}x^{3/2} = \frac{d}{dx}x \cdot x^{1/2} = x\frac{d}{dx}x^{1/2} + x^{1/2}\frac{d}{dx}(x)
$$
$$
= x \cdot \frac{1}{2x^{1/2}} + x^{1/2} \cdot 1 = \frac{x^{1/2}}{2} + x^{1/2} = \frac{3}{2}x^{1/2}
$$
**(b)** Similarly, we will find that

$$
$\implies$ The trend that we see here is $\dfrac{d}{dx}x^{p/q} = \dfrac{p}{q}x^{p/q - 1}$

\text{arc } AP = |\theta|, \quad OQ = \cos\theta \implies AQ = 1-\cos\theta, \quad PQ = \sin\theta
$$
Now we know that arc length $AP > |AP|$
$$
\implies AP^2 < \theta^2 \implies \sin^2\theta + (1-\cos\theta)^2 < \theta^2
$$
$$
\implies \sin^2\theta < \theta^2 \quad \& \quad (1-\cos\theta)^2 < \theta^2
$$
$$
\implies -|\theta| < \sin\theta < |\theta| \quad \& \quad -|\theta| < |1-\cos\theta| < |\theta|
$$
##### Example
Show that $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$ are continuous at $\theta = 0$. That is
$$
\lim_{\theta \to 0} \sin\theta = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{\theta \to 0} \cos\theta = 1
$$
**Solution:** For this we will simply use the result of prev. theorem.
$$
-|\theta| < \sin\theta < |\theta| \implies \lim_{\theta \to 0} \sin\theta = 0 \quad \text{ by sandwich theorem}
$$
Also,
$$
-|\theta| < 1-\cos\theta < |\theta| \implies \lim_{\theta \to 0}(1-\cos\theta) = 0 \implies \lim_{\theta \to 0}\cos\theta = 1
$$
Hence, both $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$ are continuous at $\theta = 0$.

##### Theorem 4
$$
\lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 1 \quad (\theta \text{ in radians})
$$
**Proof:** Again consider the unit circle diagram shown to the right. The different measurements are
![](Pasted%20image%2020260921145253.png)
area sector $OAP = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta = \frac{\theta}{2}$

Now, from the figure we know that

area $\triangle OPQ <$ area sector $OAP <$ area $\triangle OAT$

\implies \frac{\sin\theta}{2} < \frac{\theta}{2} < \frac{1}{2}\tan\theta
$$
In the first quadrant $\sin\theta > 0$, hence dividing the inequality by $\frac{1}{2}\sin\theta$ we get
$$
1 < \frac{\theta}{\sin\theta} < \frac{1}{\cos\theta} \implies \cos\theta < \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} < 1
$$
Now, using the sandwich theorem we see that
$$
\lim_{x \to 0^+} \cos\theta = 1, \quad \lim_{x \to 0^+} 1 = 1 \implies \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 1
$$
For $\lim_{x \to 0^-} \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\theta}$, we see use the fact that $\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\theta}$ is even function around neighbourhood of $0$, this means that its limit as $x \to 0^+$ is same as its limit as $x \to 0^-$
$$
\implies \lim_{x \to 0^-} \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 1 \implies \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 1, \text{ Hence Proved.}

##### Example
Show that $\lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{\cos h - 1}{h} = 0$

$$

$$

### The Derivative of Sine

Let $y = \sin x$, then
$$
\begin{align}
\dfrac{dy}{dx} & = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{\sin(x+h) - \sin x}{h} \\
\frac{dy}{dx} & = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin x \cos h + \cos x \sin h - \sin x}{h} \\
 & = \lim_{h \to 0} \sin x \frac{(\cos h - 1)}{h} + \cos x \frac{\sinh}{h} \\
 & = 0 + \cos x \cdot 1 \\
 & = \cos x
\end{align}
$$
$$
\implies \boxed{\frac{d}{dx}(\sin x) = \cos x}
$$
### The Derivative of Cosine

Let $y = \cos x$, then
$$
\begin{align}
\dfrac{dy}{dx} & = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{\cos(x+h) - \cos x}{h} \\
\frac{dy}{dx} & = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\cos x \cos h - \sin x \sin h - \cos x}{h} \\
 & = \lim_{h \to 0} \cos x \frac{(\cos h - 1)}{h} + (-\sin x)\frac{\sinh}{h} \\
 & = -\sin x
\end{align}
$$
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dx}(\cos x) = -\sin x}
$$

### The Derivatives of Other Basic Functions

The derivative of other trigonometric ratios can be calculated using Quotient Rule.
##### (i) $\dfrac{d}{dx}(\tan x) = \sec^2 x$

**Solution:**
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(\tan x) = \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\right) = \frac{\cos x\frac{d}{dx}\sin x - \sin x\frac{d}{dx}\cos x}{\cos^2 x}
$$
$$
= \frac{\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x} = \frac{1}{\cos^2 x} = \sec^2 x
$$
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dx}(\tan x) = \sec^2 x}
$$
##### (ii) $\dfrac{d}{dx}(\sec x) = \sec x \tan x$

**Solution:**
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(\sec x) = \frac{d}{dx}\frac{1}{\cos x} = \frac{-1}{\cos^2 x}(-\sin x) = \sec x \tan x
$$
##### (iii) $\dfrac{d}{dx}\cot x = -\csc^2 x$

**Solution:**
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(\cot x) = \frac{d}{dx}\frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \frac{\sin x(-\sin x) - \cos x \cdot \cos x}{\sin^2 x}
$$
$$
= -\frac{(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x)}{\sin^2 x} = -\csc^2 x
$$
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dx}(\cot x) = -\csc^2 x}
$$
##### (iv) $\dfrac{d}{dx}(\csc x) = -\csc x \cot x$

**Solution:**
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(\csc x) = \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{1}{\sin x}\right) = \frac{-1}{\sin^2 x}\cos x = -\csc x \cot x
$$
>[!note]
>Hence, the six basic trigonometric functions are differentiable throughout their domains.

### Continuity of Trigonometric Functions

Since each of the 6 basic trigonometric functions are differentiable in their domains, this means that by Theorem 1 they are also continuous in their domains.

(i) $\sin x, \cos x$ are continuous $\forall x \in \mathbb{R}$

(ii) $\sec x, \tan x$ are continuous $\forall x \in \mathbb{R} - \left\{\dfrac{(2n+1)\pi}{2}, n \in \mathbb{Z}\right\}$

(iii) $\csc x, \cot x$ are continuous $\forall x \in \mathbb{R} - \{n\pi, n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$

# 2.5 The Chain Rule

##### Theorem 5
If $f(u)$ is differentiable at the point $u = g(x)$, and $g(x)$ is differentiable at $x$, then the composite function $(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$ is differentiable at $x$ and
$$
(f \circ g)'(x) = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)
$$
In Leibniz notation, if $y = f(u)$ & $u = g(x)$, then
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}
$$
where $dy/du$ is evaluated at $u = g(x)$

>[!note]
The proof of chain rule we will see in next chapter section 3.7.

##### Example
Find the derivative of $y = \sqrt{x^2+1}$, $y = \sin(x^2 - \cos x)$

**Solution:**
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(\sqrt{x^2+1}) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x^2+1}} \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(x^2+1) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x^2+1}} \cdot 2x = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}
$$
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(\sin(x^2-\cos x)) = \cos(x^2-\cos x) \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(x^2-\cos x)
$$
$$
= \cos(x^2-\cos x) \cdot (2x+\sin x)
$$

### Power Chain Rule

If $u(x)$ is a differentiable function and $n$ is an integer, then $u^n$ is differentiable and
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(u^n) = nu^{n-1} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}
$$
##### Example
Evaluate $\dfrac{d}{dx}(\sin^5 x)$, $\dfrac{d}{dx}(2x+1)^{-3}$

**Solution:**
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(\sin^5 x) = 5\sin^4 x \cdot \cos x
$$
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(2x+1)^{-3} = -3(2x+1)^{-4} \cdot (2) = -6(2x+1)^{-4}
$$

##### Example: The Melting Ice Cube
An ice cube has volume $V_0$ at time $t = 0$, let $V$ and $s$ be it's volume and side length as a function of time. Its volume after 1 hour is $\dfrac{3}{4}V_0$. Assuming that the cube remains cubical throughout the melting process, we get the rate of melting $\dfrac{dV}{dt} = -k(6s^2)$. Find how long will it take for the ice to completely melt.

**Solution:** Both $V$ and $s$ are changing with time, hence they both are functions of time.
$$
\frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}(s^3) = 3s^2\frac{ds}{dt}, \text{ comparing this with } \frac{dV}{dt} = -k(6s^2)
$$
we get $\dfrac{ds}{dt} = -2k$.

Hence, the rate of change of $s$ is constant and decreases by $2k$ every hour.
$$
\implies s_0 - s_1 = 2k, \text{ also time after which } s = 0 \text{ is } t = \frac{s_0}{2k}
$$
$$
\implies t = \frac{s_0}{s_0-s_1} = \frac{1}{1-\frac{s_1}{s_0}}, \quad \frac{s_1}{s_0} = \frac{\left(\frac{3}{4}V_0\right)^{1/3}}{V_0^{1/3}} = \left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{1/3} \approx 0.91
$$
$$
\implies t_{melt} = \frac{1}{1-0.91} \approx 11 \text{ hours}
$$
Hence, it will take 10 more hours for the remaining ice to melt.

## 2.6 Implicit Differentiation and Rational Exponents

Many times we cannot convert a function of the form $F(x,y) = 0$ to something of the form $y = f(x)$.

**Example:** $x^3 + y^3 - 9xy = 0$
![](Pasted%20image%2020260921153033.png)
But if we look at the graph of this function we see that the function is differentiable everywhere except at $O$ & $A$.

To find the derivative of such function we consider $y$ to be a function of $x$ and then differentiate the function $F(x,y)$ w.r.t $x$ and then get an equation of the form $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = F'(x,y)$.
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(x^3+y^3-9xy) = 0 \implies 3x^2+3y^2\frac{dy}{dx} - 9\left(x\frac{dy}{dx}+y\right) = 0
$$
$$
\implies 3x^2 - 9y + (3y^2-9x)\frac{dy}{dx} = 0 \implies \boxed{\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{9y-3x^2}{3y^2-9x}}
$$
>[!note]
>From the graph of the function, we see that it is not a single function, but a union of 3 different differentiable functions which is clear by the vertical line that intersects with the graph at 3 different points.
>
>But the issue is that the functions are very hard to separate and hence the implicit differentiation method gives a better way to find slopes of this graph at a point $(x,y)$ that we know is on the graph.

##### Example
Find the slope of the circle $x^2+y^2 = 25$ at the point $(3,-4)$.

**Solution:** Differentiating on both sides w.r.t $x$, we get
$$
2x + 2y\frac{dy}{dx} = 0 \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{-x}{y} = \frac{-3}{-4} = \frac{3}{4}
$$
Hence, the slope of tangent to the circle at $(3,-4)$ is $\dfrac{3}{4}$.

>[!note]
>For equations of the form $y^2 = x$, $x^2+y^2=25$, $y^2-2xy+3x^2=0$ etc. we have the quadratic formula to separate out the different differentiable functions.
>
>For cubic equation like $x^3+y^3-9xy=0$, there exist complicated formula for finding solutions. Same for biquadratic equations.
>
>But for equations of degrees 5 or higher no general formula exists and there we need to use the implicit method to find slopes.

# Lines, Tangents, and Normal Lines

##### Normal Definition
A line is normal to a curve at a point if it is perpendicular to the curve's tangent there. The line is called the normal to the curve at that point.

##### Example
Find the tangent and normal to the curve $x^2-xy+y^2=7$ at the point $(-1,2)$.

**Solution:** First we need to find the slopes of tangent and normal for which we will use implicit differentiation to get $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(x^2-xy+y^2) = \frac{d}{dx}(7) \implies 2x - x\frac{dy}{dx} - y + 2y\frac{dy}{dx} = 0
$$
$$
(2x-y) - (x-2y)\frac{dy}{dx} = 0 \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2x-y}{x-2y}
$$
Slope of tangent $= \dfrac{2(-1)-2}{(-1)-2(2)} = \dfrac{-4}{-5} = \dfrac{4}{5}$

Slope of normal $= -\dfrac{5}{4}$

Equation of tangent $\implies \dfrac{y-2}{x+1} = \dfrac{4}{5} \implies 5y-10 = 4x+4$
$$
\implies \boxed{4x - 5y + 14 = 0}
$$
Equation of normal $\implies \dfrac{y-2}{x+1} = -\dfrac{5}{4}$
$$
\implies 5x+4y-3=0
$$
>[!note]
>The equation $x^2-xy+y^2=7$ is equation of ellipse and in real world such equations are often used to describe the surfaces of lenses. In those cases using the implicit method to find the eq. of tangents and normals is very helpful.

### Using Implicit Diff. to Find Derivatives of Higher Order

##### Example
Find $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ if $2x^3-3y^2=7$.

**Solution:**
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(2x^3-3y^2) = \frac{d}{dx}(7) \implies 6x^2 - 6y\frac{dy}{dx} = 0
$$
$$
\implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{x^2}{y} \implies y' = \frac{x^2}{y} \implies y'' = \frac{y\frac{d}{dx}(x^2) - x^2\frac{dy}{dx}}{y^2}
$$
$$
y'' = \frac{y(2x) - x^2\left(\frac{x^2}{y}\right)}{y^2} = \frac{2x}{y} - \frac{x^4}{y^3}
$$
$$
\boxed{\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = y'' = \frac{2x}{y} - \frac{x^4}{y^3}}
$$

### Rational Powers of Differentiable Functions

##### Theorem 6
If $n$ is a rational number, then $x^n$ is differentiable at every interior point $x$ of the domain of $x^{n-1}$, and
$$
\frac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1}
$$
**Proof:** Let $n = \dfrac{p}{q}$, then we have $y = x^n = x^{p/q} = (x^p)^{1/q}$
$$
\implies y^q = x^p, \text{ using implicit differentiation we get}
$$
$$
qy^{q-1}\frac{dy}{dx} = px^{p-1} \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{p}{q}\frac{x^{p-1}}{(x^{p/q})^{q-1}} = \frac{p}{q}\frac{x^{p-1}}{x^{p-\frac{p}{q}}}
$$
$$
\implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{p}{q}x^{p/q - 1} = nx^{n-1} \implies \boxed{\frac{dy}{dx} = nx^{n-1}}
$$
>[!note]
>**(i)** The domain of $x^{n-1} \subseteq$ domain of $x^n$. And in most cases they are equal, the function $x^n$ is differentiable when $x$ is in domain of $x^n$ & $x^{n-1}$, which essentially is domain of $x^{n-1}$.
>
>**(ii)** **Power Rule + Chain Rule:** If $n$ is any rational number, and $u$ be a function differentiable at $x$, and $(u(x))^{n-1}$ is defined at $x$, then $u^n$ is differentiable at $x$, and
>$$
>\frac{d}{dx}u^n = nu^{n-1}\frac{du}{dx}
>$$

##### Example
Evaluate $\dfrac{d}{dx}(1-x^2)^{1/4}$, $\dfrac{d}{dx}(\cos x)^{1/5}$.

**Solution:**
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(1-x^2)^{1/4} = \frac{1}{4}(1-x^2)^{-3/4} \cdot (-2x) = \frac{-x}{2(1-x^2)^{3/4}}
$$
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(\cos x)^{-1/5} = -\frac{1}{5}(\cos x)^{-6/5}(-\sin x) = \frac{\sin x}{5(\cos x)^{6/5}}
$$

# 2.7 Related Rates of Change

Here we learn to solve problems that ask us to calculate a rate that we cannot measure directly from a rate that we can.

##### Strategy for Solving Related Rate Problems

1. Draw a picture and name the variables and constants. Use $t$ for time, assume all variables are differentiable functions of $t$.

2. Write down the numerical information (in terms of the symbols you have chosen).

3. Write down what you are asked to find (usually a rate, expressed as a derivative).

4. Write an equation that relates the variables. You may have to combine two or more equations to get a single equation that relates the variable whose rate you want to the variable whose rate you know.

5. Differentiate with respect to $t$. Then express the rate you want in terms of the rate and variables whose values you know.

6. Evaluate. Use the known values to find the unknown rate.

= \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\left(u^{(n-k+1)}\cdot v^{(k)} + u^{(n-k)}\cdot v^{(k+1)}\right)
$$
$$
= \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}u^{(n+1-k)}\cdot v^{(k)} + \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}u^{(n-k)}v^{(k+1)}
$$
$$
= u^{(n+1)}v + \sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n}{k}u^{(n+1-k)}\cdot v^{(k)} + \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k}u^{(n-k)}v^{(k+1)} + u\cdot v^{(n+1)}
$$
$$
= u^{(n+1)}\cdot v + \sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n}{k}u^{(n+1-k)}\cdot v^{(k)} + \sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n}{k-1}u^{(n+1-k)}\cdot v^{(k)} + u\cdot v^{(n+1)}
$$
$$
= u^{(n+1)}\cdot v + \sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[\binom{n}{k}+\binom{n}{k-1}\right]u^{(n+1-k)}\cdot v^{(k)} + u\cdot v^{(n+1)}
$$
$$
= u^{(n+1)}\cdot v + \sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n+1}{k}u^{(n+1-k)}\cdot v^{(k)} + u\cdot v^{(n+1)}
$$
$$
= \sum_{k=0}^{n+1}\binom{n+1}{k}u^{(n+1-k)}\cdot v^{(k)}
$$
Hence, the assertion is true for $A(n+1)$.

Hence, the formula is true for all $n$ by the principle of mathematical induction.
