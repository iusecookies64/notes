# 8.1 Limits of Sequences of Numbers

Informally, a **sequence** is an ordered list of things. In calculus and analysis, the items in this list are almost always numbers—such as the sequence of approximations $x_0, x_1, \dots, x_n, \dots$ generated by Newton's method, or the sequence of perimeters and areas associated with the polygons $c_1, c_2, \dots, c_n, \dots$ that define Helga von Koch's snowflake. Some sequences settle down toward a definite limiting value as we move further down the list, while many equally important sequences do not. Understanding what it means for an infinite list of numbers to approach a limit is the foundation for all of infinite series.

## Definitions and Notation

How do we make the informal notion of an "ordered list of numbers" mathematically precise? Suppose we want to list the positive integer multiples of $3$ in order. We can do this by assigning each multiple a numbered position:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Domain:} \quad & 1 & & 2 & & 3 & & \dots & & n & & \dots \\
& \downarrow & & \downarrow & & \downarrow & & & & \downarrow & & \\
\text{Range:} \quad & 3 & & 6 & & 9 & & \dots & & 3n & & \dots
\end{aligned}
$$

The first number is $3$, the second is $6$, the third is $9$, and so on. Notice what is happening underneath the hood: the assignment is simply a *function* that assigns the value $3n$ to the $n$th place. That is the fundamental idea behind constructing any sequence—there is a function that tells us which number belongs in each integer position.

> [!abstract] Definition: Infinite Sequence of Numbers
> An **infinite sequence** (or **sequence**) of numbers is a function whose domain is the set of integers greater than or equal to some integer $n_0$.

Usually, the starting index is $n_0 = 1$, which means the domain of the sequence is the set of positive integers $\{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$. However, depending on the context, it is often convenient to start sequences elsewhere. For instance, we typically take $n_0 = 0$ when indexing the initial guess $x_0$ in Newton's method, and we might take $n_0 = 3$ if we are defining a sequence of $n$-sided regular polygons (since a polygon must have at least $3$ sides).

### Defining Rules and Subscript Notation

Because sequences are functions, they are defined by rules just like other functions, such as:

$$
a(n) = \sqrt{n}, \qquad a(n) = (-1)^{n+1}\frac{1}{n}, \qquad a(n) = \frac{n - 1}{n}
$$

To remind ourselves that the domain consists of integers rather than a continuous interval of real numbers, we customarily use a letter like $n$ (or $k$, $m$, $i$) from the middle of the alphabet for the independent variable, instead of the variables $x$, $y$, $z$, and $t$ used widely in continuous contexts. Nevertheless, the algebraic formulas appearing in the defining rules are often valid for domains much larger than the set of positive integers (for example, $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ makes sense for all $x \ge 0$), which turns out to be a major advantage when applying the tools of differential calculus to sequences.

The output number $a(n)$ is called the **$n$th term** of the sequence, or the **term with index $n$**. For instance, if $a(n) = \frac{n - 1}{n}$, then the terms are:

| First term | Second term | Third term | $\dots$ | $n$th term |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $a(1) = 0$ | $a(2) = \frac{1}{2}$ | $a(3) = \frac{2}{3}$ | $\dots$ | $a(n) = \frac{n - 1}{n}$ |

In practice, instead of writing function parentheses $a(n)$, we almost always use **subscript notation** $a_n$ to denote the $n$th term. In subscript notation, the sequence above is written as:

$$
a_1 = 0, \qquad a_2 = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad a_3 = \frac{2}{3}, \qquad \dots, \qquad a_n = \frac{n - 1}{n}
$$

To describe a sequence clearly, we often write out the first few terms followed by the general formula for the $n$th term (e.g., $1, \sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}, \sqrt{4}, \dots, \sqrt{n}, \dots$). Compactly, we refer to the entire sequence whose $n$th term is $a_n$ using brace notation $\{a_n\}$ (read "the sequence $a$ sub $n$"). For example, $\{1/n\}$ denotes "the sequence $1$ over $n$," and $\{3\}$ denotes "the constant sequence $3$" (whose terms are $3, 3, 3, \dots$, with $a_n = 3$ for every $n$).

### Visualizing Sequences

How can we picture the behavior of a sequence $\{a_n\}$? Because a sequence assigns a real value $a_n$ to each integer $n$, we can graph a sequence in two complementary ways:

1. **On a horizontal number line:** By plotting the values $a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots, a_n, \dots$ directly as points on a single real axis, which lets us see whether the terms cluster around a specific location.
2. **In the coordinate plane:** By plotting the discrete points $(1, a_1), (2, a_2), \dots, (n, a_n), \dots$ in the $n a_n$-plane, where the horizontal axis represents the index $n$ and the vertical axis represents the term value $a_n$.

## Convergence and Divergence

When we inspect the graphs of different sequences as the index $n$ grows larger, we see strikingly different long-term behaviors:

- Some sequences approach a single limiting value steadily or alternately. For example, the terms $a_n = 1/n$ decrease steadily toward $0$, the terms $a_n = (-1)^{n+1}(1/n)$ alternate in sign while still getting arbitrarily close to $0$, the terms $a_n = (n-1)/n$ approach $1$ steadily from below, and the constant sequence $a_n = 3$ sits at the value $3$ from the very first term.
- Other sequences fail to settle on a single value. For instance, the terms of $a_n = (-1)^{n+1}\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)$ alternate in sign so that the positive terms approach $1$ while the negative terms approach $-1$; the terms accumulate near two different values rather than one. Meanwhile, the terms of $a_n = \sqrt{n}$ grow larger without bound, eventually surpassing every integer and never accumulating anywhere.

To distinguish sequences that approach a unique limiting value $L$ as $n$ increases from those that do not, we say that the former sequences **converge**, captured rigorously by the following $\epsilon$-$N$ definition.

> [!abstract] Definition: Convergence, Divergence, and Limit of a Sequence
> The sequence $\{a_n\}$ **converges** to the number $L$ if to every positive number $\epsilon$ there corresponds an integer $N$ such that for all $n$,
> $$
> n > N \implies |a_n - L| < \epsilon
> $$
> If no such number $L$ exists, we say that $\{a_n\}$ **diverges**.
>
> If $\{a_n\}$ converges to $L$, we write $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L$, or simply $a_n \to L$, and call $L$ the **limit** of the sequence.

Let's unpack what this formal definition is saying geometrically. The inequality $|a_n - L| < \epsilon$ is equivalent to $L - \epsilon < a_n < L + \epsilon$.
- On the **number line**, this means that for any tolerance $\epsilon > 0$ (no matter how tiny), there is some point in the sequence—indexed by $N$—after which *every single subsequent term* $a_n$ (for $n > N$) stays trapped inside the open interval $(L - \epsilon, L + \epsilon)$ around $L$.
- In the **coordinate plane**, $a_n \to L$ means that the horizontal line $y = L$ is a horizontal asymptote of the sequence of points $\{(n, a_n)\}$. Once we pass $n = N$, all plotted points $(n, a_n)$ lie strictly inside the horizontal band bounded by the lines $y = L - \epsilon$ and $y = L + \epsilon$.

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: Figure 8.2 - a_n -> L if y = L is a horizontal asymptote of the sequence of points {(n, a_n)}. All the a_n's after a_N lie within epsilon of L

We can use this definition directly to establish two fundamental building-block limits:
1. **Reciprocal Sequence ($\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} = 0$):** Given any $\epsilon > 0$, we need an integer $N$ such that $n > N \implies \left|\frac{1}{n} - 0\right| < \epsilon$. Since $n$ is positive, $\frac{1}{n} < \epsilon$ is equivalent to $n > \frac{1}{\epsilon}$. Therefore, choosing $N$ to be any integer greater than $1/\epsilon$ guarantees that all $n > N$ satisfy $\left|\frac{1}{n} - 0\right| < \epsilon$, proving $\lim_{n \to \infty} (1/n) = 0$.
2. **Constant Sequence ($\lim_{n \to \infty} k = k$):** Given any $\epsilon > 0$, we need $n > N \implies |k - k| < \epsilon$. Since $|k - k| = 0 < \epsilon$ is automatically true for every $n$ whenever $\epsilon > 0$, any positive integer $N$ works, proving $\lim_{n \to \infty} k = k$ for any constant $k$.

Conversely, why does a sequence that oscillates between two values, such as $a_n = (-1)^{n+1}\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)$, fail the definition? Suppose we test whether it can converge to $1$ or $-1$. If we choose a positive $\epsilon < 1$, the horizontal $\epsilon$-band around $y = 1$ (from $1 - \epsilon$ to $1 + \epsilon$) and the horizontal $\epsilon$-band around $y = -1$ (from $-1 - \epsilon$ to $-1 + \epsilon$) do not overlap. Convergence to $1$ would require *every* point of the graph beyond some index $N$ to lie inside the upper band. However, as soon as a point $(n, a_n)$ lies in the upper band, the very next point $(n+1, a_{n+1})$ is negative and jumps down into the lower band. Thus, the sequence cannot converge to $1$, nor by the same reasoning can it converge to $-1$. Because the terms get alternately closer to $1$ and $-1$, they never accumulate near any other value $L$ either, proving that the sequence diverges.

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: Figure 8.3 - A sequence whose terms alternate between approaching 1 and -1 diverges because neither the epsilon-interval about 1 nor the epsilon-interval about -1 contains a complete tail of the sequence

### Divergence to Infinity

Notice that the divergence of an oscillating sequence like $\{(-1)^{n+1}(n-1)/n\}$ is qualitatively different from the divergence of $\{\sqrt{n}\}$. The sequence $\{\sqrt{n}\}$ diverges not because it jumps back and forth between multiple values, but because its terms outgrow every real number $L$. To describe the behavior of a sequence $\{a_n\}$ whose terms become arbitrarily large and positive, we write:

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = \infty
$$

For instance, $\lim_{n \to \infty} (\sqrt{n}) = \infty$.

> [!warning] Meaning of Infinity as a Sequence Limit
> In speaking of infinity as a limit of a sequence $\{a_n\}$, we do **not** mean that the difference between $a_n$ and infinity becomes small as $n$ increases, nor do we mean that the sequence converges ($a_n$ still diverges!). Rather, $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = \infty$ is a shorthand notation meaning that $a_n$ becomes numerically large—surpassing any finite threshold—as $n$ increases.

## Recursive Definitions

So far, we have calculated each term $a_n$ directly from the value of its index $n$ using an explicit formula. However, in many mathematical settings—especially in computer programs and numerical routines for solving differential equations—sequences are defined **recursively** by specifying how each new term is built from previous ones.

> [!abstract] Definition: Recursive Definition and Recursion Formula
> A sequence is defined **recursively** by giving:
> 1. The value(s) of the initial term or terms, and
> 2. A rule, called a **recursion formula**, for calculating any later term from terms that precede it.

Depending on how many preceding terms the recursion formula references, we may need one, two, or more initial terms to get the process started:
- **Single-step recursion:** Specifying $a_1 = 1$ and the recursion formula $a_n = a_{n-1} + 1$ (for $n \ge 2$) generates the sequence of positive integers $1, 2, 3, \dots, n, \dots$, since $a_2 = a_1 + 1 = 2$, $a_3 = a_2 + 1 = 3$, and so forth. Similarly, Newton's method defines a sequence recursively from an initial guess $x_0$ via $x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$.
- **Two-step recursion (Fibonacci Numbers):** Specifying two initial terms $a_1 = 1$ and $a_2 = 1$ along with the recursion formula $a_{n+1} = a_n + a_{n-1}$ (for $n \ge 2$) defines the celebrated sequence $1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, \dots$ of **Fibonacci numbers**, where each subsequent term is the sum of the two immediately preceding terms ($a_3 = 1 + 1 = 2$, $a_4 = 2 + 1 = 3$, $a_5 = 3 + 2 = 5$, and so on).

### Factorial Notation

Another vital sequence that arises naturally from a recursive construction is the sequence of **factorials**, generated by the initial condition $a_1 = 1$ and the multiplicative recursion formula $a_n = n \cdot a_{n-1}$, which produces $a_2 = 2 \cdot 1 = 2$, $a_3 = 3 \cdot 2 = 6$, $a_4 = 4 \cdot 6 = 24$, and in general $a_n = n!$.

> [!abstract] Definition: Factorial Notation ($n!$)
> For any positive integer $n$, the notation $n!$ ("$n$ factorial") means the product of the integers from $1$ to $n$:
> $$
> n! = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdots n
> $$
> By convention, we define $0!$ to be $1$:
> $$
> 0! = 1
> $$

Notice from the definition that factorials satisfy the recursive identity:

$$
(n + 1)! = (n + 1) \cdot n!
$$

For example, $4! = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 = 24$ and $5! = 5 \cdot 4! = 5 \cdot 24 = 120$. A crucial analytical feature of the factorial sequence $\{n!\}$ is its explosive growth rate: factorials grow even faster than exponential sequences like $\{e^n\}$, as illustrated by comparing their values for increasing $n$:

| $n$ | $e^n$ (rounded) | $n!$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $1$ | $3$ | $1$ |
| $5$ | $148$ | $120$ |
| $10$ | $22{,}026$ | $3{,}628{,}800$ |
| $20$ | $4.9 \times 10^8$ | $2.4 \times 10^{18}$ |

## Subsequences

Sometimes we form a new sequence by selecting only certain terms from an existing sequence while keeping them in their original relative order. For example, from the sequence of positive integers $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, \dots$, we can extract the even integers $2, 4, 6, \dots, 2n, \dots$, the odd integers $1, 3, 5, \dots, 2n-1, \dots$, or the prime numbers $2, 3, 5, 7, 11, \dots$.

> [!abstract] Definition: Subsequence
> If the terms of one sequence appear in another sequence in their given order, we call the first sequence a **subsequence** of the second.

### Importance of Subsequences and Tails

In the study of limits, subsequences are important for two primary reasons:

1. **Finding or Estimating Limits of Convergent Sequences:** If a sequence $\{a_n\}$ converges to $L$, then *all* of its subsequences also converge to that same limit $L$. Consequently, if we already know that a sequence converges, it is often much quicker to find or estimate its limit by examining a particularly simple subsequence.
2. **Proving Divergence:** If any subsequence of a sequence $\{a_n\}$ diverges, or if two subsequences converge to *different* limits, then the parent sequence $\{a_n\}$ must diverge. For example, the alternating sequence $\{(-1)^n\}$ diverges because its subsequence of odd-numbered terms $-1, -1, -1, \dots$ converges to $-1$, while its subsequence of even-numbered terms $1, 1, 1, \dots$ converges to $1$—a different limit.

Subsequences also give us a clean, intuitive way to think about the $\epsilon$-$N$ definition of convergence through the concept of a **tail**.

> [!abstract] Definition: Tail of a Sequence
> A **tail** of a sequence is a subsequence that consists of all terms of the sequence from some index $N$ on. In set notation, a tail is one of the sets:
> $$
> \{a_n \mid n \ge N\}
> $$

Using this terminology, the statement that $a_n \to L$ can be rephrased simply as: *every $\epsilon$-interval about $L$ contains a tail of the sequence*.

> [!note] Convergence Depends Only on the Tails
> Because convergence only requires that each $\epsilon$-interval around $L$ contain a tail $\{a_n \mid n \ge N\}$ for some index $N$, the convergence or divergence of a sequence has nothing to do with how the sequence begins. You can alter, delete, or prepend a million initial terms without changing whether the sequence converges or what limit it converges to—everything depends solely on how the tails behave.

## Bounded Nondecreasing Sequences

When we can explicitly simplify the formula for $a_n$, we can often find the limit directly. But what if a sequence is complicated and we merely want to know *whether* it converges, even if we cannot immediately compute its exact limit? For sequences whose terms move in only one direction, there is a remarkably simple criterion.

> [!abstract] Definition: Nondecreasing Sequence
> A sequence $\{a_n\}$ with the property that
> $$
> a_n \le a_{n+1} \quad \text{for all } n
> $$
> is called a **nondecreasing sequence**.

For instance, the natural numbers $1, 2, 3, \dots, n, \dots$, the fractions $\frac{1}{2}, \frac{2}{3}, \frac{3}{4}, \dots, \frac{n}{n+1}, \dots$, and the constant sequence $\{3\}$ (since $3 \le 3$) are all nondecreasing sequences. Notice that we say *nondecreasing* rather than *increasing* because consecutive terms are allowed to be equal ($a_n = a_{n+1}$).

Observe that there are only two kinds of nondecreasing sequences:
1. Those whose terms increase beyond any finite bound (like $1, 2, 3, \dots, n, \dots$), and
2. Those whose terms do not surpass some finite ceiling (like $\frac{1}{2}, \frac{2}{3}, \frac{3}{4}, \dots, \frac{n}{n+1}, \dots$, whose terms are always less than $1$).

To formalize this distinction, we define what it means for a sequence to have an upper ceiling.

> [!abstract] Definition: Bounded from Above, Upper Bound, and Least Upper Bound
> A sequence $\{a_n\}$ is **bounded from above** if there exists a number $M$ such that
> $$
> a_n \le M \quad \text{for all } n
> $$
> The number $M$ is an **upper bound** for $\{a_n\}$. If $M$ is an upper bound for $\{a_n\}$ but no number less than $M$ is an upper bound for $\{a_n\}$, then $M$ is the **least upper bound** for $\{a_n\}$.

For example, the sequence $1, 2, 3, \dots, n, \dots$ has no upper bound. In contrast, the sequence $\frac{1}{2}, \frac{2}{3}, \frac{3}{4}, \dots, \frac{n}{n+1}, \dots$ is bounded above by $M = 1$ (as well as by $2$, $100$, etc.). Moreover, because the terms $\frac{n}{n+1}$ get arbitrarily close to $1$ as $n$ increases, no number smaller than $1$ can serve as an upper bound for the entire sequence; thus, $1$ is the **least upper bound** for $\left\{\frac{n}{n+1}\right\}$.

### The Nondecreasing Sequence Theorem

A fundamental axiom of the real number system—the *completeness property of real numbers*—guarantees that any nondecreasing sequence that is bounded from above always possesses a least upper bound $L$. Even better, the sequence must actually converge to $L$.

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: Figure 8.4 - If the terms of a nondecreasing sequence have an upper bound M, they have a limit L <= M

> [!theorem] Theorem 1: The Nondecreasing Sequence Theorem
> A nondecreasing sequence of real numbers converges if and only if it is bounded from above. If a nondecreasing sequence converges, it converges to its least upper bound.

**Proof:**
Suppose $\{s_n\}$ is a nondecreasing sequence that is bounded from above. By the completeness property of real numbers, $\{s_n\}$ has a least upper bound, which we denote by $L$. We will prove that $\{s_n\}$ converges to $L$.

If we plot the points $(1, s_1), (2, s_2), \dots, (n, s_n), \dots$ in the $xy$-plane, then for any upper bound $M$, all these points lie on or below the horizontal line $y = M$. Because $L$ is the *least* upper bound, the line $y = L$ is the lowest such ceiling line: none of the points $(n, s_n)$ lies above $y = L$, but for any positive number $\epsilon > 0$, the lowered line $y = L - \epsilon$ is no longer an upper bound, so at least some points of the sequence must lie above $y = L - \epsilon$. Analytically, this gives two facts:
1. $s_n \le L$ for all values of $n$ (since $L$ is an upper bound), and
2. Given any $\epsilon > 0$, there exists at least one integer $N$ for which $s_N > L - \epsilon$ (otherwise $L - \epsilon$ would be a smaller upper bound than $L$, contradicting that $L$ is the least upper bound).

Now we use the hypothesis that $\{s_n\}$ is nondecreasing: for every index $n \ge N$, we have $s_n \ge s_N$. Combining these inequalities yields:

$$
L - \epsilon < s_N \le s_n \le L < L + \epsilon \quad \text{for all } n \ge N
$$

Thus, $|s_n - L| < \epsilon$ for all $n > N$: all numbers $s_n$ beyond the $N$th term lie within $\epsilon$ of $L$. This is precisely the condition for $L$ to be the limit of the sequence $\{s_n\}$, so $\lim_{n \to \infty} s_n = L$. (Conversely, if a nondecreasing sequence $\{s_n\}$ is not bounded from above, its terms eventually surpass every real number $L$, so it diverges to $\infty$; hence a nondecreasing sequence converges if and only if it is bounded from above.) $\blacksquare$

> [!note] Analogous Result for Nonincreasing Sequences
> A completely analogous theorem holds for **nonincreasing sequences** (sequences satisfying $a_n \ge a_{n+1}$ for all $n$): a nonincreasing sequence of real numbers converges if and only if it is **bounded from below** (meaning $a_n \ge m$ for some lower bound $m$), in which case it converges to its **greatest lower bound**.
