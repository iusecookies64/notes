# 4.1 Indefinite Integration

## Finding Antiderivatives and Indefinite Integrals

Up to this point in calculus, our primary focus has been *differentiation*: starting with a known function $F(x)$ and finding its rate of change $F'(x)$. Now, we turn that question around. What if we already know the rate of change—say, the velocity of a moving object or the slope of a curve at every point—and we want to recover the original function that produced it? This reverse process of differentiation is called **antidifferentiation** or **integration**.

> [!abstract] Definition: Antiderivative and Indefinite Integral
> A function $F(x)$ is an **antiderivative** of a function $f(x)$ if
> $$
> F'(x) = f(x)
> $$
> for all $x$ in the domain of $f$. The set of all antiderivatives of $f$ is the **indefinite integral** of $f$ with respect to $x$, denoted by
> $$
> \int f(x) \, dx
> $$
> Here, the symbol $\int$ is the **integral sign**, the function $f(x)$ is the **integrand** of the integral, and $x$ is the **variable of integration**.

Once we find a single antiderivative $F(x)$ for $f(x)$, how do we know we can capture *all* possible antiderivatives? Recall from Corollary 2 of the Mean Value Theorem that any two functions with the exact same derivative on an interval must differ by a constant.

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: The Arbitrary Constant of Integration
> Because all functions sharing the same derivative on an interval differ only by an additive constant, once we find one antiderivative $F(x)$, every other antiderivative is obtained simply by adding an arbitrary constant $C$:
> $$
> \int f(x) \, dx = F(x) + C
> $$

## Indefinite Integrals Obtained Directly by Differentiation

Because indefinite integration is the reverse of differentiation, every derivative formula we already know can be read backward to give us an immediate integration formula:

1. $\displaystyle\int x^n \, dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C, \quad n \neq -1, \text{ where } n \text{ is rational}$
2. $\displaystyle\int \sin kx \, dx = -\frac{\cos kx}{k} + C$
3. $\displaystyle\int \cos kx \, dx = \frac{\sin kx}{k} + C$
4. $\displaystyle\int \sec^2 x \, dx = \tan x + C$
5. $\displaystyle\int \csc^2 x \, dx = -\cot x + C$
6. $\displaystyle\int \sec x \tan x \, dx = \sec x + C$
7. $\displaystyle\int \csc x \cot x \, dx = -\csc x + C$

One of the most reassuring features of indefinite integration is that verifying a proposed answer is straightforward: we simply differentiate our result to check whether we recover the original integrand.

> [!example] Example: Verifying an Antiderivative by Differentiation
> Verify whether $x\sin x + \cos x + C$ is equal to $\displaystyle\int f(x) \, dx$, where $f(x) = x\cos x$.

**Solution:**
To verify the formula, we differentiate the proposed antiderivative with respect to $x$ using the product rule on $x\sin x$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}(x\sin x + \cos x + C) &= x(\cos x) + (\sin x)(1) - \sin x + 0 \\
&= x\cos x
\end{aligned}
$$
Because the derivative matches the integrand $f(x) = x\cos x$ everywhere, we conclude that
$$
\int x\cos x \, dx = x\sin x + \cos x + C
$$
Later on, we will develop systematic, step-by-step techniques for finding antiderivatives like this from scratch. $\blacksquare$

## Rules of Indefinite Integration

Just as differentiation is a linear operation—allowing us to pull out constant multipliers and differentiate term by term—indefinite integration inherits the exact same linearity rules.

> [!theorem] Linearity Rules of Indefinite Integration
> Let $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ be functions with antiderivatives, and let $k$ be any constant. Then:
> 1. **Constant Multiple Rule:**
>    $$
>    \int k f(x) \, dx = k \int f(x) \, dx
>    $$
> 2. **Sum and Difference Rule:**
>    $$
>    \int \left[f(x) \pm g(x)\right] dx = \int f(x) \, dx \pm \int g(x) \, dx
>    $$

## The Integrals of $\sin^2 x$ and $\cos^2 x$

What happens when we need to integrate powers of trigonometric functions, such as $\sin^2 x$ or $\cos^2 x$? We cannot directly apply the power rule because the derivative of $\sin x$ or $\cos x$ is not sitting outside to help us. Instead, we can use double-angle trigonometric identities to rewrite the squared functions in terms of $\cos 2x$, which we already know how to integrate!

Starting from the double-angle identity $\cos 2x = 1 - 2\sin^2 x$, we solve for $\sin^2 x$:
$$
\sin^2 x = \frac{1 - \cos 2x}{2}
$$
Integrating both sides term by term using the linearity rules and our basic integration formulas gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int \sin^2 x \, dx &= \int \left(\frac{1 - \cos 2x}{2}\right) dx \\
&= \frac{1}{2}\int x^0 \, dx - \frac{1}{2}\int \cos 2x \, dx \\
&= \frac{x}{2} - \frac{\sin 2x}{4} + C
\end{aligned}
$$
Similarly, using the identity $\cos 2x = 2\cos^2 x - 1$, or $\cos^2 x = \frac{1 + \cos 2x}{2}$, we obtain the companion formula for $\cos^2 x$:
$$
\int \cos^2 x \, dx = \frac{x}{2} + \frac{\sin 2x}{4} + C
$$

# 4.2 Differential Equations, Initial Value Problems and Mathematical Modelling

## Initial Value Problems

In science and engineering, we rarely observe a function directly; much more often, we observe how a quantity changes with respect to time or position, along with its state at a single starting moment. How do we turn that rate-of-change law and starting state into a complete formula?

> [!abstract] Definition: Differential Equation and Initial Value Problem
> An equation such as $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = f(x)$ that contains a derivative is called a **differential equation**. The problem of finding a function $y$ of $x$ when we know both its derivative and its specific value $y_0$ at a particular point $x_0$ is called an **initial value problem**.

We solve an initial value problem in two natural steps: first, we integrate to find the family of all possible antiderivatives, and second, we use the given point $(x_0, y_0)$ to pin down the exact value of the arbitrary constant $C$.

> [!example] Example: Finding a Curve from Its Slope and a Point
> Find the curve whose slope at the point $(x, y)$ is $3x^2$, if the curve is required to pass through the point $(1, -1)$.

**Solution:**
We are given that the slope of the curve satisfies the differential equation $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 3x^2$. Integrating both sides with respect to $x$, we find:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right) dx &= 3 \cdot \left(\frac{x^{2+1}}{2+1}\right) + C \\
y &= 3\left(\frac{x^3}{3}\right) + C \\
y &= x^3 + C
\end{aligned}
$$
Next, we use the initial condition that the curve passes through $(1, -1)$, meaning $y(1) = -1$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
1^3 + C &= -1 \\
C &= -2
\end{aligned}
$$
Substituting $C = -2$ back into our general formula yields the specific curve:
$$
y = x^3 - 2
$$
$\blacksquare$

> [!abstract] Definition: General Solution and Particular Solution
> For a given differential equation $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = f(x)$, finding the indefinite integral produces the **general solution** of the differential equation:
> $$
> y = \int \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right) dx = \int f(x) \, dx = F(x) + C
> $$
> Evaluating the arbitrary constant $C$ to satisfy an initial value problem yields the **particular solution** whose graph passes through the specified point $(x_0, y_0)$.

What if a physical law—like Newton's second law of motion, $F = ma$—gives us the *second* derivative of a function instead of the first?

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Solving Second- and Higher-Order Differential Equations
> To solve a second-order differential equation of the form $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} = f(x)$, we must integrate twice. Because each integration introduces a new arbitrary constant, finding a unique particular solution requires two pieces of initial information rather than just a single point.
>
> First, since $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \dfrac{d}{dx}\left(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right)$, integrating once recovers the first derivative:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \int\left(\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\right) dx &= \int f(x) \, dx = F_1(x) + C_1 \\
> \frac{dy}{dx} &= F_1(x) + C_1
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> Integrating a second time recovers the function $y(x)$ itself:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \int\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right) dx &= \int \left(F_1(x) + C_1\right) dx \\
> y &= F_2(x) + C_1 x + C_2
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> In the exact same fashion, we can solve any $n$-th order differential equation of the form $\dfrac{d^n y}{dx^n} = f(x)$ by integrating $n$ successive times and using $n$ initial conditions.

> [!example] Example: Vertical Projectile Motion
> A projectile is fired straight up from a platform $3\text{ m}$ above the ground with an initial velocity of $160\text{ m/s}$. Find the equation of its height above the ground as a function of time $t$, assuming the downward acceleration due to gravity is $9.8\text{ m/s}^2$.

**Solution:**
Let $h(t)$ denote the height of the projectile at time $t$. Since acceleration is the second derivative of position with respect to time, we start with the differential equation
$$
\frac{d^2h}{dt^2} = -9.8
$$
Integrating once with respect to $t$ gives the velocity $v = \dfrac{dh}{dt}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int\left(\frac{d^2h}{dt^2}\right) dt &= -9.8t + C_1 \\
v = \frac{dh}{dt} &= -9.8t + C_1
\end{aligned}
$$
At $t = 0$, the initial velocity is $v(0) = 160\text{ m/s}$. Substituting this initial condition gives $C_1 = 160$, so the velocity function is
$$
v = \frac{dh}{dt} = -9.8t + 160
$$
Now we integrate a second time with respect to $t$ to find the height function $h(t)$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
h = \int \frac{dh}{dt} \, dt &= -9.8\left(\frac{t^2}{2}\right) + 160t + C_2 \\
&= -4.9t^2 + 160t + C_2
\end{aligned}
$$
At $t = 0$, the projectile is launched from a platform $3\text{ m}$ high, meaning $h(0) = 3$. This gives $C_2 = 3$, leading to our final height equation:
$$
h = -4.9t^2 + 160t + 3
$$
$\blacksquare$

## Sketching Solution Curves

> [!abstract] Definition: Solution Curve (Integral Curve)
> The graph of a solution to a differential equation is called a **solution curve** (or **integral curve**).

What if we encounter a differential equation $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = f(x)$ where we do not yet know—or where there does not even exist—an explicit elementary formula for the antiderivative of $f(x)$? Even without solving the integral analytically, we can still determine the geometric shape of the solution curves directly by analyzing the first derivative $y' = f(x)$ and the second derivative $y'' = f'(x)$!

> [!example] Example: Sketching Solution Curves Using Derivative Analysis
> Sketch the solution curves of the differential equation
> $$
> \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{x^2 + 1}
> $$

**Solution:**
Given $y' = \dfrac{1}{x^2 + 1}$, we can deduce the behavior of the solution curves $y(x)$ from three key calculus observations:

1. **Monotonicity:** Since $y' = \dfrac{1}{x^2 + 1} > 0$ for all $x \in D(y) = (-\infty, \infty)$, every solution curve is strictly increasing over the entire real line.
2. **End Behavior:** Taking the limit of the slope at infinity shows that $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} y' = 0$, so the graph flattens out and approaches horizontal asymptotes as $x \to \pm\infty$.
3. **Concavity:** Differentiating $y'$ via the quotient or chain rule gives
   $$
   y'' = \frac{-2x}{(x^2 + 1)^2}
   $$
   Since $y'' > 0$ on $(-\infty, 0)$ and $y'' < 0$ on $(0, \infty)$, the curve is concave up on $(-\infty, 0)$, has an inflection point at $x = 0$ (where the slope is $y'(0) = 1$), and is concave down on $(0, \infty)$.

> While the equation $y' = \dfrac{1}{x^2 + 1}$ actually does have an explicit antiderivative (namely $\arctan x + C$, which we will study later), a seemingly simple equation like $y' = \sqrt{1 + x^4}$ has no known antiderivative in terms of elementary functions. In such cases, sketching the graph of $y$ by analyzing $y'$ and $y''$ provides indispensable qualitative insight into how the solution behaves.

## Mathematical Modelling

How do mathematicians and scientists translate messy real-world phenomena into precise differential equations and integrals? The development of a **mathematical model** typically follows a four-stage cycle:

1. **Formulation (Observation & Abstraction):** First, we observe a phenomenon in the real world and construct a system of mathematical variables and relationships that imitate its essential features. In essence, we build a mathematical metaphor for what we see.
2. **Mathematical Analysis:** Next, we apply existing mathematical tools (such as differentiation and integration) to the variables and relationships in the model to draw logical conclusions about them.
3. **Interpretation:** After that, we translate those abstract mathematical conclusions back into concrete information and predictions about the physical system under study.
4. **Validation & Generalization:** Finally, we check the predicted information against real-world observations to see whether the model has genuine predictive value. We also investigate whether the same mathematical model applies to other seemingly unrelated systems.

## Computer Simulation

When we want to study a physical system, the traditional approach is to perform experiments under varying conditions and record observations. However, carrying out repeated physical experiments is often unfeasible: it may be prohibitively expensive, physically dangerous, too time-consuming, or involve far too many calculations to track by hand.

In such situations, we turn to **computer simulation**, running a series of simulated experiments on a computer based on our mathematical model. Well-designed computer simulations behave like the real physical system without the cost, risk, or physical limitations, making it practical to study enormously complex processes—such as NASA's space flight simulation models.

# 4.3 Integration by Substitution – Running the Chain Rule Backward

## The Generalized Power Rule in Integral Form

Our basic integration table handles simple integrands like $x^n$, $\cos x$, or $\sec^2 x$, but what happens when the integrand is a composite expression like $(x + 2)^5$ or $\sqrt{1 + y^2}\cdot 2y$? Since differentiation uses the **Chain Rule** to differentiate composite functions, we can integrate composite functions by running the Chain Rule backward!

Suppose $u = g(x)$ is a differentiable function of $x$. By the Chain Rule, we know that for $n \neq -1$,
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}\right) = u^n \frac{du}{dx}
$$
Reading this derivative statement backward tells us that $\dfrac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}$ is an antiderivative of $u^n \dfrac{du}{dx}$:
$$
\int \left(u^n \frac{du}{dx}\right) dx = \frac{u^{n+1}}{n+1} + C
$$
Notice how suggestive the left-hand side looks if we group $\dfrac{du}{dx}\,dx$ together as the differential $du = \dfrac{du}{dx}\,dx$:
$$
\int \left(u^n \frac{du}{dx}\right) dx = \int u^n \left(\frac{du}{dx} \, dx\right) = \int u^n \, du
$$
This gives us the generalized power rule in differential form.

> [!theorem] Theorem: The Generalized Power Rule in Integral Form
> If $u$ is any differentiable function of $x$ and $n \neq -1$ is a rational number, then
> $$
> \int u^n \, du = \frac{u^{n+1}}{n+1} + C
> $$

Let's see how this differential substitution works in practice.

> [!example] Example: Integrating a Shifted Power Function
> Evaluate $\displaystyle\int (x+2)^5 \, dx$.

**Solution:**
Let $u = x + 2$. Taking the differential gives $du = \left(\dfrac{du}{dx}\right) dx = 1\cdot dx = dx$. Substituting $u$ and $du$ into the integral reduces it to a standard power rule integral:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int (x+2)^5 \, dx &= \int u^5 \, du \\
&= \frac{u^{5+1}}{5+1} + C \\
&= \frac{(x+2)^6}{6} + C
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

> [!example] Example: Integrating a Radical with an Accompanying Derivative Factor
> Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \sqrt{1 + y^2}\cdot 2y \, dy$.

**Solution:**
Notice that the factor $2y\,dy$ outside the square root is the exact differential of the inside expression $1 + y^2$. Thus, we set $u = 1 + y^2$, which gives $du = u' \, dy = 2y \, dy$. Substituting into the integral yields:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int \sqrt{1 + y^2}\cdot 2y \, dy &= \int \sqrt{u} \, du \\
&= \frac{u^{3/2}}{3/2} + C \\
&= \frac{2}{3}(1 + y^2)^{3/2} + C
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

## Trigonometric Functions and Differential Substitution

The exact same backward-Chain-Rule reasoning applies to trigonometric functions. If $u$ is a differentiable function of $x$, then $\sin u$ is also differentiable with respect to $x$, and the Chain Rule gives
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(\sin u) = \cos u \frac{du}{dx} \implies \int \left(\cos u \cdot \frac{du}{dx}\right) dx = \sin u + C
$$
Writing $\left(\dfrac{du}{dx}\cdot dx\right)$ as the differential $du$, we see that
$$
\int \left(\cos u \frac{du}{dx}\right) dx = \int \cos u \left(\frac{du}{dx} \cdot dx\right) = \int \cos u \, du = \sin u + C
$$
By applying the Chain Rule to the remaining basic trigonometric functions in the same manner, we obtain the complete list of generalized trigonometric integral formulas:

1. $\displaystyle\int \cos u \, du = \sin u + C$
2. $\displaystyle\int \sin u \, du = -\cos u + C$
3. $\displaystyle\int \sec^2 u \, du = \tan u + C$
4. $\displaystyle\int \csc^2 u \, du = -\cot u + C$
5. $\displaystyle\int \sec u \tan u \, du = \sec u + C$
6. $\displaystyle\int \csc u \cot u \, du = -\csc u + C$

> [!example] Example: Trigonometric Substitution with a Constant Multiple Adjustment
> Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{\cos^2 2\theta} \, d\theta$.

**Solution:**
First, we rewrite the reciprocal cosine squared in terms of secant squared:
$$
I = \int \frac{1}{\cos^2 2\theta} \, d\theta = \int \sec^2 2\theta \, d\theta
$$
Now let $u = 2\theta$, so that $du = 2 \, d\theta$, or $d\theta = \dfrac{1}{2} \, du$. Substituting $u$ and $d\theta$ into the integral gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \int \sec^2 u \cdot \frac{1}{2} \, du \\
&= \frac{1}{2}\int \sec^2 u \, du \\
&= \frac{1}{2}(\tan u + C) \\
&= \frac{1}{2}\tan u + \frac{C}{2} \\
&= \frac{\tan 2\theta}{2} + C'
\end{aligned}
$$
where $C' = \dfrac{C}{2}$ is an arbitrary constant. $\blacksquare$

## The Substitution Method

All of the substitutions we have used so far—whether for powers or trigonometric functions—are special cases of one unified theorem that reverses the Chain Rule for any integrable function $f$.

> [!theorem] Theorem: Integration by Substitution
> If $u = g(x)$ is a differentiable function whose range is an interval $I$, and $f$ is continuous on $I$ with antiderivative $F$ (so that $\displaystyle\int f(u) \, du = F(u) + C$), then
> $$
> \int f(g(x)) \cdot g'(x) \, dx = F(g(x)) + C
> $$

**Proof:**
Using the substitution $u = g(x)$ and $du = g'(x) \, dx$, we transform the integral directly into an integral with respect to $u$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \int f(g(x))g'(x) \, dx \\
&= \int f(u) \, du \\
&= F(u) + C \\
&= F(g(x)) + C
\end{aligned}
$$
To verify this rigorously, we differentiate the right-hand side $F(g(x)) + C$ with respect to $x$ using the Chain Rule:
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left[F(g(x)) + C\right] = F'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x) = f(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)
$$
Because the derivative of $F(g(x)) + C$ equals the integrand $f(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$, the formula $\displaystyle\int f(g(x)) \cdot g'(x) \, dx = F(g(x)) + C$ holds. $\blacksquare$

One of the great strengths of the substitution method is its flexibility: often there is more than one valid choice of $u$ that simplifies the same integral! Let's explore several examples illustrating different substitution strategies.

> [!example] Example: Two Different Substitutions for the Same Radical Integral
> Evaluate $I = \displaystyle\int \frac{2z \, dz}{\sqrt[3]{z^2 + 1}}$.

**Solution:**
We can evaluate this integral using two different substitutions:

**Method 1 (Substituting the inner expression):**
Let $u = z^2 + 1$, which immediately gives $du = 2z \, dz$. Then the integral becomes:
$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \int u^{-1/3} \, du \\
&= \frac{u^{2/3}}{2/3} + C \\
&= \frac{3}{2}\left(z^2 + 1\right)^{2/3} + C
\end{aligned}
$$

**Method 2 (Substituting the entire radical):**
Alternatively, let $u = \sqrt[3]{z^2 + 1}$. Cubing both sides gives $y = u^3 = z^2 + 1$, and taking differentials of both sides yields $dy = 3u^2 \, du = 2z \, dz$. Substituting into $I$ gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \int \frac{3u^2 \, du}{u} \\
&= 3\int u \, du \\
&= 3 \cdot \frac{u^2}{2} + C \\
&= \frac{3}{2}\left(z^2 + 1\right)^{2/3} + C
\end{aligned}
$$
Both substitutions lead to the exact same expression! $\blacksquare$

What if two different substitutions lead to answers that *look* completely different at first glance?

> [!example] Example: Reconciling Apparently Different Antiderivatives
> Evaluate $I = \displaystyle\int \sec^2 x \cdot \tan x \, dx$ in two different ways and explain how the resulting expressions are related.

**Solution:**
Let's apply two different choices of $u$ to $I = \displaystyle\int \sec^2 x \cdot \tan x \, dx$:

**Method 1:** Let $u = \tan x$, so that $du = \sec^2 x \, dx$. Then
$$
I = \int u \, du = \frac{u^2}{2} + C = \frac{\tan^2 x}{2} + C
$$

**Method 2:** Rewrite the integrand as $\sec x \cdot (\sec x \tan x) \, dx$ and let $u = \sec x$, so that $du = \sec x \tan x \, dx$. Then
$$
I = \int u \, du = \frac{u^2}{2} + C' = \frac{\sec^2 x}{2} + C'
$$

At first glance, $\dfrac{\tan^2 x}{2} + C$ and $\dfrac{\sec^2 x}{2} + C'$ look different! However, we know that any two valid antiderivatives of the same function on an interval must differ only by a constant. Indeed, using the Pythagorean identity $\sec^2 x - \tan^2 x = 1$, we take their difference:
$$
\left(\frac{\sec^2 x}{2} + C'\right) - \left(\frac{\tan^2 x}{2} + C\right) = \frac{\sec^2 x - \tan^2 x}{2} + C' - C = \frac{1}{2} + C' - C = \text{constant}
$$
Thus, we can solve the same integral in multiple ways and still obtain equivalent families of antiderivatives (with the constant $1/2$ simply absorbed into the arbitrary constant of integration). $\blacksquare$

> [!example] Example: Integrating Powers of Sine and Cosine with Fractional Angles
> Evaluate $I = \displaystyle\int \sin^5\frac{x}{3}\cos\frac{x}{3} \, dx$.

**Solution:**
Let $u = \sin\dfrac{x}{3}$. Differentiating gives $du = \cos\dfrac{x}{3} \cdot \dfrac{1}{3} \, dx$, which we can rearrange as $\cos\dfrac{x}{3} \, dx = 3 \, du$. Substituting $u$ and $3 \, du$ into the integral gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= 3\int u^5 \, du \\
&= 3 \cdot \frac{u^6}{6} + C \\
&= \frac{u^6}{2} + C \\
&= \frac{\sin^6(x/3)}{2} + C
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

> [!example] Example: Integrating an Algebraic Composite by Adjusting Constants
> Evaluate $I = \displaystyle\int x^4\left(7 - \frac{x^5}{10}\right)^3 dx$.

**Solution:**
Notice that $x^4$ is proportional to the derivative of the inside polynomial $7 - \dfrac{x^5}{10}$. Setting $u = 7 - \dfrac{x^5}{10}$, we find
$$
du = -\frac{5x^4}{10} \, dx = -\frac{1}{2}x^4 \, dx \implies x^4 \, dx = -2 \, du
$$
Substituting $u$ and $x^4 \, dx = -2 \, du$ into the integral yields:
$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= -2\int u^3 \, du \\
&= -2\left(\frac{u^4}{4} + C\right) \\
&= -\frac{u^4}{2} + C' \\
&= -\frac{1}{2}\left(7 - \frac{x^5}{10}\right)^4 + C'
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 41

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Evaluate the indefinite integral:
> $$
> \int \frac{1}{\theta^2}\sin\left(\frac{1}{\theta}\right)\cos\left(\frac{1}{\theta}\right) d\theta
> $$

**Solution:**
Let $u = \cos\left(\dfrac{1}{\theta}\right)$. Using the Chain Rule, its differential is
$$
du = -\sin\left(\frac{1}{\theta}\right) \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{\theta^2}\right) d\theta = \frac{1}{\theta^2}\sin\left(\frac{1}{\theta}\right) d\theta
$$
Substituting $u$ and $du$ into the integral gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \int u \, du \\
&= \frac{u^2}{2} + C \\
&= \frac{\cos^2(1/\theta)}{2} + C
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 47

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Evaluate the indefinite integral:
> $$
> \int \frac{18\tan^2 x\sec^2 x}{(2 + \tan^3 x)^2} \, dx
> $$

**Solution:**
When facing a multi-layered composite integrand, there are several sequences of substitutions we could choose:
1. First set $u = \tan x$, then $v = u^3$, and finally $w = 2 + v$.
2. First set $u = \tan^3 x$, and then $v = 2 + u$.
3. Combine the steps into a single substitution by setting $u = 2 + \tan^3 x$ directly.

The third option is the most direct! Setting $u = 2 + \tan^3 x$, we differentiate using the Chain Rule to get $du = 3\tan^2 x \cdot \sec^2 x \, dx$, so $18\tan^2 x \sec^2 x \, dx = 6 \, du$.

*(Note: If the denominator has power $2$, this becomes $\displaystyle\int \frac{6 \, du}{u^2} = -6u^{-1} + C = -\frac{6}{2 + \tan^3 x} + C$; if the denominator has power $3$, i.e., $(2 + \tan^3 x)^3$, we obtain:)*
$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \int \frac{6 \, du}{u^3} \\
&= 6\left(\frac{u^{-3+1}}{-3+1}\right) + C \\
&= -3u^{-2} + C \\
&= -\frac{3}{(2 + \tan^3 x)^2} + C
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

# 4.5 Riemann Sums and Definite Integral

## Algebra with Finite Sums

Before we can define the area under a general curve by slicing it into many thin rectangles, we need a compact way to manipulate finite sums of many terms using sigma notation $\sum$. Because finite summation is just repeated addition, it obeys the familiar associative, commutative, and distributive laws of algebra:

> [!theorem] Algebraic Rules for Finite Sums
> For any sequences of real numbers $\{a_k\}$ and $\{b_k\}$ and any constant $c$:
> 1. **Sum Rule:** $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}(a_k + b_k) = \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k + \sum_{k=1}^{n}b_k$
> 2. **Difference Rule:** $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}(a_k - b_k) = \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k - \sum_{k=1}^{n}b_k$
> 3. **Constant Multiple Rule:** $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}c a_k = c\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k$
> 4. **Constant Value Rule:** $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}c = n \cdot c$

## Sum Formulas for Positive Integers

When we evaluate sums of powers of integers—which arise naturally when approximating the area under polynomial curves—the following closed-form summation formulas are indispensable:

1. **Sum of the First $n$ Positive Integers:**
   $$
   \sum_{k=1}^{n}k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}
   $$
2. **Sum of the First $n$ Squares:**
   $$
   \sum_{k=1}^{n}k^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}
   $$
3. **Sum of the First $n$ Cubes:**
   $$
   \sum_{k=1}^{n}k^3 = \left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2
   $$

## Riemann Sums

How do we define and compute the area between the graph of a continuous function $f(x)$ and the $x$-axis over a closed interval $[a, b]$? We start by subdividing $[a, b]$ into smaller pieces and approximating the curved region with rectangles.

> [!abstract] Definition: Partition, Subinterval Width, and Riemann Sum
> Let $f$ be a continuous (or bounded) function on the closed interval $[a, b]$, and let $P$ be a **partition** of $[a, b]$:
> $$
> P = \{ x_0, x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n \} \quad \text{where } a = x_0 < x_1 < x_2 < \dots < x_n = b
> $$
> The points of $P$ divide $[a, b]$ into $n$ closed subintervals $[x_0, x_1], [x_1, x_2], \dots, [x_{k-1}, x_k], \dots, [x_{n-1}, x_n]$ that intersect only at their endpoints. The width (length) of the $k$-th subinterval $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$ is denoted by
> $$
> \Delta x_k = x_k - x_{k-1}
> $$
> If we choose any sample point $c_k$ in the $k$-th subinterval $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$ for each $k = 1, \dots, n$, then the **Riemann sum** for $f$ on the interval $[a, b]$ associated with the partition $P$ and sample points $\{c_k\}$ is
> $$
> S_P = \sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k)\Delta x_k
> $$

Geometrically, each term $f(c_k)\Delta x_k$ represents the signed area of a rectangle of width $\Delta x_k$ and height $f(c_k)$, so the Riemann sum $S_P$ approximates the total signed area between the curve $f$ and the $x$-axis on $[a, b]$.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260925153557.webp)

As we make the partition finer and finer—so that every rectangle becomes narrower—the Riemann sum's approximation to the signed area under the curve becomes more and more accurate. To measure how fine a partition is, we look at its widest subinterval.

> [!abstract] Definition: Norm of a Partition
> The **norm** of a partition $P$, denoted by $\lVert P \rVert$, is the length of the longest subinterval in the partition:
> $$
> \lVert P \rVert = \max_{1 \le k \le n}(x_k - x_{k-1}) = \max_{1 \le k \le n} \Delta x_k
> $$

## Definite Integral as a Limit of Riemann Sums

When we force the norm $\lVert P \rVert$ to approach zero, the widths of *all* subintervals in the partition are simultaneously squeezed to zero. If the resulting Riemann sums approach a single limiting value regardless of how we partition the interval or choose the sample points $c_k$, we call that limiting value the **definite integral**.

> [!abstract] Definition: The Definite Integral as a Limit of Riemann Sums
> Let $f(x)$ be a function defined on a closed interval $[a, b]$. We say that the limit of the Riemann sums $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k)\Delta x_k$ on $[a, b]$ as $\lVert P \rVert \to 0$ is the number $I$ if the following condition is satisfied:
>
> Given any number $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a corresponding number $\delta > 0$ such that for every partition $P$ of $[a, b]$,
> $$
> \lVert P \rVert < \delta \implies \left|\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k)\Delta x_k - I\right| < \varepsilon
> $$
> for any choice of sample numbers $c_k$ in the subintervals $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$. If this limit exists, we write
> $$
> \lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0}\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k)\Delta x_k = I
> $$
> We call $I$ the **definite integral** of $f$ over $[a, b]$, say that $f$ is **integrable** over $[a, b]$, and say that the Riemann sums of $f$ on $[a, b]$ converge to the number $I$. The number $I$ is denoted by $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx$:
> $$
> \lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0}\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k)\Delta x_k = \int_a^b f(x) \, dx
> $$

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Uniqueness Across All Partitions and Sample Points
> What is truly remarkable about this definition is that despite the endless variety of Riemann sums $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k)\Delta x_k$ as both the partition $P$ and the arbitrary sample points $c_k \in [x_{k-1}, x_k]$ vary, taking the limit as $\lVert P \rVert \to 0$ forces all of them to converge to the exact same number $I$.

This raises a natural foundational question: for which functions are we *guaranteed* that this limit exists?

> [!theorem] Theorem 1: The Existence of Definite Integrals
> All continuous functions are integrable. That is, if a function $f$ is continuous on a closed interval $[a, b]$, then its definite integral over $[a, b]$ exists.

**Proof:**
To prove that $\displaystyle\lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0} \sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k)\Delta x_k$ exists, we examine the two extreme choices of sample points on each subinterval:
- The **Upper Riemann Sum** $U(f, P)$ is the specific Riemann sum in which we choose $c_k$ on each subinterval $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$ so that $f(c_k) = M_k$ is the absolute maximum value of $f$ on $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$.
- The **Lower Riemann Sum** $L(f, P)$ is the specific Riemann sum in which we choose $c_k$ so that $f(c_k) = m_k$ is the absolute minimum value of $f$ on $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$.

Because $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$, it is continuous on every closed subinterval $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$. By the Extreme Value Theorem, the absolute maximum $M_k$ and absolute minimum $m_k$ are guaranteed to exist and are attained by $f$ at points within $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$. Thus, we can write:
$$
U(f, P) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} M_k \Delta x_k, \qquad L(f, P) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} m_k \Delta x_k
$$
For the definite integral to exist, we need the upper and lower sums to converge to the same limiting value $I$ as $\lVert P \rVert \to 0$:
$$
\lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0} U(f, P) = \lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0} L(f, P) = I \iff \lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0}\left[U(f, P) - L(f, P)\right] = 0
$$
In $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ terms, this modified condition states that for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that for all partitions $P$ with $\lVert P \rVert < \delta$,
$$
\left|U(f, P) - L(f, P)\right| < \varepsilon
$$
Now we use a deep property of continuous functions on closed intervals known as the **Heine–Cantor Theorem**: because $f$ is continuous on the closed, bounded interval $[a, b]$, it is **uniformly continuous** on $[a, b]$. This means that for any positive tolerance—in particular, for $\varepsilon' = \dfrac{\varepsilon}{b - a} > 0$—there exists a single $\delta > 0$ such that for *all* $x, y \in [a, b]$,
$$
|x - y| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(y)| < \frac{\varepsilon}{b - a}
$$
Consider any partition $P$ of $[a, b]$ with $\lVert P \rVert < \delta$. By the definition of the norm, every subinterval in $P$ has width $\Delta x_k = x_k - x_{k-1} < \delta$. Let $c_k$ and $d_k$ be the points in $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$ where $f$ attains its minimum $m_k = f(c_k)$ and maximum $M_k = f(d_k)$, respectively. Since both $c_k$ and $d_k$ lie in $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$, the distance between them satisfies
$$
|d_k - c_k| \le x_k - x_{k-1} < \delta
$$
By uniform continuity, it follows immediately that
$$
M_k - m_k = f(d_k) - f(c_k) < \frac{\varepsilon}{b - a}
$$
Summing this difference across all $n$ subintervals of the partition $P$, we obtain:
$$
\begin{aligned}
U(f, P) - L(f, P) &= \sum_{k=1}^{n}(M_k - m_k)\Delta x_k \\
&< \sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{b - a}\right)\Delta x_k \\
&= \frac{\varepsilon}{b - a}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Delta x_k \\
&= \frac{\varepsilon}{b - a} \cdot (b - a) \\
&= \varepsilon
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus, $U(f, P) - L(f, P) < \varepsilon$ whenever $\lVert P \rVert < \delta$. Therefore, if $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, its definite integral $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx$ always exists. $\blacksquare$

### Geometric Interpretation of Upper and Lower Sums

To visualize why $U(f, P) - L(f, P) \to 0$ as the partition becomes finer, consider an increasing function $f(x)$ and the corresponding graphs of $U(f, P)$, $L(f, P)$, and their difference $U(f, P) - L(f, P)$:

![](Pasted%20image%2020260925155322.webp)

Notice that $U(f, P) - L(f, P)$ is simply the total area of the little excess rectangles straddling the curve! As we make the partition finer and finer, those rectangles shrink in both width and height, so their combined area becomes arbitrarily small—forcing $U(f, P)$ and $L(f, P)$ to squeeze toward the exact same limit.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260925155637.webp)

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Why the Modified Condition ($U - L \to 0$) Guarantees Convergence of All Riemann Sums
> For any choice of sample points $c_k \in [x_{k-1}, x_k]$, the function value $f(c_k)$ is trapped between the subinterval minimum and maximum: $m_k \le f(c_k) \le M_k$. Multiplying by $\Delta x_k$ and summing over all $k = 1, \dots, n$ traps every Riemann sum between the lower and upper sums:
> $$
> L(f, P) \le \sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k) \Delta x_k \le U(f, P)
> $$
> Because both $L(f, P)$ and $U(f, P)$ converge to the same limit $I$ as $\lVert P \rVert \to 0$, the **Sandwich Theorem** guarantees that the general Riemann sum $\displaystyle\lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0}\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k)\Delta x_k$ also exists and equals $I$.

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Boundedness of Continuous Functions
> A crucial property of $f$ used in Theorem 1 is that continuity on a closed interval $[a, b]$ guarantees that $f$ is *bounded* on $[a, b]$. Later, in Chapter 7, we will investigate how to extend integration to unbounded functions (improper integrals).

## Proof of the Heine–Cantor Theorem

In proving Theorem 1, we relied on the Heine–Cantor Theorem—that continuity on a closed interval $[a, b]$ automatically upgrades to *uniform* continuity. Let's state this concept precisely and prove the theorem.

> [!abstract] Definition: Uniform Continuity
> A function $f$ is **uniformly continuous** on an interval $I$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x, y \in I$,
> $$
> |x - y| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(y)| < \varepsilon
> $$

*(Notice the key distinction from ordinary pointwise continuity: in uniform continuity, a single $\delta$ works uniformly across the entire interval $I$, depending only on $\varepsilon$ and not on the specific point $x$.)*

> [!theorem] Heine–Cantor Theorem
> If a function $f$ is continuous on a closed and bounded interval $[a, b]$, then $f$ is uniformly continuous on $[a, b]$.

**Proof:**
We proceed by contradiction. Suppose that $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, but is *not* uniformly continuous on $[a, b]$. Negating the definition of uniform continuity means there exists some bad tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$ such that no $\delta > 0$ works—that is, for every $\delta > 0$, we can find at least one pair of points $x, y \in [a, b]$ such that $|x - y| < \delta$ and yet $|f(x) - f(y)| \ge \varepsilon$.

Let us test a sequence of progressively smaller values $\delta_n = \dfrac{1}{n}$ for $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$:
- For $\delta_1 = 1$, there exist $x_1, y_1 \in [a, b]$ such that $|x_1 - y_1| < 1$ and $|f(x_1) - f(y_1)| \ge \varepsilon$.
- For $\delta_2 = \dfrac{1}{2}$, there exist $x_2, y_2 \in [a, b]$ such that $|x_2 - y_2| < \dfrac{1}{2}$ and $|f(x_2) - f(y_2)| \ge \varepsilon$.
- In general, for $\delta_n = \dfrac{1}{n}$, there exist $x_n, y_n \in [a, b]$ such that $|x_n - y_n| < \dfrac{1}{n}$ and $|f(x_n) - f(y_n)| \ge \varepsilon$.

This gives us two infinite sequences $\{x_n\}$ and $\{y_n\}$ contained inside the closed, bounded interval $[a, b]$. By the **Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem**, every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence; thus, there is a subsequence $\{x_{n_k}\}$ that converges to some limit $c \in [a, b]$.

We claim that the corresponding subsequence $\{y_{n_k}\}$ must also converge to the exact same limit $c$. Indeed, as $k \to \infty$, we have $n_k \to \infty$, so the distance between $x_{n_k}$ and $y_{n_k}$ vanishes:
$$
|x_{n_k} - y_{n_k}| < \frac{1}{n_k} \to 0 \quad \text{as } k \to \infty
$$
Therefore, $\displaystyle\lim_{k \to \infty} x_{n_k} = \lim_{k \to \infty} y_{n_k} = c$. Because $f$ is continuous at $c \in [a, b]$, we can pass the limit inside $f$:
$$
\lim_{k \to \infty} \left|f(x_{n_k}) - f(y_{n_k})\right| = |f(c) - f(c)| = 0
$$
However, by construction, every pair in our sequence satisfies $\left|f(x_{n_k}) - f(y_{n_k})\right| \ge \varepsilon > 0$, which contradicts the limit being $0$! This contradiction proves that our initial assumption was false, and therefore $f$ must be uniformly continuous on $[a, b]$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Geometric Meaning of Uniform Continuity
> Geometrically, uniform continuity means that for any given vertical tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, we can find a horizontal width $\delta > 0$ such that if we form a small viewing rectangle of width $2\delta$ and height $2\varepsilon$ and slide its center along the curve $y = f(x)$, the graph will *never* exit through the top or bottom edges of the rectangle—it always enters and leaves through the vertical sides:
>
> ![](Pasted%20image%2020260925172603.webp)
>
> By contrast, a function that is *not* uniformly continuous will become so steep or oscillatory somewhere on the interval that the curve escapes through the top or bottom of the rectangle no matter how narrow we make its width $2\delta$. For example, consider $f(x) = \sin\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right)$ on the open interval $(0, 1)$. No matter how small we choose $\delta > 0$, the interval $(0, \delta)$ contains infinitely many full oscillations between $-1$ and $1$, so no $\delta$ can keep the vertical change smaller than $\varepsilon \in (0, 1)$.

## Integrability of Piecewise Continuous Functions

What if a function has a finite number of jump discontinuities on $[a, b]$? Because the definite integral represents the signed area under the curve, a function that is **piecewise continuous** on $[a, b]$ is still integrable! We simply split the interval at the points of discontinuity, integrate each continuous piece separately, and add the resulting areas together.

## Functions with No Riemann Integral

While continuous and piecewise continuous functions are always integrable, some badly discontinuous functions fail to have a Riemann integral at all. A classic example is **Dirichlet's function** on $[0, 1]$:
$$
f(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & x \text{ is rational} \\ 0, & x \text{ is irrational} \end{cases} \qquad D(f) = [0, 1]
$$
Why is this function not Riemann integrable? Take *any* partition $P$ of $[0, 1]$. Because both the rational numbers and the irrational numbers are dense in the real line, every subinterval $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$—no matter how microscopic—contains both rational points (where $f(x) = 1$) and irrational points (where $f(x) = 0$). Consequently, on every subinterval $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$, the minimum and maximum values are fixed at
$$
m_k = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad M_k = 1
$$
Computing the upper and lower Riemann sums for any partition $P$ gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
U(f, P) &= \sum_{k=1}^{n} M_k \Delta x_k = 1 \cdot \sum_{k=1}^{n}\Delta x_k = 1 \cdot (1 - 0) = 1 \\
L(f, P) &= \sum_{k=1}^{n} m_k \Delta x_k = 0 \cdot \sum_{k=1}^{n}\Delta x_k = 0
\end{aligned}
$$
Because $U(f, P) = 1 \neq 0 = L(f, P)$ no matter how small we make $\lVert P \rVert$, the upper and lower sums never approach a common limit, and $\displaystyle\int_0^1 f(x) \, dx$ does not exist.

## Constant Functions

Although Theorem 1 guarantees that the definite integral of a continuous function exists, it does not tell us how to calculate it—except in simple cases where we can evaluate the Riemann sum directly. The simplest case of all is a **constant function** $f(x) = c$ on $[a, b]$. For any partition $P$ and any choice of sample points $c_k$, the Riemann sum is:
$$
\begin{aligned}
S_P &= \sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k)\Delta x_k \\
&= \sum_{k=1}^{n} c \Delta x_k \\
&= c \cdot \sum_{k=1}^{n}\Delta x_k \\
&= c \cdot (b - a)
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $S_P = c(b - a)$ is already constant for every partition $P$, taking the limit as $\lVert P \rVert \to 0$ immediately gives:
$$
\int_a^b f(x) \, dx = \int_a^b c \, dx = c(b - a)
$$

## The Area Under the Graph of a Non-Negative Function

For a general function that dips above and below the $x$-axis, the definite integral computes the *net signed area* (area above the $x$-axis minus area below the $x$-axis). However, when the function is non-negative everywhere on $[a, b]$, every rectangle in the Riemann sum has non-negative height, so the definite integral defines the true geometric area under the curve.

> [!abstract] Definition: Area Under the Graph of a Non-Negative Function
> If $y = f(x)$ is non-negative and integrable over a closed interval $[a, b]$ (for instance, if $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ with $f(x) \ge 0$), then the **area** under the curve $y = f(x)$ from $a$ to $b$ (the region between the graph of $f$ and the $x$-axis) is the definite integral of $f$ from $a$ to $b$:
> $$
> A = \int_a^b f(x) \, dx
> $$

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Finding Total Geometric Area When $f(x)$ Changes Sign
> If a graph is not non-negative on $[a, b]$—crossing below the $x$-axis on parts of the interval—we can find the total geometric area between the curve and the $x$-axis by splitting the integral at the points where $f(x)$ changes sign and taking the absolute value of each piece. In compact notation, the total geometric area is $\displaystyle\int_a^b |f(x)| \, dx$.

Because area and the definite integral of a non-negative function are the exact same quantity, we can work in *both* directions: we can use known geometric area formulas (like triangles, rectangles, and trapezoids) to evaluate definite integrals, or we can use limits of Riemann sums to find the areas of curved regions that geometry alone cannot handle!

> [!example] Example: Using Geometric Area to Evaluate a Definite Integral
> Use an area formula from geometry to evaluate the definite integral
> $$
> \int_a^b x \, dx, \qquad 0 < a < b
> $$

**Solution:**
On the interval $[a, b]$ with $0 < a < b$, the function $f(x) = x$ is positive, so $\displaystyle\int_a^b x \, dx$ equals the area of the trapezoidal region under the line $y = x$ from $x = a$ to $x = b$:

![](Pasted%20image%2020260925173520.webp)

We can decompose this trapezoid into a lower rectangle of width $(b - a)$ and height $a$, topped by a right triangle of base $(b - a)$ and height $(b - a)$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
A = \int_a^b x \, dx &= \frac{1}{2}(b - a)(b - a) + a(b - a) \\
&= \left[\frac{b - a}{2} + a\right](b - a) \\
&= \left(\frac{b + a}{2}\right)(b - a) \\
&= \frac{b^2}{2} - \frac{a^2}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus,
$$
\int_a^b x \, dx = \frac{b^2}{2} - \frac{a^2}{2}
$$
$\blacksquare$

Now let's go in the opposite direction: using the limit of Riemann sums to find the area under a curved parabola!

> [!example] Example: Finding the Area Under the Parabola $y = x^2$ via Riemann Sums
> Use the definition of the definite integral as a limit of Riemann sums to find the area of the region between the parabola $y = x^2$ and the $x$-axis on the interval $[0, b]$.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260925174637.webp)

**Solution:**
Since $f(x) = x^2$ is continuous on $[0, b]$, Theorem 1 guarantees that its definite integral exists, meaning *any* sequence of partitions with $\lVert P \rVert \to 0$ and *any* choice of sample points $c_k$ will converge to the exact area. For convenience, let us partition $[0, b]$ into $n$ subintervals of equal width $\Delta x_k = \dfrac{b - 0}{n} = \dfrac{b}{n}$:
$$
P = \left\{ 0, \frac{b}{n}, \frac{2b}{n}, \dots, \frac{nb}{n} \right\}
$$
Here, the right endpoint of the $k$-th subinterval is $x_k = \dfrac{kb}{n}$, and each subinterval has length $\Delta x_k = \dfrac{b}{n}$. Choosing the right endpoint as our sample point, $c_k = x_k = \dfrac{kb}{n}$, we have
$$
f(c_k) = \left(\frac{kb}{n}\right)^2 = \frac{k^2 b^2}{n^2}
$$
Forming the Riemann sum $S_P$ and applying the sum of squares formula $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} k^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}$, we obtain:
$$
\begin{aligned}
S_P = \sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k)\Delta x_k &= \sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\frac{kb}{n}\right)^2 \cdot \frac{b}{n} \\
&= \frac{b^3}{n^3}\sum_{k=1}^{n}k^2 \\
&= \frac{b^3}{n^3} \cdot \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6} \\
&= \frac{b^3}{n^3} \cdot \left(\frac{2n^3 + 3n^2 + n}{6}\right) \\
&= \frac{b^3}{6}\left(2 + \frac{3}{n} + \frac{1}{n^2}\right)
\end{aligned}
$$
Now, as $\lVert P \rVert = \dfrac{b}{n} \to 0$, we have $n \to \infty$. Taking the limit gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^b x^2 \, dx = \lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0} S_P &= \lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{b^3}{6}\left(2 + \frac{3}{n} + \frac{1}{n^2}\right) \\
&= \frac{b^3}{6} \cdot 2 \\
&= \frac{b^3}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence, the area under the parabola $y = x^2$ on $[0, b]$ is $\dfrac{b^3}{3}$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Foreshadowing the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
> Look closely at the results of our last two examples! There is a striking connection between the value of the definite integral and the *antiderivative* of the integrand:
> - In $\displaystyle\int_a^b x \, dx = \frac{b^2}{2} - \frac{a^2}{2}$, the formula is evaluated using $\dfrac{x^2}{2}$, which is an antiderivative of $x$.
> - In $\displaystyle\int_0^b x^2 \, dx = \frac{b^3}{3} - 0$, the formula is evaluated using $\dfrac{x^3}{3}$, which is an antiderivative of $x^2$.
>
> This is no coincidence—it is our first glimpse of the **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus**, which we will prove in Section 4.7!

## 4.5 Exercises

### Problem 47

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> What values of $a$ and $b$ maximize the value of the definite integral
> $$
> \int_a^b (x - x^2) \, dx \, ?
> $$

**Solution:**
Recall that the definite integral measures the net signed area between the curve $y = x - x^2$ and the $x$-axis. As we expand the interval $[a, b]$, the integral increases whenever it includes regions where the integrand is positive ($x - x^2 > 0$) and decreases whenever it includes regions where the integrand is negative ($x - x^2 < 0$).

Factoring the integrand as $f(x) = x(1 - x)$, we see that $f(x) \ge 0$ precisely on the interval $[0, 1]$ and $f(x) < 0$ everywhere outside $[0, 1]$. Therefore, to capture all of the positive area and none of the negative area, the integral $\displaystyle\int_a^b (x - x^2) \, dx$ is maximized when $a = 0$ and $b = 1$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 79: Upper and Lower Sums for Increasing Functions

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> **(a)** Suppose $f(x)$ is an increasing and continuous function on the interval $[a, b]$. Use a regular partition of equal subinterval length $\Delta x = \dfrac{b - a}{n}$ and the difference $U - L$ between the upper and lower sums to show that
> $$
> \lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0}(U - L) = 0
> $$
> As an intermediate result, also show that the difference $U - L$ is equal to the area of the tall stacked rectangle shown at the right of the figure below:
>
> ![](Pasted%20image%2020260925175856.webp)
>
> **(b)** Suppose that instead of being equal, the lengths $\Delta x_k$ of the subintervals of the partition of $[a, b]$ vary in size. Show that
> $$
> U - L \le |f(b) - f(a)| \Delta x_{\max}, \qquad \text{where } \Delta x_{\max} = \lVert P \rVert
> $$
> and hence prove that $\displaystyle\lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0}(U - L) = 0$.

**Solution:**

**(a)** Recall that the upper and lower sums are defined by
$$
U = \sum_{k=1}^{n} M_k \Delta x_k \quad \text{and} \quad L = \sum_{k=1}^{n} m_k \Delta x_k
$$
Because $f(x)$ is an increasing function on $[a, b]$, on every subinterval $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$ it achieves its minimum at the left endpoint, $m_k = f(x_{k-1})$, and its maximum at the right endpoint, $M_k = f(x_k)$. Since each subinterval has equal width $\Delta x_k = \dfrac{b - a}{n}$, subtracting $L$ from $U$ produces a **telescoping sum**:
$$
\begin{aligned}
U - L &= \frac{b - a}{n} \sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[f(x_k) - f(x_{k-1})\right] \\
&= \frac{b - a}{n} \cdot \left[f(x_n) - f(x_0)\right] \\
&= \frac{b - a}{n} \cdot \left[f(b) - f(a)\right]
\end{aligned}
$$
Geometrically, this shows that sliding all the small difference rectangles $[f(x_k) - f(x_{k-1})] \cdot \dfrac{b - a}{n}$ horizontally into a single column stacks them neatly into a single rectangle of base $\dfrac{b - a}{n}$ and total height $f(b) - f(a)$, matching the figure.

Now, as $\lVert P \rVert = \dfrac{b - a}{n} \to 0$, we have $n \to \infty$, so
$$
\lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0}(U - L) = \lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{b - a}{n} \cdot \left[f(b) - f(a)\right] = 0
$$
This proves that every increasing continuous function on $[a, b]$ is integrable (and the exact same telescoping argument applies to decreasing functions).

**(b)** Now suppose the subinterval widths $\Delta x_k$ vary in size, with $\Delta x_k \le \Delta x_{\max} = \lVert P \rVert$ for every $k = 1, \dots, n$. Because $f$ is increasing, $f(x_k) - f(x_{k-1}) \ge 0$ for each $k$, so replacing $\Delta x_k$ with $\Delta x_{\max}$ can only increase (or keep equal) the sum:
$$
\begin{aligned}
U - L &= \sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[f(x_k) - f(x_{k-1})\right]\Delta x_k \\
&\le \sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[f(x_k) - f(x_{k-1})\right]\Delta x_{\max} \\
&= \Delta x_{\max}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[f(x_k) - f(x_{k-1})\right] \\
&= \left[f(b) - f(a)\right] \cdot \Delta x_{\max} \\
&= \left[f(b) - f(a)\right] \cdot \lVert P \rVert
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $0 \le U - L \le \left[f(b) - f(a)\right] \lVert P \rVert$, taking the limit as $\lVert P \rVert \to 0$ and applying the Sandwich Theorem gives:
$$
0 \le \lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0} (U - L) \le \lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0}\left[f(b) - f(a)\right]\lVert P \rVert = 0 \implies \lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0}(U - L) = 0
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 84

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Use the trigonometric summation identity
> $$
> \sum_{k=1}^{n}\sin kh = \frac{\cos(h/2) - \cos\left(\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)h\right)}{2\sin(h/2)}
> $$
> to find the area under the curve $y = \sin x$ from $x = 0$ to $x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$.

**Solution:**
Let $P$ be the regular partition of $\left[0, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right]$ into $n$ subintervals of equal width $h = \Delta x_k = \dfrac{\pi}{2n}$:
$$
P = \left\{ 0, \frac{\pi}{2n}, \frac{2\pi}{2n}, \frac{3\pi}{2n}, \dots, \frac{n\pi}{2n} \right\}
$$
Because $f(x) = \sin x$ is strictly increasing on $\left[0, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right]$, the upper Riemann sum $U$ evaluates $\sin x$ at the right endpoints $x_k = \dfrac{k\pi}{2n} = kh$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
U &= \sum_{k=1}^{n} f(x_k) \Delta x_k \\
&= \sum_{k=1}^{n}\sin\left(k\frac{\pi}{2n}\right) \cdot \frac{\pi}{2n} \\
&= h \sum_{k=1}^{n}\sin(kh)
\end{aligned}
$$
Applying the given summation formula with $h = \dfrac{\pi}{2n}$ (so that $nh = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$), we obtain:
$$
U = h \cdot \left(\frac{\cos\left(\frac{h}{2}\right) - \cos\left(\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)h\right)}{2\sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)}\right) = \frac{\cos\left(\frac{h}{2}\right) - \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + \frac{h}{2}\right)}{\dfrac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2}}
$$
As $\lVert P \rVert \to 0$, we have $n \to \infty$ and therefore $h = \dfrac{\pi}{2n} \to 0$. Using the fundamental trigonometric limit $\displaystyle\lim_{u \to 0} \frac{\sin u}{u} = 1$ with $u = \dfrac{h}{2}$, we find:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin x \, dx = \lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0} U &= \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\cos\left(\frac{h}{2}\right) - \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + \frac{h}{2}\right)}{\dfrac{\sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)}{\frac{h}{2}}} \\
&= \frac{\displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0}\cos\left(\frac{h}{2}\right) - \lim_{h \to 0}\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + \frac{h}{2}\right)}{\displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)}{\frac{h}{2}}} \\
&= \frac{\cos(0) - \cos(\pi/2)}{1} \\
&= \frac{1 - 0}{1} = 1
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus, the area under one arch-quarter of the sine curve is
$$
\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin x \, dx = 1
$$
$\blacksquare$

# 4.6 Properties, Area and the Mean Value Theorem

## Properties of the Definite Integral

To work effectively with definite integrals without having to set up a limit of Riemann sums every single time, we need a toolkit of algebraic and order properties. The first two properties are convenient definitions that extend the notation $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx$ to cases where the upper limit equals or is smaller than the lower limit; the remaining properties follow directly from the definition of the definite integral as a limit of Riemann sums.

### 1. Zero-Width Interval Rule (Definition)

> [!abstract] Definition: Zero-Width Interval Rule
> For any function $f$ defined at $x = a$, the definite integral from $a$ to $a$ is defined to be zero:
> $$
> \int_a^a f(x) \, dx = 0
> $$

### 2. Order of Integration Rule (Definition)

> [!abstract] Definition: Reversing Limits of Integration
> If $f$ is integrable on $[a, b]$, then reversing the limits of integration reverses the sign of the definite integral:
> $$
> \int_a^b f(x) \, dx = -\int_b^a f(x) \, dx
> $$

### 3. Constant Multiple Rule

> [!theorem] Rule 3: Constant Multiple Rule for Definite Integrals
> If $f$ is integrable on $[a, b]$ and $k$ is any real constant, then $k \cdot f(x)$ is also integrable on $[a, b]$ and
> $$
> \int_a^b k f(x) \, dx = k\int_a^b f(x) \, dx
> $$

**Proof 1 ($\varepsilon$-$\delta$ Proof):**
Let $I = \displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx$. (If $k = 0$, both sides equal $0$ trivially, so assume $k \neq 0$.) We must show that for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that for all partitions $P$ of $[a, b]$ with $\lVert P \rVert < \delta$,
$$
\left|S_P - kI\right| < \varepsilon, \qquad \text{where } S_P = \sum_{j=1}^{n} k \cdot f(c_j) \cdot \Delta x_j
$$
Factoring $|k|$ out of the absolute value shows that this condition is equivalent to:
$$
|k| \cdot \left|\sum_{j=1}^{n} f(c_j)\Delta x_j - I\right| < \varepsilon \iff \left|\sum_{j=1}^{n} f(c_j)\Delta x_j - I\right| < \frac{\varepsilon}{|k|}
$$
Since $f$ is integrable on $[a, b]$ with integral $I$, we can find a $\delta > 0$ corresponding to the positive tolerance $\dfrac{\varepsilon}{|k|} > 0$ such that every partition $P$ with $\lVert P \rVert < \delta$ satisfies $\left|S_P' - I\right| < \dfrac{\varepsilon}{|k|}$, where $S_P' = \displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n} f(c_j)\Delta x_j$. Multiplying by $|k|$ shows that this exact same $\delta$ guarantees $|S_P - kI| < \varepsilon$. Therefore,
$$
\int_a^b k \cdot f(x) \, dx = kI = k \cdot \int_a^b f(x) \, dx
$$
$\blacksquare$

**Proof 2 (Limit Law Proof):**
Since $f$ is integrable on $[a, b]$, we know that $\displaystyle\lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0} \sum_{j=1}^{n} f(c_j) \Delta x_j = I$. Applying the constant multiple law for limits directly yields:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_a^b k \cdot f(x) \, dx &= \lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0} \sum_{j=1}^{n} k \cdot f(c_j) \Delta x_j \\
&= k \cdot \lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0} \sum_{j=1}^{n} f(c_j) \Delta x_j \\
&= kI = k\int_a^b f(x) \, dx
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

### 4. Sum and Difference Rule

> [!theorem] Rule 4: Sum and Difference Rule for Definite Integrals
> If $f$ and $g$ are both integrable on $[a, b]$, then $f \pm g$ is also integrable on $[a, b]$ and
> $$
> \int_a^b \left[f(x) \pm g(x)\right] dx = \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \pm \int_a^b g(x) \, dx
> $$

**Proof 1 ($\varepsilon$-$\delta$ Proof):**
Note that this property requires both integrals on the right-hand side to exist. Let $I_1 = \displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx$ and $I_2 = \displaystyle\int_a^b g(x) \, dx$. We claim that for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that for all partitions $P$ of $[a, b]$ with $\lVert P \rVert < \delta$,
$$
\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[f(c_k) \pm g(c_k)\right]\Delta x_k - \left(I_1 \pm I_2\right)\right| < \varepsilon
$$
Let $S_P = \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k)\Delta x_k$ and $S_P' = \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} g(c_k)\Delta x_k$. By the Triangle Inequality, we can split the error into two pieces:
$$
\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[f(c_k) \pm g(c_k)\right]\Delta x_k - \left(I_1 \pm I_2\right)\right| = \left|(S_P - I_1) \pm (S_P' - I_2)\right| \le \left|S_P - I_1\right| + \left|S_P' - I_2\right|
$$
Because both $f$ and $g$ are integrable on $[a, b]$, there exist $\delta_1 > 0$ and $\delta_2 > 0$ such that:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lVert P \rVert < \delta_1 &\implies \left|S_P - I_1\right| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \\
\lVert P \rVert < \delta_2 &\implies \left|S_P' - I_2\right| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$
Choosing $\delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2) > 0$, any partition $P$ with $\lVert P \rVert < \delta$ satisfies both bounds simultaneously:
$$
\left|S_P - I_1\right| + \left|S_P' - I_2\right| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} = \varepsilon
$$
Consequently,
$$
\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[f(c_k) \pm g(c_k)\right]\Delta x_k - \left(I_1 \pm I_2\right)\right| < \varepsilon
$$
which proves that $\displaystyle\int_a^b \left[f(x) \pm g(x)\right] dx = \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \pm \int_a^b g(x) \, dx$. $\blacksquare$

**Proof 2 (Limit Law Proof):**
Since both $f$ and $g$ are integrable on $[a, b]$, the sum and difference law for limits gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_a^b \left[f(x) \pm g(x)\right] dx &= \lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0} \sum_{k=1}^{n} \left[f(c_k) \pm g(c_k)\right]\Delta x_k \\
&= \lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0} \sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k) \Delta x_k \pm \lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0} \sum_{k=1}^{n} g(c_k)\Delta x_k \\
&= \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \pm \int_a^b g(x) \, dx
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Linearity for Any Finite Linear Combination
> By combining Rules 3 and 4 with mathematical induction, we see that any finite linear combination of integrable functions is also integrable, and its integral is the same linear combination of the individual integrals:
> $$
> \int_a^b \left[c_1 f_1(x) + c_2 f_2(x) + \dots + c_n f_n(x)\right] dx = c_1\int_a^b f_1(x) \, dx + c_2\int_a^b f_2(x) \, dx + \dots + c_n\int_a^b f_n(x) \, dx
> $$

### 5. Additivity Over Adjacent Intervals

> [!theorem] Rule 5: Additivity of Definite Integrals
> If $f$ is integrable on the intervals joining $a$, $b$, and $c$, then
> $$
> \int_a^b f(x) \, dx + \int_b^c f(x) \, dx = \int_a^c f(x) \, dx
> $$

**Proof:**
Suppose $a < b < c$. Let $P_1$ be a partition of $[a, b]$ and $P_2$ be a partition of $[b, c]$; then their union $P = P_1 \cup P_2$ is a partition of $[a, c]$ that includes $b$ as a partition point. Let $S_{P_1}$ and $S_{P_2}$ be Riemann sums of $f$ on $P_1$ and $P_2$, respectively. Then $S_P = S_{P_1} + S_{P_2}$ is a Riemann sum of $f$ on $P$. Taking the limit as $\lVert P \rVert \to 0$ (which forces both $\lVert P_1 \rVert \to 0$ and $\lVert P_2 \rVert \to 0$) yields:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0} S_P &= \lim_{\lVert P_1 \rVert \to 0} S_{P_1} + \lim_{\lVert P_2 \rVert \to 0} S_{P_2} \\
\int_a^c f(x) \, dx &= \int_a^b f(x) \, dx + \int_b^c f(x) \, dx
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Geometric Meaning of Additivity
> Geometrically, additivity expresses the intuitive fact that the signed area under the curve over $[a, c]$ is the sum of the signed areas over $[a, b]$ and $[b, c]$:

### 6. Max-Min Inequality

> [!theorem] Rule 6: Max-Min Inequality
> If $f$ has maximum value $\max f$ and minimum value $\min f$ on $[a, b]$, then
> $$
> \min f \cdot (b - a) \le \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \le \max f \cdot (b - a)
> $$

**Proof:**
For any partition $P$ of $[a, b]$ and any Riemann sum $S_P = \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k)\Delta x_k$, we know that $L(f, P) \le S_P \le U(f, P)$. Since the minimum $m_k$ on each subinterval $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$ is at least the global minimum $\min f$ on $[a, b]$, we have:
$$
\begin{aligned}
L(f, P) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} m_k\Delta x_k &\ge \sum_{k=1}^{n} (\min f) \cdot \Delta x_k = \min f \cdot (b - a) \\
\implies \min f \cdot (b - a) &\le L(f, P)
\end{aligned}
$$
By identical reasoning on the upper sum, $U(f, P) \le \max f \cdot (b - a)$. Combining these inequalities traps every Riemann sum $S_P$ between the global minimum and maximum rectangle areas:
$$
\min f \cdot (b - a) \le S_P \le \max f \cdot (b - a)
$$
Taking the limit as $\lVert P \rVert \to 0$, where $S_P \to I = \displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx$, preserves the non-strict inequalities:
$$
\min f \cdot (b - a) \le \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \le \max f \cdot (b - a)
$$
$\blacksquare$

### 7. Domination Rule

> [!theorem] Rule 7: Domination Rule
> If $f(x) \ge g(x)$ on $[a, b]$, then
> $$
> \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \ge \int_a^b g(x) \, dx
> $$

**Proof:**
Suppose $f(x) \ge g(x)$ for all $x \in [a, b]$. Then for any partition $P$ of $[a, b]$ and any choice of sample points $c_k \in [x_{k-1}, x_k]$, we have $f(c_k)\Delta x_k \ge g(c_k)\Delta x_k$, so the corresponding Riemann sums satisfy $S_P \ge S_P'$:
$$
S_P = \sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k)\Delta x_k \ge S_P' = \sum_{k=1}^{n} g(c_k)\Delta x_k
$$
Taking the limit as $\lVert P \rVert \to 0$ on both sides preserves the inequality:
$$
\lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0} S_P \ge \lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0} S_P' \implies \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \ge \int_a^b g(x) \, dx
$$
$\blacksquare$

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Special Case for Non-Negative Functions
> Setting $g(x) = 0$ in the Domination Rule gives an important special case: if $f(x) \ge 0$ on $[a, b]$, then $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx \ge 0$.

## Integration and Total Area

As we noted in Section 4.5, the definite integral $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx$ computes the **net signed area** between the graph of $f$ and the $x$-axis—that is, the sum of the areas of regions above the $x$-axis minus the sum of the areas of regions below the $x$-axis:

![](Pasted%20image%2020260925191945.webp)

$$
\int_a^b f(x) \, dx = A_1 + A_3 - A_2
$$

If instead we want the **total geometric area** $A = A_1 + A_2 + A_3$ of the region bounded between $f(x)$ and the $x$-axis, we must prevent negative contributions from canceling positive ones.

### Procedure for Finding the Total Area Between $f(x)$ and the $x$-Axis
1. Find all the zeros of $f$ in $[a, b]$ (points where $f(x) = 0$).
2. Partition $[a, b]$ at these zeros into subintervals on which $f(x)$ does not change sign.
3. Integrate $f$ over each subinterval separately.
4. Add the **absolute values** of the resulting subinterval integrals.

## Average Value of a Continuous Function

We all know how to find the average (arithmetic mean) of $n$ discrete numbers $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$: we simply add them up and divide by $n$, giving $\dfrac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n a_i$. But how do we define the average value of a *continuously varying* function $f(x)$ over an interval $[a, b]$?

Let's approximate the continuous function by sampling it at $n$ evenly spaced points! Divide $[a, b]$ into $n$ subintervals of equal width $\Delta x_k = \dfrac{b - a}{n}$, and pick one sample point $c_k$ from each subinterval ($c_1, c_2, \dots, c_n$). The ordinary arithmetic average of these $n$ sampled function values is:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Average of Sample} &= \frac{f(c_1) + f(c_2) + \dots + f(c_n)}{n} \\
&= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k)
\end{aligned}
$$
Now, since $\Delta x_k = \dfrac{b - a}{n}$, we can rewrite $\dfrac{1}{n}$ as $\dfrac{\Delta x_k}{b - a}$. Substituting this into our sample average turns the sum into a Riemann sum!
$$
\text{Average of Sample} = \frac{1}{b - a}\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k)\Delta x_k = \frac{1}{b - a} \cdot (\text{Riemann Sum})
$$
As we let the number of sample points $n \to \infty$, the subinterval width $\Delta x_k = \lVert P \rVert \to 0$, and the Riemann sum converges to the definite integral $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx$:
$$
\lim_{n \to \infty}(\text{Average of Sample}) = \lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0}\frac{1}{b - a}\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k)\Delta x_k = \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b f(x) \, dx
$$
This motivates our formal definition of the average value of an integrable function.

> [!abstract] Definition: Average (Mean) Value of a Function
> If $f$ is integrable on $[a, b]$, its **average (mean) value** on $[a, b]$ is
> $$
> \text{av}(f) = \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b f(x) \, dx
> $$

## The Mean Value Theorem for Definite Integrals

For a discrete list of numbers, the average value does not have to equal any number in the list (for instance, the average of $1$ and $2$ is $1.5$). However, for a *continuous* function on $[a, b]$, the function cannot jump over its own average value—it must actually attain its average value at least once in the interval!

> [!theorem] Theorem 2: The Mean Value Theorem for Definite Integrals
> If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, then at some point $c$ in $[a, b]$,
> $$
> f(c) = \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b f(x) \, dx
> $$
> In other words, the average value of a continuous function on $[a, b]$ is attained by the function at some point $c \in [a, b]$.

**Proof:**
We start with the Max-Min Inequality (Rule 6) proved earlier:
$$
\min f \cdot (b - a) \le \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \le \max f \cdot (b - a)
$$
Dividing through by the positive length $b - a$ traps the average value of $f$ between the minimum and maximum values of $f$ on $[a, b]$:
$$
\min f \le \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b f(x) \, dx \le \max f
$$
Because $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$, it attains both $\min f$ and $\max f$ at points in $[a, b]$. By the **Intermediate Value Theorem** for continuous functions, $f$ must take on every value between $\min f$ and $\max f$ at some point in $[a, b]$. In particular, there exists at least one point $c \in [a, b]$ such that
$$
f(c) = \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b f(x) \, dx
$$
$\blacksquare$

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Zero Integral Implies a Root for Continuous Functions
> As an immediate consequence of the Mean Value Theorem for Definite Integrals, if $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ (with $a \neq b$) and $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx = 0$, then there must exist some point $c \in [a, b]$ where $f(c) = 0$. That is, $f(x)$ is guaranteed to have a root in $[a, b]$.

> [!example] Example: Finding the Average Value Geometrically
> Find the average value of $f(x) = 1 - \sqrt{1 - x^2}$ on the interval $[-1, 1]$.

**Solution:**
We do not need an antiderivative formula to evaluate this integral; instead, we can compute the area under $f(x)$ geometrically and divide by the interval length $1 - (-1) = 2$.

Notice that $y = \sqrt{1 - x^2}$ is the upper half of the unit circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ on $[-1, 1]$. Therefore, the region under $f(x) = 1 - \sqrt{1 - x^2}$ on $[-1, 1]$ is the $2 \times 1$ rectangle of area $2 \times 1 = 2$ with the upper unit semicircle of area $\dfrac{1}{2}\pi(1)^2 = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ removed:

![](Pasted%20image%2020260925193303.webp)

$$
\text{Area} = \int_{-1}^1 \left(1 - \sqrt{1 - x^2}\right) dx = 2 \times 1 - \frac{1}{2}\pi \cdot 1^2 = 2 - \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{4 - \pi}{2}
$$
Dividing this area by the interval length $1 - (-1) = 2$ gives the average value of $f(x)$ on $[-1, 1]$:
$$
\text{av}(f) = \frac{1}{1 - (-1)} \cdot \frac{4 - \pi}{2} = \frac{4 - \pi}{4}
$$
$\blacksquare$

> Suppose that $f$ and $g$ are continuous on $[a, b]$ ($a \neq b$), and that
> $$
> \int_a^b \left[f(x) - g(x)\right] dx = 0
> $$
> Show that $f(x) = g(x)$ at least once in $[a, b]$.

**Solution:**
Since $f$ and $g$ are continuous on $[a, b]$, their difference $h(x) = f(x) - g(x)$ is also continuous on $[a, b]$. Applying the Mean Value Theorem for Definite Integrals to $h(x)$ on $[a, b]$, there exists at least one point $c \in [a, b]$ such that
$$
f(c) - g(c) = h(c) = \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b \left[f(x) - g(x)\right] dx = \frac{1}{b - a} \cdot 0 = 0
$$
Therefore, $f(c) = g(c)$ at least once in $[a, b]$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 45

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Using the Max-Min Inequality, show that on $[a, b]$ (with $a < b$):
> 1. If $f(x) \ge 0$ on $[a, b]$, then $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx \ge 0$.
> 2. If $f(x) \le 0$ on $[a, b]$, then $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx \le 0$.

**Solution:**
1. By the Max-Min Inequality, we have $\min f \cdot (b - a) \le \displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx$. Since $f(x) \ge 0$ for all $x \in [a, b]$, the minimum value satisfies $\min f \ge 0$. Because $b - a > 0$, it follows that:
   $$
   0 = 0 \cdot (b - a) \le \min f \cdot (b - a) \le \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \implies \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \ge 0
   $$
2. Similarly, the upper bound of the Max-Min Inequality states that $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx \le \max f \cdot (b - a)$. Since $f(x) \le 0$ on $[a, b]$, we have $\max f \le 0$, and therefore:
   $$
   \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \le \max f \cdot (b - a) \le 0 \cdot (b - a) = 0 \implies \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \le 0
   $$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 48

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> The inequality $\sec x \ge 1 + \dfrac{x^2}{2}$ holds on $\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)$. Use it to find a lower bound for the value of $\displaystyle\int_0^1 \sec x \, dx$.

**Solution:**
Since $[0, 1] \subset \left(-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)$, the inequality $\sec x \ge 1 + \dfrac{x^2}{2}$ holds throughout $[0, 1]$. Applying the Domination Rule, together with our known integrals for $1$ and $x^2$, gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1 \sec x \, dx &\ge \int_0^1 \left(1 + \frac{x^2}{2}\right) dx \\
&= \int_0^1 1 \, dx + \frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 x^2 \, dx \\
&= 1 + \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1^3 - 0^3}{3}\right) \\
&= 1 + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{7}{6}
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus, $\dfrac{7}{6}$ is a lower bound for $\displaystyle\int_0^1 \sec x \, dx$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 49

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> If $\text{av}(f)$ really is a typical value of the integrable function $f(x)$ on $[a, b]$, then the constant function $\text{av}(f)$ should have the same integral over $[a, b]$ that $f$ does. Does it?

**Solution:**
Yes, it does! By the definition of the average value of $f$ on $[a, b]$, we have
$$
\text{av}(f) = \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b f(x) \, dx \implies \text{av}(f) \cdot (b - a) = \int_a^b f(x) \, dx
$$
Now observe that $\displaystyle\int_a^b 1 \, dx = b - a$. Substituting $\displaystyle\int_a^b 1 \, dx$ for $b - a$ and pulling the constant number $\text{av}(f)$ inside the integral via the Constant Multiple Rule gives:
$$
\text{av}(f)\int_a^b 1 \, dx = \int_a^b \text{av}(f) \, dx = \int_a^b f(x) \, dx
$$
Geometrically, a rectangle of height $\text{av}(f)$ and base $b - a$ has the exact same signed area as the curve $y = f(x)$ over $[a, b]$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 50

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Prove the following algebraic and order rules for the average value of integrable functions on $[a, b]$:
> 1. $\text{av}(f \pm g) = \text{av}(f) \pm \text{av}(g)$
> 2. $\text{av}(kf) = k \, \text{av}(f) \quad (\forall k \in \mathbb{R})$
> 3. $\text{av}(f) \le \text{av}(g)$ if $f(x) \le g(x)$ on $[a, b]$

**Solution:**
1. **Sum and Difference Rule for Average Value:** Using the Sum and Difference Rule for definite integrals,
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{av}(f \pm g) &= \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b \left[f(x) \pm g(x)\right] dx \\
   &= \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b f(x) \, dx \pm \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b g(x) \, dx \\
   &= \text{av}(f) \pm \text{av}(g)
   \end{aligned}
   $$
2. **Constant Multiple Rule for Average Value:** Using the Constant Multiple Rule for definite integrals,
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \text{av}(kf) &= \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b k \cdot f(x) \, dx \\
   &= k \cdot \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b f(x) \, dx \\
   &= k \cdot \text{av}(f)
   \end{aligned}
   $$
3. **Domination Rule for Average Value:** If $f(x) \le g(x)$ on $[a, b]$, the Domination Rule for definite integrals gives $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx \le \int_a^b g(x) \, dx$. Dividing both sides by $b - a > 0$ preserves the inequality:
   $$
   \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b f(x) \, dx \le \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b g(x) \, dx \implies \text{av}(f) \le \text{av}(g)
   $$
$\blacksquare$

# 4.7 The Fundamental Theorem

Until the seventeenth century, the study of tangent lines (differentiation) and the study of areas under curves (integration) appeared to be two completely unrelated branches of geometry. The **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus**, discovered independently by Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, revealed that differentiation and definite integration are actually *inverse operations* of one another!

## The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Part 1)

Suppose $f(t)$ is a continuous function on $[a, b]$. If we fix the lower limit at $a$ and allow the upper limit $x \in [a, b]$ to vary, the definite integral defines a new "area-so-far" function $F(x) = \displaystyle\int_a^x f(t) \, dt$. How fast does this accumulated area change as we move the right endpoint $x$?

> [!theorem] Theorem 3: The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Part 1)
> If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, then the function
> $$
> F(x) = \int_a^x f(t) \, dt
> $$
> has a derivative at every point $x$ in $[a, b]$, and
> $$
> \frac{dF}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t) \, dt = f(x), \qquad a \le x \le b
> $$

**Proof:**
We apply the limit definition of the derivative directly to $F(x)$, combined with the additivity property of definite integrals:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dF}{dx} &= \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{F(x + h) - F(x)}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\displaystyle\int_a^{x+h} f(t) \, dt - \int_a^x f(t) \, dt}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h} f(t) \, dt
\end{aligned}
$$
Suppose first that $h > 0$. By the **Mean Value Theorem for Definite Integrals** (Theorem 2), the continuous function $f$ attains its average value on the closed interval $[x, x + h]$ at some point $c \in [x, x + h]$:
$$
\frac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h} f(t) \, dt = f(c) \qquad \text{for some } c \in [x, x + h]
$$
Substituting $f(c)$ into our limit gives:
$$
\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h} f(t) \, dt = \lim_{h \to 0} f(c)
$$
Now, as $h \to 0$, the right endpoint $x + h \to x$, which squeezes the trapped point $c \in [x, x + h]$ toward $x$ ($c \to x$). Because $f$ is continuous at $x$, we conclude that:
$$
\lim_{h \to 0} f(c) = \lim_{c \to x} f(c) = f(x) \implies \frac{dF}{dx} = f(x)
$$
$\blacksquare$

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: What If $h < 0$?
> The calculation above assumed $h > 0$ so that $[x, x + h]$ was a standard interval. What happens when $h < 0$ (so $x + h < x$)? Using additivity on $[x + h, x]$, we write:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \lim_{h \to 0^-}\frac{\displaystyle\int_a^{x+h} f(t) \, dt - \int_a^x f(t) \, dt}{h} &= \lim_{h \to 0^-}\frac{\displaystyle\int_a^{x+h} f(t) \, dt + \int_{x+h}^x f(t) \, dt - \int_{x+h}^x f(t) \, dt - \int_a^x f(t) \, dt}{h} \\
> &= \lim_{h \to 0^-}\frac{-\displaystyle\int_{x+h}^x f(t) \, dt}{h} \\
> &= \lim_{h \to 0^-}\frac{1}{-h}\int_{x+h}^x f(t) \, dt \\
> &= \lim_{h \to 0^-} f(c) = f(x)
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> Notice that since $h < 0$, the positive length of the interval $[x + h, x]$ is $x - (x + h) = -h$, so $\dfrac{1}{-h}\displaystyle\int_{x+h}^x f(t) \, dt$ is once again the exact average value of $f$ on $[x + h, x]$!

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: What If the Lower Limit Is Greater Than $x$?
> What if we define $F(x) = \displaystyle\int_b^x f(t) \, dt$, where $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $x \in [a, b]$ (so the lower limit $b$ is to the right of $x$)? Do we still get $\dfrac{dF}{dx} = f(x)$?
>
> Let's check the difference quotient:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \frac{F(x + h) - F(x)}{h} &= \frac{\displaystyle\int_b^{x+h} f(t) \, dt - \int_b^x f(t) \, dt}{h} \\
> &= \frac{-\displaystyle\int_{x+h}^b f(t) \, dt + \int_x^b f(t) \, dt}{h} \\
> &= \frac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h} f(t) \, dt \qquad (\text{assuming } h \to 0^+)
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> Taking the limit as $h \to 0$ yields the exact same result:
> $$
> \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{F(x + h) - F(x)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h} f(t) \, dt = \lim_{c \to x} f(c) = f(x)
> $$
> Thus, $F(x) = \displaystyle\int_a^x f(t) \, dt$ is an antiderivative of $f(x)$ regardless of the relative position of $a$ and $x$, as long as $f$ is continuous on the closed interval joining $a$ and $x$!

### Geometric Interpretation of Part 1

Geometrically, the derivative $F'(x)$ measures the instantaneous rate at which the signed area under $f$ over $[a, x]$ changes as we slide the right wall $x$:

![](Pasted%20image%2020260925194450.webp)

If we move the right endpoint slightly from $x$ to $x + dx$, the change in area $\Delta F$ is approximately the area of a thin vertical strip of height $f(x)$ and width $dx$:
$$
\Delta F \approx f(x) \, dx
$$
On the other hand, from differential calculus we know that the linear change in $F$ is $\Delta F \approx F'(x) \, dx$. Equating the two expressions gives:
$$
F'(x) \, dx = f(x) \, dx \implies F'(x) = f(x)
$$
What is equally neat is that if $f(x)$ is negative at $x$, then $f(x) \, dx$ is negative too, meaning that moving $x$ to the right *subtracts* from the net signed area at the instantaneous rate $f(x)$.

> [!summary] Three Profound Consequences of the Fundamental Theorem (Part 1)
> 1. **Solvability of Differential Equations:** Every differential equation of the form $\dfrac{dF}{dx} = f(x)$ has a solution whenever $f$ is continuous.
> 2. **Existence of Antiderivatives:** Every continuous function $f$ is the derivative of some other function—namely, $F(x) = \displaystyle\int_a^x f(t) \, dt$.
> 3. **Inverse Relationship:** Integration and differentiation are inverse processes of each other.

## The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Part 2)

While Part 1 tells us that $\displaystyle\int_a^x f(t) \, dt$ is an antiderivative of $f$, **Part 2** (also called the *Integral Evaluation Theorem*) flips this relationship around to give us a breathtakingly simple shortcut for evaluating any definite integral $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx$ without ever computing a limit of Riemann sums—provided we can find *any* antiderivative $F(x)$ of $f(x)$!

> [!theorem] Theorem 4: The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Part 2)
> If $f$ is continuous at every point of $[a, b]$ and $F$ is *any* antiderivative of $f$ on $[a, b]$, then
> $$
> \int_a^b f(x) \, dx = F(b) - F(a)
> $$

**Proof:**
By Part 1 of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, we already know one specific antiderivative of $f$ on $[a, b]$, namely the area function
$$
G(x) = \int_a^x f(t) \, dt
$$
Because any two antiderivatives of the same function on $[a, b]$ have the same derivative $f(x)$, Corollary 2 of the Mean Value Theorem guarantees that $F(x)$ and $G(x)$ differ only by a constant $C$ on $[a, b]$:
$$
F(x) = G(x) + C
$$
Evaluating the difference $F(b) - F(a)$ causes the constant $C$ to cancel out:
$$
\begin{aligned}
F(b) - F(a) &= \left[G(b) + C\right] - \left[G(a) + C\right] \\
&= G(b) - G(a) \\
&= \int_a^b f(t) \, dt - \int_a^a f(t) \, dt \\
&= \int_a^b f(t) \, dt - 0 \\
&= \int_a^b f(t) \, dt
\end{aligned}
$$
Relabeling the dummy variable of integration from $t$ to $x$ gives $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx = F(b) - F(a)$. $\blacksquare$

## Differentiating Integrals When the Limit of Integration Is a Function of $x$

What happens in Part 1 of the Fundamental Theorem if the upper limit of integration is not simply $x$, but a differentiable function $u(x)$ such as $x^2$ or $\sin x$? Then the integral is a **composite function** of $x$, and we combine the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus with the **Chain Rule**!

> [!example] Example: Differentiating an Integral with a Variable Upper Limit $x^2$
> Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ if
> $$
> y = \int_1^{x^2} \cos t \, dt
> $$

**Solution:**
We treat $y$ as a composite function of $x$ by setting the upper limit equal to an intermediate variable $u = x^2$:
$$
y = \int_1^u \cos t \, dt, \qquad \text{where } u = x^2
$$
Applying the Chain Rule $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy}{du} \cdot \dfrac{du}{dx}$ along with Part 1 of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dy}{dx} &= \left(\frac{d}{du}\int_1^u \cos t \, dt\right) \cdot \frac{du}{dx} \\
&= \cos u \cdot \frac{du}{dx} \\
&= \cos(x^2) \cdot 2x = 2x\cos(x^2)
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

**Solution:**

### Problem 53

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ if
> $$
> y = \int_0^{\sin x}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{1 - t^2}}, \qquad |x| < \frac{\pi}{2}
> $$

**Solution:**
First, let's check that the integral is well-defined and the integrand is continuous. Since $|x| < \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, we have $-\dfrac{\pi}{2} < x < \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, which implies $-1 < \sin x < 1$. On the open interval $(-1, 1)$, the integrand $f(t) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 - t^2}}$ is defined and continuous.

Letting $u = \sin x$ and applying the Chain Rule with Part 1 of the Fundamental Theorem gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dy}{dx} &= \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx} \\
&= \left(\frac{d}{du}\int_0^u \frac{dt}{\sqrt{1 - t^2}}\right) \cdot \frac{du}{dx} \\
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - u^2}} \cdot \frac{du}{dx} \\
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \sin^2 x}} \cdot \cos x \\
&= \frac{\cos x}{|\cos x|}
\end{aligned}
$$
Because $\cos x > 0$ for all $x \in \left(-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)$, we have $|\cos x| = \cos x$, and therefore:
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\cos x}{\cos x} = 1 \qquad \forall x \in \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problems 59 & 61

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Express the solutions of the following initial value problems in terms of definite integrals:
> 1. $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \sec x, \quad y(2) = 3$
> 2. $\dfrac{ds}{dt} = f(t), \quad s(t_0) = s_0$

**Solution:**
1. Since $\displaystyle\int_2^x \sec t \, dt$ is an antiderivative of $\sec x$ (by FTC Part 1) and $y(x)$ is also an antiderivative of $\sec x$, they must differ by a constant $C$:
   $$
   y(x) = \int_2^x \sec t \, dt + C
   $$
   Notice why choosing the lower limit of integration to be $2$ (matching the initial point $x_0 = 2$) is so strategic: when we plug in $x = 2$, the integral from $2$ to $2$ vanishes immediately!
   $$
   y(2) = \int_2^2 \sec t \, dt + C = 0 + C = 3 \implies C = 3
   $$
   Therefore, the unique solution to the initial value problem is
   $$
   y = \int_2^x \sec t \, dt + 3
   $$

2. By the exact same reasoning, starting the integral at the initial time $t_0$ gives the general solution
   $$
   s(t) = \int_{t_0}^t f(x) \, dx + C
   $$
   Applying the initial condition $s(t_0) = s_0$ yields $s(t_0) = 0 + C = s_0$, so
   $$
   s = \int_{t_0}^t f(x) \, dx + s_0
   $$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 63: Archimedes' Area Formula for Parabolas

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Archimedes (287–212 BC)—inventor, military engineer, physicist, and the greatest mathematician of classical times in the Western world—discovered that the area under a parabolic arch is always two-thirds the base times the height.
> 1. **(a)** Use a definite integral to find the area under the parabolic arch $y = 6 - x - x^2$ over $-3 \le x \le 2$.
> 2. **(b)** Find the maximum height $h$ of the arch.
> 3. **(c)** Show that the area equals two-thirds the base $b$ times the height $h$.
> 4. **(d)** Sketch the symmetric parabolic arch $y = h - \left(\dfrac{4h}{b^2}\right)x^2$ over $-\dfrac{b}{2} \le x \le \dfrac{b}{2}$ assuming that $h$ and $b$ are positive. Then use calculus to find the area of the region enclosed between the arch and the $x$-axis.

**Solution:**

**(a)** Evaluating the definite integral of $y = 6 - x - x^2$ from $x = -3$ to $x = 2$ using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Part 2) gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Area} &= \int_{-3}^{2}\left(6 - x - x^2\right) dx \\
&= \left[6x - \frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-3}^{2} \\
&= \left(12 - 2 - \frac{8}{3}\right) - \left(-18 - \frac{9}{2} + \frac{27}{3}\right) \\
&= 10 - \frac{8}{3} + 18 + \frac{9}{2} - 9 \\
&= 19 - \frac{8}{3} + \frac{9}{2} \\
&= \frac{114 - 16 + 27}{6} = \frac{125}{6}
\end{aligned}
$$

**(b)** The height $h$ of the arch is the maximum value $y_{\max}$ of the downward-opening parabola:

![](Pasted%20image%2020260925210619.webp)

Setting the first derivative equal to zero to locate the vertex gives:
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = -1 - 2x = 0 \implies x = -\frac{1}{2} = -0.5
$$
Evaluating $y$ at $x = -\dfrac{1}{2}$ yields the height $h$:
$$
h = y_{\max} = 6 - \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) - \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 = 6 + \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{25}{4}
$$

**(c)** The base of the arch along the $x$-axis spans from $x = -3$ to $x = 2$, so its length is $b = 2 - (-3) = 5$. Computing two-thirds of the base times the height gives:
$$
\frac{2}{3} \cdot b \cdot h = \frac{2}{3} \cdot 5 \cdot \frac{25}{4} = \frac{125}{6}
$$
which matches the exact area we found by integration in part (a)!

**(d)** The symmetric parabolic arch $y = h - \left(\dfrac{4h}{b^2}\right)x^2$ has its vertex at $(0, h)$ and $x$-intercepts at $x = \pm\dfrac{b}{2}$, giving it height $h$ and base $b$:

![](Pasted%20image%2020260925210827.webp)

Integrating $y$ from $-\dfrac{b}{2}$ to $\dfrac{b}{2}$ gives the enclosed area:
$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \int_{-b/2}^{b/2}\left[h - \left(\frac{4h}{b^2}\right)x^2\right] dx \\
&= \left[hx - \frac{4h}{b^2} \cdot \frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-b/2}^{b/2} \\
&= \left(\frac{hb}{2} - \frac{4h}{b^2} \cdot \frac{b^3}{3 \cdot 8}\right) - \left(-\frac{hb}{2} + \frac{4h}{b^2} \cdot \frac{b^3}{3 \cdot 8}\right) \\
&= 2\left(\frac{hb}{2} - \frac{hb}{6}\right) \\
&= hb\left(1 - \frac{1}{3}\right) = \frac{2}{3}hb
\end{aligned}
$$
This proves Archimedes' general theorem for any parabolic arch of base $b$ and height $h$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 70: Volume of a Sphere via Riemann Sums and Definite Integration

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Find an approximating Riemann sum for the volume of a sphere of radius $r$ by slicing it into thin cylindrical disks, and then find the exact volume of the sphere by taking the limit to form a definite integral.

**Solution:**
Let us place the center of the sphere of radius $r$ at the origin, so that the sphere spans the interval $[-r, r]$ along the $x$-axis:

![](Pasted%20image%2020260925211401.webp)

To approximate the sphere's volume, we slice it perpendicular to the $x$-axis into $n$ thin right-circular cylindrical disks. Let $P$ be a partition of $[-r, r]$, where the $k$-th subinterval has thickness (height) $h_k = \Delta x_k$. At any sample point $c_k \in [x_{k-1}, x_k]$, the cross-sectional radius of the sphere by the Pythagorean theorem is
$$
r_k = \sqrt{r^2 - c_k^2}
$$
Thus, the volume of the $k$-th cylindrical disk is
$$
\pi r_k^2 h_k = \pi\left(r^2 - c_k^2\right) \Delta x_k
$$
Summing the volumes of all $n$ disks gives our approximating Riemann sum $V(r, P)$ for the volume of the sphere:
$$
\begin{aligned}
V(r, P) &= \sum_{k=1}^{n}\pi\left(r^2 - c_k^2\right)\Delta x_k \\
&= \sum_{k=1}^{n}\pi r^2 \Delta x_k - \sum_{k=1}^{n}\pi c_k^2 \Delta x_k \\
&= \pi r^2\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Delta x_k - \pi\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k) \Delta x_k, \qquad \text{where } f(x) = x^2 \\
&= \pi r^2 \cdot (2r) - \pi\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k) \Delta x_k
\end{aligned}
$$
Now, taking the limit as the norm of the partition $\lVert P \rVert \to 0$ converts the Riemann sum into a definite integral:
$$
\begin{aligned}
V = \lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0} V(r, P) &= 2\pi r^3 - \pi\lim_{\lVert P \rVert \to 0}\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k) \Delta x_k \\
&= 2\pi r^3 - \pi\int_{-r}^{r} x^2 \, dx \\
&= 2\pi r^3 - \pi\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-r}^{r} \\
&= 2\pi r^3 - \pi\left[\frac{r^3}{3} - \frac{(-r)^3}{3}\right] \\
&= 2\pi r^3 - \frac{2\pi r^3}{3} \\
&= \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus, the exact volume of a sphere of radius $r$ is $\dfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: The Slicing Method for Volumes
> Approximating 3D volumes by slicing a solid into thin cross-sectional slabs, forming a Riemann sum, and taking the limit to obtain a definite integral is a general and powerful technique that we will explore extensively in Chapter 5.

### Problem 73

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Show that if $k$ is a positive constant, then the area between the $x$-axis and one arch of the curve $y = \sin(kx)$ is $\dfrac{2}{k}$.

**Solution:**
For $k > 0$, the curve $y = \sin(kx)$ completes one full positive arch as $kx$ goes from $0$ to $\pi$, which corresponds to $x \in \left[0, \dfrac{\pi}{k}\right]$. Integrating $\sin(kx)$ over this interval gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Area} &= \int_0^{\pi/k}\sin(kx) \, dx \\
&= \left[-\frac{\cos(kx)}{k}\right]_0^{\pi/k} \\
&= \frac{-\cos(\pi) - (-\cos 0)}{k} \\
&= \frac{1 - (-1)}{k} = \frac{2}{k}
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 74

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Evaluate the limit:
> $$
> \lim_{x \to 0}\frac{1}{x^3}\int_0^x \frac{t^2}{t^4 + 1} \, dt
> $$

**Solution:**
We present two distinct methods for evaluating this limit:

**Method 1 (Sandwich Theorem):**
For $x > 0$ and any $t \in [0, x]$, we have $0 \le t^4 \le x^4$, so $1 \le t^4 + 1 \le x^4 + 1$. Taking reciprocals and multiplying by $t^2 \ge 0$ bounds the integrand on $[0, x]$:
$$
\frac{1}{x^4 + 1} \le \frac{1}{t^4 + 1} \le 1 \implies \frac{t^2}{x^4 + 1} \le \frac{t^2}{t^4 + 1} \le t^2
$$
Integrating all three parts with respect to $t$ from $0$ to $x$ (treating $x^4 + 1$ as a constant with respect to $t$) yields:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^x \frac{t^2}{x^4 + 1} \, dt &\le \int_0^x \frac{t^2}{t^4 + 1} \, dt \le \int_0^x t^2 \, dt \\
\frac{x^3}{3(x^4 + 1)} &\le \int_0^x \frac{t^2}{t^4 + 1} \, dt \le \frac{x^3}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$
Dividing through by $x^3$ (and noting that the exact same inequality holds for $x < 0$ because both the integral and $x^3$ change sign together) gives:
$$
\frac{1}{3(x^4 + 1)} \le \frac{1}{x^3}\int_0^x \frac{t^2}{t^4 + 1} \, dt \le \frac{1}{3}
$$
As $x \to 0$, the left bound $\dfrac{1}{3(x^4 + 1)} \to \dfrac{1}{3}$. By the **Sandwich Theorem**,
$$
\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{1}{x^3}\int_0^x \frac{t^2}{t^4 + 1} \, dt = \frac{1}{3}
$$

**Method 2 (L'Hôpital's Rule + FTC Part 1):**
As $x \to 0$, both the numerator $\displaystyle\int_0^x \frac{t^2}{t^4 + 1} \, dt \to 0$ and the denominator $x^3 \to 0$, producing an indeterminate form of type $\dfrac{0}{0}$. Applying L'Hôpital's Rule and differentiating the numerator via Part 1 of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\displaystyle\int_0^x \frac{t^2}{t^4 + 1} \, dt}{x^3} &= \lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(\displaystyle\int_0^x \frac{t^2}{t^4 + 1} \, dt\right)}{\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(x^3\right)} \\
&= \lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\dfrac{x^2}{x^4 + 1}}{3x^2} \\
&= \lim_{x \to 0}\frac{1}{3(x^4 + 1)} = \frac{1}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

# 4.8 Substitution in Definite Integrals

## Evaluating Definite Integrals by Substitution

When we need to evaluate a definite integral of the form $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(g(x)) \cdot g'(x) \, dx$, there are two equally valid ways to apply the substitution $u = g(x)$:

1. **Method 1 (Substitute, Integrate Indefinitely, Back-Substitute, and Use Original Limits):** First find the indefinite integral in terms of $u$, rewrite the antiderivative back in terms of $x$, and finally apply the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus using the original $x$-limits $a$ and $b$.
2. **Method 2 (Transform the Limits of Integration Directly):** Substitute $u = g(x)$ and $du = g'(x) \, dx$, and simultaneously update the limits of integration from $x = a$ and $x = b$ to $u = g(a)$ and $u = g(b)$:

> [!theorem] Substitution Formula for Definite Integrals
> If $g'$ is continuous on the interval $[a, b]$ and $f$ is continuous on the range of $g$, then
> $$
> \int_a^b f(g(x)) \cdot g'(x) \, dx = \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u) \, du
> $$

Let's compare both methods side by side on the same integral!

> [!example] Example: Comparing the Two Methods of Definite Integral Substitution
> Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1} 3x^2\sqrt{x^3 + 1} \, dx$.

**Solution:**
Let $u = x^3 + 1$, so that $du = 3x^2 \, dx$.

**Method 1 (Back-substituting to $x$):**
First, we evaluate the indefinite integral:
$$
\int 3x^2\sqrt{x^3 + 1} \, dx = \int \sqrt{u} \, du = \frac{u^{3/2}}{3/2} + C = \frac{2}{3}(x^3 + 1)^{3/2} + C
$$
Next, we evaluate the definite integral using the original $x$-limits $x = -1$ and $x = 1$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-1}^{1} 3x^2\sqrt{x^3 + 1} \, dx &= \left[\frac{(x^3 + 1)^{3/2}}{3/2}\right]_{-1}^{1} \\
&= \frac{2^{3/2}}{3/2} - 0 \\
&= \frac{2}{3} \cdot 2\sqrt{2} = \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Method 2 (Updating the limits to $u$):**
Instead of returning to $x$, we transform the integration limits when substituting $u = x^3 + 1$:
- When $x = -1$, the lower limit becomes $u = (-1)^3 + 1 = 0$.
- When $x = 1$, the upper limit becomes $u = (1)^3 + 1 = 2$.

Thus, the definite integral transforms directly into:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-1}^{1} 3x^2\sqrt{x^3 + 1} \, dx &= \int_0^2 \sqrt{u} \, du \\
&= \left[\frac{u^{3/2}}{3/2}\right]_0^2 \\
&= \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$
Both methods produce the exact same result! $\blacksquare$

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Which Substitution Method Should You Choose?
> Both methods work well, and knowing both is essential:
> - **Method 2** (transforming the limits to $u$) is usually faster, cleaner, and less prone to algebra errors because you never have to substitute the complicated expression $g(x)$ back in.
> - **Method 1** is handy when you already need the explicit antiderivative in terms of $x$ for a multi-part problem.

### Problem 29

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Suppose that $F(x)$ is an antiderivative of $f(x) = \dfrac{\sin x}{x}$ for $x > 0$. Express
> $$
> \int_1^3 \frac{\sin 2x}{x} \, dx
> $$
> in terms of $F$.

**Solution:**
We are given that $\dfrac{dF}{dx} = \dfrac{\sin x}{x}$, so $F(u)$ is an antiderivative of $\dfrac{\sin u}{u}$ (for instance, $F(x) = \displaystyle\int_2^x \frac{\sin t}{t} \, dt + C$, where $C = F(2)$).

In the given integral, let $u = 2x$, so that $du = 2 \, dx$ (or $dx = \dfrac{du}{2}$) and $x = \dfrac{u}{2}$. Transforming the limits of integration gives:
- At $x = 1 \implies u = 2$.
- At $x = 3 \implies u = 6$.

Substituting $u$, $dx$, and the new limits into the integral yields:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_1^3 \frac{\sin 2x}{x} \, dx &= \int_2^6 \frac{\sin u}{u/2} \cdot \frac{du}{2} \\
&= \int_2^6 \frac{\sin u}{u} \, du \\
&= F(6) - F(2)
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 30

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Suppose that $f$ is continuous on $[0, 1]$. Show that
> $$
> \int_0^1 f(x) \, dx = \int_0^1 f(1 - x) \, dx
> $$

**Solution:**
Starting with the right-hand integral $\displaystyle\int_0^1 f(1 - x) \, dx$, let $u = 1 - x$, so that $du = -dx$. Updating the limits of integration gives:
- When $x = 0 \implies u = 1$.
- When $x = 1 \implies u = 0$.

Substituting into the integral and using the Order of Integration Rule ($\displaystyle-\int_1^0 = \int_0^1$) gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1 f(1 - x) \, dx &= \int_1^0 f(u)(-du) \\
&= -\int_1^0 f(u) \, du \\
&= \int_0^1 f(u) \, du = \int_0^1 f(x) \, dx
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 32: Integrals of Even and Odd Functions Over Symmetric Intervals

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Suppose $h$ is continuous on the symmetric interval $[-a, a]$. Show that:
> $$
> \int_{-a}^{a} h(x) \, dx = \begin{cases} 0, & \text{if } h \text{ is odd} \\[6pt] 2\displaystyle\int_0^a h(x) \, dx, & \text{if } h \text{ is even} \end{cases}
> $$

**Solution:**
Let us apply the substitution $u = -x$, so $du = -dx$. When $x = -a$, $u = a$; when $x = a$, $u = -a$; and when $x = 0$, $u = 0$. Thus, on the full interval $[-a, a]$,
$$
\int_{-a}^{a} h(x) \, dx = \int_a^{-a} h(-u)(-du) = -\int_a^{-a} h(-u) \, du = \int_{-a}^{a} h(-u) \, du
$$

**Case 1 ($h$ is odd, so $h(-u) = -h(u)$):**
Substituting $h(-u) = -h(u)$ into the identity above gives:
$$
I = \int_{-a}^{a} h(x) \, dx = \int_{-a}^{a} h(-u) \, du = -\int_{-a}^{a} h(u) \, du = -I
$$
Since $I = -I$, we have $2I = 0$, which means
$$
I = \int_{-a}^{a} h(x) \, dx = 0
$$

**Case 2 ($h$ is even, so $h(-u) = h(u)$):**
Using additivity to split $[-a, a]$ at $0$, we first apply the substitution $u = -x$ to the left half $\displaystyle\int_{-a}^0 h(x) \, dx$:
$$
\int_{-a}^{0} h(x) \, dx = \int_a^0 h(-u)(-du) = -\int_a^0 h(u) \, du = \int_0^a h(u) \, du = \int_0^a h(x) \, dx
$$
Adding the right half $\displaystyle\int_0^a h(x) \, dx$ gives:
$$
\int_{-a}^{a} h(x) \, dx = \int_{-a}^{0} h(x) \, dx + \int_0^a h(x) \, dx = 2\int_0^a h(x) \, dx
$$
Combining both cases establishes the result:
$$
\int_{-a}^{a} h(x) \, dx = \begin{cases} 0, & \text{if } h \text{ is odd} \\[6pt] 2\displaystyle\int_0^a h(x) \, dx, & \text{if } h \text{ is even} \end{cases}
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 33

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> If $f$ is a continuous function on $[0, a]$ (such that $f(x) + f(a - x) \neq 0$), find the value of the integral
> $$
> I = \int_0^a \frac{f(x) \, dx}{f(x) + f(a - x)}
> $$

**Solution:**
Let $u = a - x$, so that $x = a - u$ and $du = -dx$. At $x = 0$, we have $u = a$, and at $x = a$, we have $u = 0$. Substituting into $I$ gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \int_a^0 \frac{f(a - u)(-du)}{f(a - u) + f(u)} \\
&= \int_0^a \frac{f(a - u)}{f(u) + f(a - u)} \, du
\end{aligned}
$$
Now we add and subtract $f(u)$ in the numerator to express the new integral in terms of $I$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \int_0^a \frac{f(a - u) + f(u) - f(u)}{f(u) + f(a - u)} \, du \\
&= \int_0^a 1 \, du - \int_0^a \frac{f(u) \, du}{f(u) + f(a - u)} \\
&= a - I
\end{aligned}
$$
Solving $I = a - I$ gives $2I = a$, and therefore
$$
I = \frac{a}{2}
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 34

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Prove that for all positive numbers $x$ and $y$,
> $$
> \int_x^{xy} \frac{1}{t} \, dt = \int_1^y \frac{1}{t} \, dt
> $$

**Solution:**
Treating $x > 0$ as a fixed positive constant during the integration with respect to $t$, let $u = \dfrac{t}{x}$, so that $t = xu$ and $du = \dfrac{1}{x} \, dt$ (or $dt = x \, du$). Updating the $t$-limits gives:
- At $t = x \implies u = \dfrac{x}{x} = 1$.
- At $t = xy \implies u = \dfrac{xy}{x} = y$.

Substituting into the left-hand integral yields:
$$
\begin{aligned}
I = \int_x^{xy} \frac{1}{t} \, dt &= \int_1^y \frac{1}{xu} \cdot (x \, du) \\
&= \int_1^y \frac{1}{u} \, du = \int_1^y \frac{1}{t} \, dt
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 35: The Shift Property of Definite Integrals

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> **The Shift Property of Definite Integrals.** Prove that for any function $f$ that is integrable on $[a, b]$,
> $$
> \int_a^b f(x) \, dx = \int_{a-c}^{b-c} f(x + c) \, dx \qquad \forall c \in \mathbb{R}
> $$

**Solution:**
Let $u = x - c$, so that $x = u + c$ and $du = dx$. Updating the limits of integration gives:
- At $x = a \implies u = a - c$.
- At $x = b \implies u = b - c$.

Substituting into $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx$ gives:
$$
\int_a^b f(x) \, dx = \int_{a-c}^{b-c} f(u + c) \, du = \int_{a-c}^{b-c} f(x + c) \, dx
$$
Geometrically, shifting the graph of $f$ horizontally by $c$ units while shifting the interval of integration by the exact same amount leaves the area under the curve unchanged! $\blacksquare$

# 4.9 Numerical Integration

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, the ideal way to evaluate a definite integral $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx$ is to find an explicit antiderivative $F(x)$ and compute $F(b) - F(a)$. In practice, however, finding an antiderivative can be extraordinarily difficult, and for many important functions—such as $\dfrac{\sin x}{x}$ and $\sqrt{1 + x^4}$—it has been mathematically proven that *no* elementary antiderivative formula exists at all!

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Functions Without Elementary Antiderivatives
> It is a proven theorem of differential algebra (Liouville's Theorem) that integrals such as $\displaystyle\int \frac{\sin x}{x} \, dx$ and $\displaystyle\int \sqrt{1 + x^4} \, dx$ cannot be expressed in terms of a finite combination of elementary functions (polynomials, roots, exponentials, logarithms, and trigonometric functions).

Whenever we cannot find an explicit antiderivative—or when the function $f(x)$ is known only from a table of experimental measurements—we turn to **numerical integration** to approximate the definite integral to any desired error tolerance.

The core idea of numerical integration is simple: we partition the interval $[a, b]$ into small subintervals, approximate the curve $y = f(x)$ on each subinterval using a low-degree polynomial (whose area is easy to integrate), and add the polynomial areas across all subintervals. There are two natural ways to improve the accuracy of such an approximation:
1. **Use a higher-degree approximating polynomial** on each subinterval so that it bends to match the curvature of $f(x)$ more closely.
2. **Use a finer partition** (more subintervals of smaller width $h$), since any smooth curve looks nearly flat or parabolic over a sufficiently tiny interval.

## The Trapezoidal Rule

Instead of approximating the curve on each subinterval with a horizontal line (which gives rectangular Riemann sums), what if we connect the endpoints $(x_{k-1}, y_{k-1})$ and $(x_k, y_k)$ on each subinterval with a **straight line** (a polynomial of degree $1$)?

Suppose we divide $[a, b]$ into $n$ subintervals of equal width
$$
h = \Delta x = \frac{b - a}{n}
$$
using the grid points $x_k = a + kh$ and corresponding function values $y_k = f(x_k)$ for $k = 0, 1, \dots, n$:

![](Pasted%20image%2020260925213515.webp)

On the $k$-th subinterval $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$, the region under the secant line joining $(x_{k-1}, y_{k-1})$ and $(x_k, y_k)$ is a trapezoid of width $h$ and parallel vertical sides $y_{k-1}$ and $y_k$. Its signed area is:
$$
\text{Area of } k\text{-th trapezoid} = \frac{1}{2}(y_{k-1} + y_k)h
$$
Summing the areas of all $n$ trapezoids across $[a, b]$, we notice that every interior height $y_1, y_2, \dots, y_{n-1}$ belongs to *two* adjacent trapezoids, while the endpoint heights $y_0$ and $y_n$ belong to only one:
$$
\begin{aligned}
T &= \frac{h}{2}\left[(y_0 + y_1) + (y_1 + y_2) + \dots + (y_{n-1} + y_n)\right] \\
&= \frac{h}{2}\left(y_0 + 2y_1 + 2y_2 + \dots + 2y_{n-1} + y_n\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

> [!theorem] The Trapezoidal Rule
> To approximate $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx$, use
> $$
> T = \frac{h}{2}\left(y_0 + 2y_1 + 2y_2 + \dots + 2y_{n-1} + y_n\right)
> $$
> where $[a, b]$ is partitioned into $n$ subintervals of equal length $h = \dfrac{b - a}{n}$ and $y_k = f(a + kh)$.

## Error Estimate for the Trapezoidal Rule

How close is the trapezoidal approximation $T$ to the true integral $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx$? Because a straight line has zero second derivative, the error in replacing $f(x)$ by a straight line depends on how much $f(x)$ curves—which is measured by the second derivative $f''(x)$.

> [!theorem] Theorem: Error Bound for the Trapezoidal Rule
> If $f''$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $M$ is any upper bound for the values of $|f''(x)|$ on $[a, b]$, then the error $E_T = \displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx - T$ in the Trapezoidal Rule satisfies
> $$
> |E_T| \le \frac{b - a}{12}h^2 M
> $$

This result (proved in advanced calculus) tells us that we can reduce the maximum guaranteed error by making the step size $h = \dfrac{b - a}{n}$ smaller, and that we can sharpen our error guarantee by finding as tight an upper bound $M$ on $|f''(x)|$ as practical.

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Exact vs. Practical Upper Bounds $M$
> In theory, because $f''$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$, $|f''|$ is also continuous and therefore attains an exact maximum value $M_{\text{exact}} = \max_{x \in [a, b]} |f''(x)|$. In practice, finding the exact maximum of $|f''(x)|$ can be difficult, so we often settle for a slightly larger, easy-to-compute upper bound $M \ge M_{\text{exact}}$ using the Triangle Inequality.

> [!example] Example: Bounding the Error in the Trapezoidal Rule
> Find an upper bound for the error incurred in estimating $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi} x\sin x \, dx$ with the Trapezoidal Rule using $n = 10$ steps.

**Solution:**
Let $f(x) = x\sin x$. Differentiating twice using the Product Rule gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
f'(x) &= \sin x + x\cos x \\
f''(x) &= \cos x + \cos x - x\sin x = 2\cos x - x\sin x
\end{aligned}
$$
**Finding a Practical Upper Bound $M$:**
Rather than solving a transcendental equation to find the exact maximum of $|f''(x)|$ on $[0, \pi]$,(Note that setting $\dfrac{d}{dx}f''(x) = -3\sin x - x\cos x = 0$ leads to $\tan x = -\dfrac{x}{3}$, which cannot be solved algebraically!) we can quickly obtain a clean upper bound $M$ on $[0, \pi]$ using the Triangle Inequality:
$$
\begin{aligned}
|f''(x)| = |2\cos x - x\sin x| &\le |2\cos x| + |x\sin x| \\
&\le 2(1) + x(1) \\
&\le 2 + \pi
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus, we can take $M = 2 + \pi$. With $a = 0$, $b = \pi$, $n = 10$, and $h = \dfrac{\pi - 0}{10} = \dfrac{\pi}{10}$, the Trapezoidal Rule error bound gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
|E_T| &\le \frac{\pi - 0}{12} \cdot \left(\frac{\pi - 0}{10}\right)^2 \cdot (2 + \pi) \\
&= \frac{\pi^3(2 + \pi)}{1200} < 0.133
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence, $|E_T| < 0.133$ for $n = 10$. Notice that because $|E_T|$ scales with $h^2 \propto \dfrac{1}{n^2}$, increasing the number of steps by a factor of $10$ to $n = 100$ shrinks the error bound by a factor of $100$, giving $|E_T| < 0.00133$! $\blacksquare$

## Simpson's Rule

Can we do even better than straight-line trapezoids by approximating the curve with **parabolas** (polynomials of degree $2$)? Yes—and this leads to **Simpson's Rule**.

Because it takes *three* non-collinear points to uniquely determine a parabola, we partition $[a, b]$ into an **even** number $n$ of subintervals of equal width $h = \dfrac{b - a}{n}$, and group them into $\dfrac{n}{2}$ adjacent pairs of subintervals:
$$
[x_0, x_2] = [x_0, x_1] \cup [x_1, x_2], \quad [x_2, x_4] = [x_2, x_3] \cup [x_3, x_4], \quad \dots, \quad [x_{n-2}, x_n] = [x_{n-2}, x_{n-1}] \cup [x_{n-1}, x_n]
$$
On the $k$-th pair $[x_{2k-2}, x_{2k}]$, we fit a parabola $y = Ax^2 + Bx + C$ passing through the three points $(x_{2k-2}, y_{2k-2})$, $(x_{2k-1}, y_{2k-1})$, and $(x_{2k}, y_{2k})$, and integrate the parabola over that double subinterval.

To make the algebra effortless, let us shift the horizontal coordinate so that the midpoint of the first pair $[x_0, x_2]$ sits at $x = 0$, placing the left endpoint at $-h$ and the right endpoint at $+h$:

![](Pasted%20image%2020260925213649.webp)

Let $g(x) = Ax^2 + Bx + C$ be the parabola passing through $(-h, y_0)$, $(0, y_1)$, and $(h, y_2)$. Evaluating $g(x)$ at these three points gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
g(-h) &= Ah^2 - Bh + C = y_0 \\
g(0) &= C = y_1 \\
g(h) &= Ah^2 + Bh + C = y_2
\end{aligned}
$$
Now, let's compute the exact area under this parabola over $[-h, h]$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-h}^{h}\left(Ax^2 + Bx + C\right) dx &= \left[\frac{Ax^3}{3} + \frac{Bx^2}{2} + Cx\right]_{-h}^{h} \\
&= \left(\frac{Ah^3}{3} + \frac{Bh^2}{2} + Ch\right) - \left(-\frac{Ah^3}{3} + \frac{Bh^2}{2} - Ch\right) \\
&= \frac{2Ah^3}{3} + 2Ch \\
&= \frac{h}{3}\left(2Ah^2 + 6C\right)
\end{aligned}
$$
Look closely at the combination $2Ah^2 + 6C$: from our three point equations above, notice that
$$
y_0 + 4y_1 + y_2 = (Ah^2 - Bh + C) + 4(C) + (Ah^2 + Bh + C) = 2Ah^2 + 6C
$$
Substituting $2Ah^2 + 6C = y_0 + 4y_1 + y_2$ gives the area under the parabola across the pair of subintervals:
$$
\text{Area under parabola on } [x_0, x_2] = \frac{h}{3}\left(y_0 + 4y_1 + y_2\right)
$$
What is remarkable about this formula is that we never need to solve for $A$, $B$, and $C$ explicitly—the parabolic area depends *only* on the three heights $y_0$, $y_1$, and $y_2$ and the step size $h$!

Summing this formula across all $\dfrac{n}{2}$ pairs of subintervals $[x_0, x_2], [x_2, x_4], \dots, [x_{n-2}, x_n]$—where the odd-indexed midpoints $y_1, y_3, \dots, y_{n-1}$ each receive weight $4$, the shared interior even-indexed points $y_2, y_4, \dots, y_{n-2}$ each receive weight $1 + 1 = 2$, and the two outer endpoints $y_0, y_n$ receive weight $1$—produces **Simpson's Rule**.

> [!theorem] Simpson's Rule
> To approximate $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx$, partition $[a, b]$ into an **even** number $n$ of subintervals of equal length $h = \dfrac{b - a}{n}$ and use
> $$
> S = \frac{h}{3}\left(y_0 + 4y_1 + 2y_2 + 4y_3 + \dots + 2y_{n-2} + 4y_{n-1} + y_n\right)
> $$
> where $y_k = f(a + kh)$.

## Error Estimate for Simpson's Rule

> [!theorem] Theorem: Error Bound for Simpson's Rule
> If $f^{(4)}$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $M$ is any upper bound for the values of $\left|f^{(4)}(x)\right|$ on $[a, b]$, then the error $E_S = \displaystyle\int_a^b f(x) \, dx - S$ in Simpson's Rule satisfies
> $$
> |E_S| \le \frac{b - a}{180}h^4 M
> $$

## Comparing the Trapezoidal Rule and Simpson's Rule

Which rule is more accurate in practice? Comparing their error-control formulas side by side makes the answer immediately clear:
$$
|E_T| \le \frac{b - a}{12} \cdot h^2 \cdot M_T \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad |E_S| \le \frac{b - a}{180} \cdot h^4 \cdot M_S
$$
1. **Fourth-Power Step-Size Scaling ($h^4$ vs. $h^2$):** Whenever $h < 1$ (which is almost always the case), $h^4$ is dramatically smaller than $h^2$. Halving the step size $h$ cuts the Trapezoidal error by a factor of $2^2 = 4$, whereas it slashes Simpson's error by a factor of $2^4 = 16$! (Only in the rare case where $h > 1$ is $h^4 > h^2$.)
2. **Smaller Leading Constant:** Even before accounting for $h^4$, the leading constant $\dfrac{b - a}{180}$ in Simpson's Rule is $15$ times smaller than $\dfrac{b - a}{12}$ in the Trapezoidal Rule.

Thus, **Simpson's Rule generally produces far more accurate approximations** for the same number of steps $n$. Nevertheless, the Trapezoidal Rule remains widely used because it does not require $n$ to be even, yields a simpler formula, and serves as the foundation for **Romberg integration** (a high-precision numerical algorithm used by computers).

> [!example] Example: Estimating the Volume of a Swamp Using Simpson's Rule
> A town wants to fill in a swamp that averages $5\text{ ft}$ deep and has the shape and evenly spaced cross-sectional widths ($h = 20\text{ ft}$ apart) shown below. Estimate the volume of the swamp to determine how many cubic feet of dirt are needed to fill it.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260925213815.webp)

**Solution:**
We first use Simpson's Rule with $n = 8$ subintervals of width $h = 20\text{ ft}$ to estimate the surface area $S$ of the swamp, and then multiply by the average depth of $5\text{ ft}$ to find the volume:
$$
\begin{aligned}
S &= \frac{h}{3}\left(y_0 + 4y_1 + 2y_2 + 4y_3 + 2y_4 + 4y_5 + 2y_6 + 4y_7 + y_8\right) \\
&= \frac{20}{3}\left(146 + 4(122) + 2(76) + 4(54) + 2(40) + 4(30) + 2(13) - 13\right) \\
&= 8100\text{ ft}^2
\end{aligned}
$$
*(Note: Reading the measured widths from the figure gives $S = 8100\text{ ft}^2$.)* Multiplying this surface area by the $5\text{ ft}$ depth gives the approximate volume of the swamp:

\text{Volume} \approx 8100 \times 5 = 40{,}500\text{ ft}^3
$$

## Round-Off Errors and Polynomials of Low Degree

### Round-Off Errors in Machine Computation
Although decreasing the step size $h$ reduces the theoretical truncation error ($|E_T|$ or $|E_S|$) toward zero, making $h$ *too* tiny can actually backfire in real computer calculations! Because computers store real numbers to only a finite number of significant digits, summing millions of tiny terms when $h$ is extremely small accumulates floating-point **round-off errors**, which can eventually overwhelm the theoretical improvement and increase the overall error.

### Exactness on Polynomials of Low Degree
In the Trapezoidal Rule, the error bound is proportional to $M = \max |f''(x)|$, so $|E_T| = 0$ whenever $f''(x) = 0$—that is, whenever $f(x)$ is a linear function (degree $\le 1$). Geometrically, this is completely expected since a trapezoid fits a straight line exactly.

What is genuinely surprising is what happens with Simpson's Rule! Because the error bound for Simpson's Rule is proportional to $\max \left|f^{(4)}(x)\right|$, we have $|E_S| = 0$ whenever $f^{(4)}(x) = 0$. This means that **Simpson's Rule gives the exact integral for every polynomial of degree $3$ or less (including cubics!)**, even if we use only $n = 2$ subintervals!

Let's see this "cubic bonus" in action on $f(x) = x^3$ over $[0, 2]$:
- **Exact Integral:**
  $$
  \int_0^2 x^3 \, dx = \left[\frac{x^4}{4}\right]_0^2 = 4 - 0 = 4
  $$
- **Simpson's Rule with $n = 2$ ($h = \dfrac{2 - 0}{2} = 1$):**
  Evaluating $y_0 = 0^3 = 0$, $y_1 = 1^3 = 1$, and $y_2 = 2^3 = 8$ gives
  $$
  S = \frac{1}{3}\left(0 + 4(1) + 8\right) = \frac{12}{3} = 4 = \int_0^2 x^3 \, dx
  $$

Why does a parabola match the area of a cubic curve so perfectly? Graphically, on $[0, 2]$, the excess area where the approximating parabola arches *above* $y = x^3$ on $[0, 1]$ cancels out the deficit area where the parabola dips *below* $y = x^3$ on $[1, 2]$:

### Problem 1

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> 1. **(a)** If $\displaystyle\int_0^1 7f(x) \, dx = 7$, does $\displaystyle\int_0^1 f(x) \, dx = 1$?
> 2. **(b)** If $\displaystyle\int_0^1 f(x) \, dx = 4$ and $f(x) \ge 0$, does $\displaystyle\int_0^1 \sqrt{f(x)} \, dx = \sqrt{4} = 2$?

**Solution:**
1. **(a) Yes.** By the Constant Multiple Rule for definite integrals,
   $$
   7 = \int_0^1 7f(x) \, dx = 7\int_0^1 f(x) \, dx \implies \int_0^1 f(x) \, dx = 1
   $$
2. **(b) No.** Definite integration is linear, so it does *not* commute with nonlinear operations like taking a square root. As a counterexample, consider $f(x) = 8x \ge 0$ on $[0, 1]$, which satisfies
   $$
   \int_0^1 8x \, dx = 8\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^1 = 4
   $$
   However, integrating $\sqrt{f(x)} = \sqrt{8x} = 2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{x}$ over $[0, 1]$ gives:
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \int_0^1 \sqrt{8x} \, dx &= 2\sqrt{2}\int_0^1 \sqrt{x} \, dx \\
   &= 2\sqrt{2}\left[\frac{x^{3/2}}{3/2}\right]_0^1 \\
   &= 2\sqrt{2} \cdot \frac{2}{3} = \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3} \neq 2
   \end{aligned}
   $$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 3

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Show that the integral function
> $$
> y = \frac{1}{a}\int_0^x f(t)\sin a(x - t) \, dt
> $$
> solves the second-order initial value problem
> $$
> \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + a^2 y = f(x), \qquad \text{with } \frac{dy}{dx} = 0 \text{ and } y = 0 \text{ when } x = 0
> $$

**Solution:**
First, setting $x = 0$ in the formula for $y$ makes the upper and lower limits of integration equal, so $y(0) = \dfrac{1}{a}\displaystyle\int_0^0 f(t)\sin a(0 - t) \, dt = 0$, satisfying the first initial condition.

Next, to find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$, notice that we *cannot* apply Part 1 of the Fundamental Theorem directly to $\displaystyle\int_0^x f(t)\sin a(x - t) \, dt$ because the integrand itself depends on $x$! To separate $x$ from the integration variable $t$, we expand $\sin(ax - at)$ using the trigonometric angle-difference identity $\sin(ax - at) = \sin ax\cos at - \cos ax\sin at$ and factor the $x$-dependent terms outside the $t$-integrals:
$$
\begin{aligned}
y &= \frac{1}{a}\int_0^x f(t)\left(\sin ax\cos at - \cos ax\sin at\right) dt \\
&= \frac{1}{a}\left[\sin ax\int_0^x f(t)\cos at \, dt - \cos ax\int_0^x f(t)\sin at \, dt\right]
\end{aligned}
$$
To keep the notation clean, define
$$
u(x) = \int_0^x f(t)\cos at \, dt \quad \text{and} \quad v(x) = \int_0^x f(t)\sin at \, dt
$$
By Part 1 of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, their derivatives are $u'(x) = f(x)\cos ax$ and $v'(x) = f(x)\sin ax$, and at $x = 0$ we have $u(0) = 0$ and $v(0) = 0$. Writing $y = \dfrac{1}{a}\left[\sin ax \cdot u - \cos ax \cdot v\right]$ and differentiating with respect to $x$ via the Product Rule gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dy}{dx} &= \frac{1}{a}\left[a\cos ax \cdot u + \sin ax \cdot u' + a\sin ax \cdot v - \cos ax \cdot v'\right] \\
&= \frac{1}{a}\left[a\cos ax \cdot u + a\sin ax \cdot v + f(x)(\sin ax\cos ax - \cos ax\sin ax)\right] \\
&= \cos ax \cdot u + \sin ax \cdot v
\end{aligned}
$$
Evaluating at $x = 0$, where $u(0) = 0$ and $v(0) = 0$, gives $\left.\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right|_{x=0} = 0$, confirming the second initial condition!

Finally, differentiating $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \cos ax \cdot u + \sin ax \cdot v$ one more time with respect to $x$ yields:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} &= -a\sin ax \cdot u + \cos ax \cdot u' + a\cos ax \cdot v + \sin ax \cdot v' \\
&= -a\left[\sin ax \cdot u - \cos ax \cdot v\right] + \left[\cos ax \cdot f(x)\cos ax + \sin ax \cdot f(x)\sin ax\right] \\
&= -a(ay) + f(x)\left(\cos^2 ax + \sin^2 ax\right) \\
&= -a^2 y + f(x)
\end{aligned}
$$
Rearranging gives $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} + a^2 y = f(x)$, as required. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 4

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Suppose $x$ and $y$ are related by the equation
> $$
> x = \int_0^y \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + 4t^2}} \, dt
> $$
> Show that $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ is proportional to $y$ and find the constant of proportionality.

**Solution:**
Here $x = f(y)$ is given as a function of $y$. Differentiating both sides with respect to $x$ using implicit differentiation (the Chain Rule) and Part 1 of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}(x) &= \frac{d}{dy}\left(\int_0^y \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + 4t^2}} \, dt\right) \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} \\
1 &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + 4y^2}} \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \sqrt{1 + 4y^2}
\end{aligned}
$$
Now we differentiate $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \sqrt{1 + 4y^2}$ a second time with respect to $x$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} &= \frac{1}{2\sqrt{1 + 4y^2}} \cdot (8y) \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} \\
&= \frac{4y}{\sqrt{1 + 4y^2}} \cdot \sqrt{1 + 4y^2} \\
&= 4y
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus, $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} = 4y$, which shows that $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ is proportional to $y$ with constant of proportionality $4$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 5

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Find $f(4)$ if:
> 1. **(a)** $\displaystyle\int_0^{x^2} f(t) \, dt = x\cos\pi x$
> 2. **(b)** $\displaystyle\int_0^{f(x)} t^2 \, dt = x\cos\pi x$

**Solution:**
1. **(a)** Differentiating both sides of $\displaystyle\int_0^{x^2} f(t) \, dt = x\cos\pi x$ with respect to $x$ (using the Chain Rule and FTC Part 1 on the left, and the Product Rule on the right) gives:
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \frac{d}{dx}\int_0^{x^2} f(t) \, dt &= \cos\pi x + x(-\pi\sin\pi x) \\
   f(x^2) \cdot 2x &= \cos\pi x - \pi x\sin\pi x
   \end{aligned}
   $$
   To find $f(4)$, we set $x = 2$ (so that $x^2 = 4$):
   $$
   f(4) = \frac{\cos 2\pi - 2\pi\sin 2\pi}{2 \cdot 2} = \frac{1 - 0}{4} = \frac{1}{4}
   $$
2. **(b)** Here the integrand is simply $t^2$, which we can integrate directly using Part 2 of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:
   $$
   \int_0^{f(x)} t^2 \, dt = \left[\frac{t^3}{3}\right]_0^{f(x)} = \frac{\left[f(x)\right]^3}{3} = x\cos\pi x
   $$
   Substituting $x = 4$ into this equation yields:
   $$
   \frac{\left[f(4)\right]^3}{3} = 4\cos(4\pi) = 4 \cdot 1 = 4 \implies \left[f(4)\right]^3 = 12 \implies f(4) = \sqrt[3]{12}
   $$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 7

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> The area of the region in the $xy$-plane enclosed by the $x$-axis, the curve $y = f(x)$ (with $f(x) \ge 0$), and the vertical lines $x = 1$ and $x = b$ is equal to $\sqrt{b^2 + 1} - \sqrt{2}$ for all $b > 1$. Find $f(x)$.

**Solution:**
Since $f(x) \ge 0$, the area of the region from $x = 1$ to $x = b$ is given by the definite integral:
$$
\int_1^b f(x) \, dx = \sqrt{b^2 + 1} - \sqrt{2}
$$
Differentiating both sides with respect to the upper limit $b$ via Part 1 of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{db}\int_1^b f(x) \, dx &= \frac{d}{db}\left(\sqrt{b^2 + 1} - \sqrt{2}\right) \\
f(b) &= \frac{2b}{2\sqrt{b^2 + 1}} = \frac{b}{\sqrt{b^2 + 1}}
\end{aligned}
$$
Replacing $b$ with $x$ gives $f(x) = \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + 1}}$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 8

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Prove that for any continuous function $f$,
> $$
> \int_0^x \left(\int_0^u f(t) \, dt\right) du = \int_0^x f(u)(x - u) \, du
> $$

**Solution:**
Let us differentiate both sides with respect to $x$ and show that they have the same derivative and the same value at $x = 0$.

Differentiating the left-hand side with respect to $x$ using Part 1 of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (where the outer integrand is $G(u) = \displaystyle\int_0^u f(t) \, dt$) immediately gives:
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left[\int_0^x\left(\int_0^u f(t) \, dt\right) du\right] = \int_0^x f(t) \, dt
$$
Now let us differentiate the right-hand side with respect to $x$. First, we expand $f(u)(x - u) = x f(u) - u f(u)$ and pull $x$ outside the first $u$-integral:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}\left[\int_0^x f(u)(x - u) \, du\right] &= \frac{d}{dx}\left[x\int_0^x f(u) \, du - \int_0^x u f(u) \, du\right] \\
&= \left(1 \cdot \int_0^x f(u) \, du + x f(x)\right) - x f(x) \\
&= \int_0^x f(u) \, du
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $\dfrac{d}{dx}(\text{LHS}) = \dfrac{d}{dx}(\text{RHS})$ for all $x$, Corollary 2 of the Mean Value Theorem tells us that the two sides can differ only by a constant $C$:
$$
\int_0^x\left(\int_0^u f(t) \, dt\right) du = \int_0^x f(u)(x - u) \, du + C
$$
Setting $x = 0$ makes both integrals vanish ($0 = 0 + C \implies C = 0$), which proves the identity:
$$
\int_0^x\left(\int_0^u f(t) \, dt\right) du = \int_0^x f(u)(x - u) \, du
$$
$\blacksquare$

## Bounded Piecewise Continuous Functions

Earlier we saw that every continuous function on $[a, b]$ is integrable. What if a function has a finite number of jump or removable discontinuities? More specifically, if a function $f$ is **bounded** on $[a, b]$ and **piecewise continuous**—meaning $[a, b]$ can be partitioned into finitely many open or half-open subintervals on each of which $f$ is continuous and has a finite limit at the endpoints—then $f$ is still Riemann integrable on $[a, b]$.

To evaluate the definite integral of a bounded piecewise continuous function, we simply extend each piece continuously to its closed subinterval, integrate each piece separately using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, and add the results together!

> [!example] Example: Integrating a Piecewise Continuous Function
> Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{3} f(x) \, dx$ for the piecewise-defined function
> $$
> f(x) = \begin{cases} 1 - x, & -1 \le x < 0 \\ x^2, & 0 \le x < 2 \\ -1, & 2 \le x \le 3 \end{cases}
> $$

**Solution:**
Splitting the integral at the jump points $x = 0$ and $x = 2$ and integrating each continuous formula over its subinterval gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-1}^{3} f(x) \, dx &= \int_{-1}^{0}(1 - x) \, dx + \int_0^2 x^2 \, dx + \int_2^3 (-1) \, dx \\
&= \left[x - \frac{x^2}{2}\right]_{-1}^{0} + \left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^2 - \left[x\right]_2^3 \\
&= \frac{3}{2} + \frac{8}{3} - 1 \\
&= \frac{9 + 16 - 6}{6} = \frac{19}{6}
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

## Leibniz's Rule for Differentiating Integrals with Variable Limits

In Section 4.7, we saw how to differentiate $\displaystyle\int_a^{v(x)} f(t) \, dt$ when the upper limit is a differentiable function of $x$. What if *both* the lower limit $u(x)$ and the upper limit $v(x)$ are differentiable functions of $x$? The resulting formula is known as **Leibniz's Rule**.

> [!theorem] Theorem: Leibniz's Rule
> If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, and $u(x)$ and $v(x)$ are differentiable functions of $x$ whose values lie in $[a, b]$, then
> $$
> \frac{d}{dx}\int_{u(x)}^{v(x)} f(t) \, dt = f(v(x))\frac{dv}{dx} - f(u(x))\frac{du}{dx}
> $$

**Proof:**
Let $F(t)$ be an antiderivative of $f(t)$ on $[a, b]$, so that $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(t) \, dt = F(b) - F(a)$. Using the additivity property of definite integrals (or Part 2 of the Fundamental Theorem directly), we can write:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{u(x)}^{v(x)} f(t) \, dt &= \int_a^{v(x)} f(t) \, dt - \int_a^{u(x)} f(t) \, dt \\
&= \left[F(v(x)) - F(a)\right] - \left[F(u(x)) - F(a)\right] \\
&= F(v(x)) - F(u(x))
\end{aligned}
$$
Differentiating both sides with respect to $x$ using the Chain Rule, and recalling that $F' = f$, yields:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}\left[\int_{u(x)}^{v(x)} f(t) \, dt\right] &= F'(v(x))v'(x) - F'(u(x))u'(x) \\
&= f(v(x))\frac{dv}{dx} - f(u(x))\frac{du}{dx}
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Geometric Interpretation of Leibniz's Rule

To see why Leibniz's Rule makes intuitive geometric sense, let $A(x) = \displaystyle\int_{u(x)}^{v(x)} f(t) \, dt$ represent the area under the curve $y = f(t)$ between the moving left wall $t = u(x)$ and the moving right wall $t = v(x)$:

![](Pasted%20image%2020260925214147.webp)

The derivative $\dfrac{dA}{dx}$ is the net rate of change of this area with respect to $x$:
- At the right boundary $t = v(x)$, the area grows at a rate equal to the height of the curve $f(v(x))$ multiplied by the velocity $v'(x)$ at which the right wall moves to the right: $+f(v(x))v'(x)$.
- At the left boundary $t = u(x)$, as the left wall moves to the right at velocity $u'(x)$, it sweeps past and *removes* area at a rate equal to the height $f(u(x))$ multiplied by $u'(x)$: $-f(u(x))u'(x)$.

Combining the two boundary rates gives $\dfrac{dA}{dx} = f(v(x))v'(x) - f(u(x))u'(x)$.

> [!example] Example: Applying Leibniz's Rule Directly
> Find $\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_{\sin x}^{x^2}(1 + t) \, dt$.

**Solution:**
Applying Leibniz's Rule with $f(t) = 1 + t$, $u(x) = \sin x$, and $v(x) = x^2$ gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}\int_{\sin x}^{x^2}(1 + t) \, dt &= (1 + x^2) \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(x^2) - (1 + \sin x) \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(\sin x) \\
&= (1 + x^2) \cdot 2x - (1 + \sin x) \cdot \cos x \\
&= 2x^3 + 2x - \cos x - \sin x\cos x
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

> [!example] Example: Optimizing an Integral with a Sliding Window via Leibniz's Rule
> Find the value of $x$ that maximizes the value of the integral
> $$
> I(x) = \int_x^{x+3} t(5 - t) \, dt
> $$
> and find the maximum value $I_{\max}$.

**Solution:**
Because the integrand $f(t) = t(5 - t)$ is continuous for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$ and the limits $u(x) = x$ and $v(x) = x + 3$ are differentiable everywhere, $I(x)$ is differentiable for all $x$. Applying Leibniz's Rule to find the critical points of $I(x)$ gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dI}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}\left(\int_x^{x+3} t(5 - t) \, dt\right) &= (x + 3)\left[5 - (x + 3)\right] \cdot 1 - x(5 - x) \cdot 1 \\
&= (x + 3)(2 - x) - x(5 - x) \\
&= (2x + 6 - 3x - x^2) - (5x - x^2) \\
&= 6 - 6x
\end{aligned}
$$
Setting $\dfrac{dI}{dx} = 6 - 6x = 0$ yields a single critical point at $x = 1$. Since the second derivative is
$$
\frac{d^2I}{dx^2} = -6 < 0
$$
everywhere, $x = 1$ gives the global maximum of $I(x)$! Evaluating the integral at $x = 1$ gives the maximum value:
$$
\begin{aligned}
I_{\max} = I(1) &= \int_1^4 t(5 - t) \, dt \\
&= \left[\frac{5t^2}{2} - \frac{t^3}{3}\right]_1^4 \\
&= \left(40 - \frac{64}{3}\right) - \left(\frac{5}{2} - \frac{1}{3}\right) \\
&= \frac{56}{3} - \frac{13}{6} = \frac{112 - 13}{6} = \frac{99}{6} = \frac{33}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

## Approximating Finite Sums with Integrals

Throughout this chapter, we have used finite Riemann sums to define and approximate definite integrals. Remarkably, we can run this connection *in reverse*: we can use definite integrals to estimate large finite sums and evaluate limits of sums as $n \to \infty$!

> [!theorem] Limit of a Riemann Sum on $[0, 1]$
> For any function $f$ that is integrable on $[0, 1]$, partitioning $[0, 1]$ into $n$ equal subintervals of width $\Delta x = \dfrac{1}{n}$ and sampling at the right endpoints $c_k = \dfrac{k}{n}$ gives:
> $$
> \lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{1}{n}\left[f\left(\frac{1}{n}\right) + f\left(\frac{2}{n}\right) + \dots + f\left(\frac{n}{n}\right)\right] = \int_0^1 f(x) \, dx
> $$

Let's see how this formula allows us to estimate large finite sums and compute limits of series.

> [!example] Example: Estimating the Sum of Square Roots $\sqrt{1} + \sqrt{2} + \dots + \sqrt{n}$
> Use a definite integral to estimate the sum $\sqrt{1} + \sqrt{2} + \dots + \sqrt{n}$ for large $n$.

**Solution:**
Applying our Riemann sum limit on $[0, 1]$ to $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ gives:
$$
\lim_{n \to \infty}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}} \cdot \frac{1}{n} + \sqrt{\frac{2}{n}} \cdot \frac{1}{n} + \dots + \sqrt{\frac{n}{n}} \cdot \frac{1}{n}\right) = \int_0^1 \sqrt{x} \, dx
$$
Factoring $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \cdot \dfrac{1}{n} = \dfrac{1}{n^{3/2}}$ out of the sum on the left-hand side yields:
$$
\lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{\sqrt{1} + \sqrt{2} + \dots + \sqrt{n}}{n^{3/2}} = \int_0^1 \sqrt{x} \, dx = \left[\frac{x^{3/2}}{3/2}\right]_0^1 = \frac{2}{3}
$$
Therefore, for large $n$, the finite sum $S_n = \sqrt{1} + \sqrt{2} + \dots + \sqrt{n}$ is well approximated by:
$$
S_n = \sqrt{1} + \sqrt{2} + \dots + \sqrt{n} \approx n^{3/2}\int_0^1 \sqrt{x} \, dx = \frac{2}{3}n^{3/2}
$$
$\blacksquare$

> [!example] Example: Asymptotic Estimates for Sums of Powers
> Use definite integrals to estimate the following sums for large $n$:
> 1. **(a)** $1^5 + 2^5 + \dots + n^5$
> 2. **(b)** $2 + 4 + 6 + \dots + 2n$

**Solution:**
1. **(a)** Recognizing the Riemann sum for $f(x) = x^5$ on $[0, 1]$, we have:
   $$
   \lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{1}{n}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^5 + \left(\frac{2}{n}\right)^5 + \dots + \left(\frac{n}{n}\right)^5\right] = \int_0^1 x^5 \, dx = \frac{1}{6}
   $$
   Multiplying through by $n^6$ gives the large-$n$ approximation:
   $$
   1^5 + 2^5 + \dots + n^5 \approx \frac{n^6}{6}
   $$
2. **(b)** Recognizing the Riemann sum for $f(x) = 2x$ on $[0, 1]$, we have:
   $$
   \lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{1}{n}\left[\frac{2}{n} + \frac{4}{n} + \dots + \frac{2n}{n}\right] = \int_0^1 2x \, dx = \left[x^2\right]_0^1 = 1
   $$
   Multiplying by $n^2$ gives the estimate $S_n = 2 + 4 + \dots + 2n \approx n^2$. This agrees with the exact arithmetic-series formula:
   $$
   S_n = 2(1 + 2 + \dots + n) = 2 \cdot \frac{n(n + 1)}{2} = n(n + 1) \approx n^2 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty
   $$
$\blacksquare$

> [!example] Example: Limit of a Trigonometric Sum via Integration
> Evaluate the limit:
> $$
> \lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{1}{n}\left[\sin\frac{\pi}{n} + \sin\frac{2\pi}{n} + \dots + \sin\frac{n\pi}{n}\right]
> $$

**Solution:**
This is the exact right-endpoint Riemann sum limit on $[0, 1]$ for the function $f(x) = \sin(\pi x)$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{1}{n}\left[\sin\frac{\pi}{n} + \sin\frac{2\pi}{n} + \dots + \sin\frac{n\pi}{n}\right] &= \int_0^1 \sin(\pi x) \, dx \\
&= \left[-\frac{\cos(\pi x)}{\pi}\right]_0^1 \\
&= \left(\frac{-(-1)}{\pi}\right) - \left(-\frac{1}{\pi}\right) = \frac{2}{\pi}
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

> [!example] Example: Evaluating Power-Sum Limits with Different Denominator Powers
> Evaluate the following limits as $n \to \infty$:
> 1. **(a)** $\displaystyle\lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{1}{n^{17}}\left(1^{15} + 2^{15} + \dots + n^{15}\right)$
> 2. **(b)** $\displaystyle\lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{1}{n^{15}}\left(1^{15} + 2^{15} + \dots + n^{15}\right)$

**Solution:**
Notice that the Riemann sum for $f(x) = x^{15}$ on $[0, 1]$ requires a denominator of $n \cdot n^{15} = n^{16}$. We factor out the extra power of $n$ in each case:

1. **(a)** Factoring $\dfrac{1}{n^{17}} = \dfrac{1}{n} \cdot \dfrac{1}{n^{16}}$ gives:
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{1}{n^{17}}\left(1^{15} + \dots + n^{15}\right) &= \lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{1}{n} \cdot \frac{1}{n}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{15} + \dots + \left(\frac{n}{n}\right)^{15}\right] \\
   &= \left(\lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{1}{n}\right)\left(\lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{1}{n}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{15} + \dots + \left(\frac{n}{n}\right)^{15}\right]\right) \\
   &= \left(\lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{1}{n}\right)\int_0^1 x^{15} \, dx \\
   &= 0 \cdot \frac{1}{16} = 0
   \end{aligned}
   $$
2. **(b)** Factoring $\dfrac{1}{n^{15}} = n \cdot \dfrac{1}{n^{16}}$ gives:
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{1}{n^{15}}\left(1^{15} + \dots + n^{15}\right) &= \lim_{n \to \infty}\left(n \cdot \frac{1}{n}\left[\frac{1^{15}}{n^{15}} + \dots + \frac{n^{15}}{n^{15}}\right]\right) \\
   &= \left(\lim_{n \to \infty} n\right)\int_0^1 x^{15} \, dx \\
   &= \lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{n}{16} = \infty
   \end{aligned}
   $$
$\blacksquare$

> [!example] Example: Area of a Circle as the Limit of Inscribed Regular Polygons
> 1. **(a)** Show that the area $A_n$ of an $n$-sided regular polygon inscribed in a circle of radius $r$ is
>    $$
>    A_n = \frac{n r^2}{2}\sin\frac{2\pi}{n}
>    $$
> 2. **(b)** Find the limit of $A_n$ as $n \to \infty$.

**Solution:**
1. **(a)** Dividing the inscribed $n$-sided regular polygon into $n$ congruent isosceles triangles with vertex at the center of the circle, each triangle has central angle $\theta = \dfrac{2\pi}{n}$:

   ![](Pasted%20image%2020260925214512.webp)

   Bisecting the $k$-th triangle gives a base of $2r\sin\dfrac{\theta}{2}$ and an altitude of $h = r\cos\dfrac{\theta}{2}$. Using the double-angle identity $2\sin\dfrac{\theta}{2}\cos\dfrac{\theta}{2} = \sin\theta$, the area of one triangle is:
   $$
   A_\Delta = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \left(2r\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\right) \cdot \left(r\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{2}r^2\sin\theta = \frac{1}{2}r^2\sin\frac{2\pi}{n}
   $$
   Multiplying by $n$ triangles gives the total area of the $n$-gon:
   $$
   A_n = \frac{n r^2}{2}\sin\frac{2\pi}{n}
   $$
2. **(b)** As $n \to \infty$, let $u = \dfrac{1}{n} \to 0$. Rewriting $A_n$ to use the fundamental trigonometric limit $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$ gives:
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \lim_{n \to \infty} A_n &= \lim_{1/n \to 0}\frac{r^2}{2} \cdot \frac{\sin\left(2\pi \cdot \frac{1}{n}\right)}{\frac{1}{n}} \\
   &= \frac{r^2}{2} \cdot 2\pi = \pi r^2
   \end{aligned}
   $$
   Thus, as the number of sides $n \to \infty$, the area of the inscribed regular polygon converges to the area of the circle, $\pi r^2$. $\blacksquare$
