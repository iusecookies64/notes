# 3.1 Extreme Values of Functions

### The Max-Min Theorem for Continuous Functions

> [!abstract] Theorem 1
> If $f$ is continuous at every point of a closed interval $I$, then $f$ assumes an absolute maximum value $M$ and an absolute minimum value $m$ somewhere in $I$. That is, there are numbers $x_1$ and $x_2$ in $I$ with $f(x_1)=m$, $f(x_2)=M$ and $$m \le f(x) \le M$$ for every other $x$ in $I$.

**Proof.** The proof is divided into two parts: the first is to prove that $f$ is bounded on $[a,b]$, and the second is that it attains these bounds in the interval $[a,b]$ itself.
##### Part I: $f$ is bounded
We start by assuming that $f$ is **not** bounded, i.e. for every integer $n$ there exists $x_n\in[a,b]$ such that $f(x_n)>n$. This gives us an infinite bounded sequence 
$$
x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n,\dots \quad \text{ where } x_{i} \in [a,b]
$$
By the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem, every bounded infinite sequence has at least one converging subsequence, hence we get a subsequence $x_{n_k}$ of $x_n$ that converges to a fixed point $x_0$.

Since $x_{n_k}$ is bounded in $[a,b]$, $x_0\in[a,b]$.
$$
\implies \displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}f(x_{n_k})=\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=f(x_0) \quad \text{ (since f is continuous)}
$$
But we defined $x_{n_k}$ in such a way that as $k\to\infty$ we get $n_k\to\infty$ which further means that $f(x_{n_{k}}) \to \infty$. This contradicts $\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}f(x_{n_k})=f(x_0)$. The contradiction arose due to our false assumption that $f$ is not bounded, hence $f$ is bounded from above.

In a similar manner we can prove that $f$ is bounded from below too.

##### Part 2: $f$ attains its bounds in $[a,b]$
Now that we know that $f$ is bounded and non-empty, there exists its supremum. Let 
$$
\sup{f(x):x\in[a,b]}=M.
$$
Hence, for every $n\in\mathbb{Z}^+$ we get $x_n\in[a,b]$ such that 
$$
M-\frac1n<f(x_n)\le M.
$$
This again gives us an infinite bounded sequence, hence by Bolzano–Weierstrass we get a convergent subsequence $x_{n_k}$. Let 
$$
\lim_{k\to\infty}n_k=\infty \quad \lim_{k\to\infty}x_{n_k}=d
$$
Applying the limits to the inequality, we get 
$$
\lim_{k\to\infty}\left(M-\frac1{n_k}\right)<f(x_{n_k})\le M.
$$
Both lower and upper limits converge to $M$, hence by the sandwhich theorem
$$
\lim_{k\to\infty}f(x_{n_k})=\lim_{x\to d}f(x)=f(d)=M.
$$
Hence $f$ attains its max value at $d$. Similarly we can find a point $c$ where the function attains its infimum, i.e. $f(c)=m$.
$$
\implies f(c)\le f(x)\le f(d)\ \ \forall x\in[a,b]
$$

> [!note] Notes
> 
> 1. This theorem is also called the **Extreme Value Theorem**.
> 2. Here is the [Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem Proof](#Bolzano–Weierstrass%20Theorem%20Proof).
> 3. If the function is not continuous, then it may or may not have a maximum or minimum value, as shown in the examples below.

### Local vs Absolute (Global) Extrema

> [!abstract] Absolute Extreme Values
> Let $f$ be a function with domain $D$. Then $f$ has an **absolute maximum** value on $D$ at a point $c$ if 
> $$
> f(x) \le f(c) \quad \forall x \in D
> $$
> and an **absolute minimum** value on $D$ at $c$ if 
> $$
> f(x) \ge f(c) \quad \forall x \in D
> $$

> [!note] Notes
> 
> 1. Absolute maximum and minimum values are called **absolute extrema** (plural of the Latin _extremum_). Absolute extrema are also called **global extrema**.
> 2. Functions with the same defining rule can have different extrema depending on the domain.
>     - $y=x^2$ on $(-\infty,\infty)$ has no max value, but has min value $0$.
>     - $y=x^2$ on $(0,2]$ has no min value, but has max value $4$.

> [!abstract] Local Extreme Values
> A function $f$ has a **local maximum** value at an interior point $c$ of its domain if
> $$
> f(x) \le f(c) \quad \text{for all } x \text{ in some open interval containing } c
> $$
> A function $f$ has a **local minimum** value at an interior point $c$ of its domain if 
> $$
> f(x) \ge f(c) \quad \text{for all } x \text{ in some open interval containing } c
> $$

> [!note] Notes
> 
> 1. We can extend the definition of local extrema to the endpoints of an interval: $f$ has a local minimum (or maximum) value at an endpoint $c$ if the appropriate inequality holds for all $x$ in some half-open interval in its domain containing $c$.
> 2. An absolute maximum is also a local maximum; the same goes for a local minimum and an absolute minimum. Hence a list of all local maxima/minima will include the absolute maximum/minimum.

### Finding Extrema

> [!abstract] Theorem 2 — First Derivative Theorem for Local Extreme Values
> If $f$ has a local maximum (or minimum) value at an interior point $c$ of its domain, and if $f'$ is defined at $c$, then 
> $$
> f'(c)=0
> $$

**Proof.** We prove that $f'(c)$ cannot be positive or negative, which leaves only one option: $0$. Let $c$ be the point where $f(x)$ has a local maximum. Then $f(x)\le f(c)$ for all points $x$ near enough to $c$.

Since $f'(c)$ is defined and $c$ is an interior point, both of the following one-sided limits must exist (and both equal $f'(c)$): 
$$
\text{(i)}\ \lim_{x\to c^+}\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}, \qquad \text{(ii)}\ \lim_{x\to c^-}\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}
$$
Now $f(x)-f(c)\le 0$, and $x-c>0$ in (i) while $x-c<0$ in (ii). Hence 
$$
\text{(i)}\ \lim_{x\to c^+}\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}\le 0, \qquad \text{(ii)}\ \lim_{x\to c^-}\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}\ge 0.
$$
Since the two limits are equal, we have $0 \le f'(c) \le 0$, so 
$$
\lim_{x\to c^+}\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}=\lim_{x\to c^-}\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}=0 \implies f'(c)=0.
$$
Hence the derivative of a function, if defined, is $0$ at local extrema at interior points.

In a similar manner we can prove the theorem for a local minimum. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]
> The converse is not always true, i.e. if $f'(x)=0$ at a point, the function may or may not have a local extremum there. To understand this, look at the graph of $x^{3}$. It $f'(0) = 3 \cdot (0)^{2} = 0$, but still no maxima or minima,

> [!note] Notes on Theorem 2
> 
> 1. Theorem 2 says there are 3 places where a function can take maximum/minimum values:
>     - interior points where $f'(x)=0$
>     - interior points where $f'(x)$ is undefined
>     - endpoints of the domain of $f$
> 2. **Definition:** An interior point of the domain where $f'(x)$ is $0$ or undefined is a **critical point** of $f$.
> 3. The only domain points where the function can take extreme values are **critical points** or **endpoints**.
> 4. **How to find absolute extrema of a continuous function?**
>     1. Find all critical points (most of the time they are very few in number); also include the endpoints in the list if the domain is closed.
>     2. Evaluate $f$ at all critical points and endpoints.
>     3. Take the largest and smallest of these values.

> [!example] Example Find the absolute extrema of $h(x)=x^{2/3}$ on $[-2,3]$.

**Solution.** $h'(x)=\dfrac{2}{3}x^{-1/3}=\dfrac{2}{3x^{1/3}}$. The function $h'(x)$ has no roots but is undefined at $x=0$. So we need to evaluate the function at $x=0,-2,3$:
$$
h(0)=0,\qquad h(-2)=(-2)^{2/3}=4^{1/3},\qquad h(3)=9^{1/3}.
$$
Hence the function takes its absolute extreme values at $x=0$ and $x=3$, and they are $0$ (min) and $9^{1/3}$ (max).

> [!note]
> While a function's extreme values occur only at critical points (or endpoints), the vice-versa is not true: the existence of a critical point does not automatically mean there is an extreme value. For example, look at the functions below.

Consider $f(x)=x^{1/3}$: $f'(x)=\tfrac{1}{3}x^{-2/3}$ is not defined at $x=0$.

Why can't the conclusions of Theorem 2 be expected to hold if $c$ is an endpoint of the function's domain?

**Sol.** At the endpoints of the domain the function only has a one-sided derivative, hence we can't say for sure if it is $0$. In fact it can take any real value.

##### Exercises 31 & 32
If an even/odd function has a local maximum value at $x=c$, can we say anything about the value of $f$ at $x=-c$? Give reasons for your answer.

**Sol.**
1. **Even $f$.** Since $f$ has a local max value at $c$, there is an interval containing $c$, say $(a,b)$, such that $f(x)\le f(c)\ \forall x\in(a,b)$. Now for all $x\in(-b,-a)$ we have $-x\in(a,b)$, and also $f(-c)=f(c)$. So 
	$$
	f(x)=f(-x)\le f(c)=f(-c) \implies f(x)\le f(-c)\quad \forall x\in(-b,-a)
	$$
	 Hence the function has a local maximum value at $x=-c$.
	 
2. **Odd $f$.** In a similar way we can prove that if $f$ is odd and has a local max at $x=c$, then $f$ has a local **min** at $x=-c$.

##### Exercise 33
We know how to find the extreme values of a continuous function $f(x)$ by investigating its values at critical points and endpoints. But what if there are no critical points or endpoints? What happens then? Do such functions really exist? Give reasons for your answer.

**Sol.** If a function has no critical point or endpoint, then it won't have extrema in that domain. Below are the conditions. If a function has an extremum at a point $c$ in its domain, then we have the following cases:

1. $c$ is an endpoint.
2. $f'(c)$ exists — in that case $f'(c)=0$.
3. $f'(c)$ does not exist — then it is again a critical point.

This means that $c$ is either an endpoint or a critical point, no other option is possible.

Yes, we can have functions without endpoints or critical points in their domain. For example, consider the straight line $y = x$, it has no endpoint and no endpoint. But as discussed above, they will not have any extrema in that domain.

The function is defined on $[0,1]$ but has no local maxima/minima at $x = 0$. The function is not differentiable at $x = 0$, the values around $x = 0$ oscillate infinitely taking values that are close to 0, but also from both directions (-ve and +ve) at the same time.

# 3.2 The Mean Value Theorem

### Rolle's Theorem

> [!abstract] Theorem 3
> Suppose that $y=f(x)$ is continuous at every point of the closed interval $[a,b]$ and differentiable at every point of its interior $(a,b)$. If 
> $$
> f(a)=f(b)=0
> $$
> then there is at least one number $c$ in $(a,b)$ at which 
> $$
> f'(c)=0
> $$ 

**Proof.** Being continuous means that $f(x)$ assumes a max and a min in the interval. There are 3 options for the extrema to occur:

1. At an endpoint,
2. At an interior point with $f'(c)=0$,
3. At an interior point with $f'(c)$ undefined.

But as stated in the hypothesis of the theorem, $f$ is differentiable on $(a,b)$, hence option 3 is not possible, so we need to look at options 1 and 2.

- If the min/max occurs at any interior $c$, then $f'(c)=0$.
- If the min and max both occur at endpoints, then 
$$
\min=\max=f(a)=f(b)=0 \implies f \text{ is constant and } f'(c)=0\ \ \forall c\in[a,b].
$$
- Hence we proved that there is at least one $c$ such that $f'(c)=0$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]
> Essentially the theorem says that a differentiable function will have at least one horizontal tangent in $[a,b]$ if $f(a)=f(b)=0$.

### The Mean Value Theorem (MVT)

> [!abstract] Theorem 4
> Suppose $y=f(x)$ is continuous on a closed interval $[a,b]$ and differentiable on the interval's interior $(a,b)$. Then there is at least one point $c$ in $(a,b)$ at which 
> $$
> \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}=f'(c)
> $$

**Proof.** $f$ is a continuous function with $\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ as the slope of the secant line joining the points $(a,f(a))$ and $(b,f(b))$. The equation of this line is 
$$
g(x)=f(a)+\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}(x-a).
$$
Now consider the function $h(x)=f(x)-g(x)$. $h$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$ because $f$ and $g$ are. Also $h(a)=h(b)=0$, hence by Rolle's theorem we have $h'(c)=0$ for some $c\in(a,b)$.
$$
h'(c)=0 \implies f'(c)-g'(c)=0 \implies \boxed{f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}}
$$
![](Pasted%20image%2020260922092830.png)
> [!note] Notes
> 
> 1. The theorem tells us that such a $c$ exists, but nothing about how to find it. Sometimes we can get it by solving the equation $f'(c)=\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$.
> 2. **Physical interpretation:** the number $\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ can be thought of as the _average rate of change_ and $f'(c)$ as the _instantaneous rate of change_. Then the theorem states that at some interior point the instantaneous rate of change equals the average rate of change.

### Corollaries and Some Answers

##### Functions with zero derivative are constant

> [!abstract] Corollary 1
> If $f'(x)=0$ at each point of an interval $I$, then $f(x)=C$ for all $x$ in $I$, where $C$ is a constant.

**Proof.** Take any two points $x_1$ and $x_2$ in the interval ($x_1\ne x_2$). The average rate of change of the function between them is $\dfrac{f(x_2)-f(x_1)}{x_2-x_1}$.

Since the function is differentiable on $I$, there exists $c\in(x_1,x_2)$ such that $f'(c)=\dfrac{f(x_2)-f(x_1)}{x_2-x_1}$. But $f'(c)=0 \implies \dfrac{f(x_2)-f(x_1)}{x_2-x_1}=0 \implies f(x_2)=f(x_1)$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]
> We know that if $f(x)$ is a constant function then its derivative is $0$ everywhere in its domain, this corollary gives the converse.

##### Functions with the same derivative differ by a constant

> [!abstract] Corollary 2
> If $f'(x)=g'(x)$ at each point of an interval $I$, then there exists a constant $C$ such that $f(x)=g(x)+C$ for all $x$ in $I$.

**Proof.** Consider $h(x)=f(x)-g(x)$. Then $h'(x)=f'(x)-g'(x)=0\ \ \forall x\in I$. By Corollary 1, $h(x)=C$, which means 
$$
f(x)-g(x)=C \implies f(x)=g(x)+C \qquad\blacksquare
$$

##### Finding velocity and position from acceleration

The acceleration of a free-falling body is $9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$; we need to find the velocity and position functions of time.

**Velocity.**

- If $v(t)$ is the velocity function then $v'(t)=9.8$. We also know that $g(t)=9.8t$ is a function with derivative $g'(t)=9.8$.
- So by Corollary 2: $v(t)=g(t)+C=9.8t+C$.
- At time $t=0$ we have $v(0)=0 \implies 9.8(0)+C=0 \implies C=0$, hence 
$$
\boxed{v(t)=9.8t}
$$
**Position.**

- Similarly, $s(t)$ is the position function with $s'(t)=9.8t$. Also $h(t)=4.9t^2$ has $h'(t)=9.8t$.
- $\implies s(t)=h(t)+C=4.9t^2+C$.
- $s(0)=0 \implies 4.9(0)+C=0 \implies C=0$, so 
$$
\boxed{s(t)=4.9t^2}
$$
### Increasing Functions and Decreasing Functions

##### The First Derivative Test for Increasing and Decreasing

> [!abstract] Corollary 3
> Suppose that $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$.
> - If $f'>0$ at each point of $(a,b)$, then $f$ increases on $[a,b]$.
> - If $f'<0$ at each point of $(a,b)$, then $f$ decreases on $[a,b]$.

**Proof.** Let $x_1$ and $x_2$ be any two points in $(a,b)$ such that $x_2>x_1$. By the MVT there exists some $c\in(x_1,x_2)$ such that 
$$
\frac{f(x_2)-f(x_1)}{x_2-x_1}=f'(c)\implies f(x_2)-f(x_1)=f'(c)(x_2-x_1).
$$
- If $f'(c)>0 \implies f(x_2)-f(x_1)>0 \implies f(x_2)>f(x_1)$.
- If $f'(c)<0 \implies f(x_2)-f(x_1)<0 \implies f(x_2)<f(x_1)$.

Hence $f$ is increasing if $f'(x)>0$ and decreasing if $f'(x)<0$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]
> If at a given point $f'(x)>0$ (or $f'(x)<0$), then momentarily the function is increasing (or decreasing) around some open interval containing that $x$.

### A Note on MVT and Differentiability on $[a,b]$

- In the definition of the MVT we specify that $f$ must be continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$.
- The definition of mathematical theorems always tries to have minimal conditions for a strong result. Hence, if the function is differentiable on $[a,b]$, then that is completely fine as well — it's just that that is not a necessary condition.

**Functions continuous on $[a,b]$ but differentiable only on $(a,b)$.** These are the functions we would miss if we made the definition of the MVT valid only for functions differentiable on $[a,b]$. Such a function is non-differentiable at the endpoints; there are a few reasons for it, below are the two main ones.

1. **Vertical tangents:** the function has a vertical tangent at the endpoints. Example: the semicircle $y=\sqrt{1-x^2}$ has vertical tangents at the endpoints of its domain $[-1,1]$.
2. **Infinite oscillation:** unlike the previous one where the slope becomes $\infty$, here the slope oscillates without converging to a fixed value. Example: the function 
$$
y=\begin{cases}x\sin\left(\dfrac1x\right), & x\ne0\\[1mm] 0, & x=0\end{cases}
$$
This function is continuous at $x=0$, but not differentiable at $x=0$.

For both of these types of non-differentiable functions, the MVT still applies, because the action of the MVT happens in the **interior** points of the interval and not at the endpoints.

### Derivative (vs) Limit of Derivative

The derivative at a point $x_0$ of a function $f$ is the limiting value of the slope of the secant $PQ$, where $P(x_0,f(x_0))$ and $Q$ is any nearby point $Q(x_0+h,,f(x_0+h))$: 
$$
f'(x_0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}\qquad\text{(left figure)}
$$
The limit of $f'(x)$ as $x\to x_0$ is the limiting value of the slope of the tangent at points nearby $P$ (right figure).
![](Pasted%20image%2020260922114848.png)
As discussed earlier, for a function to be differentiable we need the **first** limit to exist, and that's it; the second limit may or may not exist.

**Example.** 
$$
y=\begin{cases}x^2\sin\left(\dfrac1x\right), & x\ne0\\[4pt] 0, & x=0\end{cases} \qquad\implies\qquad y'\big|_{x=0}=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{f(x)-f(0)}{x-0}=\lim_{x\to0}x\sin\left(\frac1x\right)=0.
$$
Now 
$$
y'=\begin{cases}2x\sin\left(\dfrac1x\right)-\cos\left(\dfrac1x\right), & x\ne0\\[4pt] 0, & x=0\end{cases}
$$
Clearly $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}y'$ does not exist, but the function is still differentiable at $x=0$.

**Geometric visualisation.** If we draw the secants from $(0,0)$ to nearby points, the slope gets closer and closer to $0$, but the slope of tangents at those nearby points varies significantly.
![](Screenshot%20from%202026-09-22%2011-55-14.png)![](Screenshot%20from%202026-09-22%2011-57-38.png)
> [!note]
> If the derivative of $f$, i.e. $f'$, is continuous, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}f'(x)=f'(c)$, i.e. the limiting slope of secant lines is equal to the limiting slope of tangents.

### The Limit of Derivative Theorem

> [!abstract] Theorem
> Let $f$ be a function that is
> 
> 1. continuous at a point $c$, and
> 2. differentiable on an open interval containing $c$ (except possibly at $c$ itself).
> 
> If the limit $L=\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}f'(x)$ exists and is finite, then $f$ is differentiable at $c$ and $f'(c)=L$.

**Proof.** Given that $f$ is differentiable in some open interval containing $c$ (except possibly at $c$), let that interval be $(c-\delta,,c+\delta)$.

- For any $x\in(c-\delta,c+\delta)$, $x\ne c$, we have $f$ continuous on $[x,c]$ (or $[c,x]$) and differentiable on $(x,c)$ (or $(c,x)$) $=I$.
- Hence, by the MVT there exists $y\in I$ such that 
$$
\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}=f'(y).
$$
- Also as $x\to c$ we have $y\to c$, so 
$$
\lim_{x\to c}\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}=\lim_{y\to c}f'(y)=L\quad(\text{given}).
$$
- $\implies f'(c)=\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}=L$. Hence proved. $\blacksquare$

> [!example]
> For what values of $a$, $m$ and $b$ does the function
> $$
> f(x)=\begin{cases}3, & x=0\\ -x^2+3x+a, & 0<x<1\\ mx+b, & 1\le x\le 2\end{cases}
> $$
> satisfy the hypothesis of the MVT on the interval $[0,2]$?

**Solution.** The piecewise functions are polynomials and their derivatives are continuous, so we don't have to check within each interval. We only need to verify at the breaking points.

Two limits must satisfy, first is the limit of the function must be same and second is the limit of the difference quotient (derivative) must be the same.

Continuity at $x = 0$,
$$
\lim_{x\to0^{+}}f(x)=a=f(0)=3\implies\boxed{a=3}.
$$
Continuity at $x=1$,
$$
\begin{align}
\lim_{x\to1^-}f(x) & =-1+3+a=2+a=5 \\
\lim_{x\to1^+}f(x) & =m+b \\
 \implies m+b & =5
\end{align}
$$
Now, we need to check for limits of difference quotient, since, the functions are polynomials, the limit is same as the limit of derivative (prev theorem), hence we have,
$$
\begin{align}
\lim_{ x \to 1^{-} } f'(x) & = \lim_{ x \to 1^{-} } (-2x + 3) = 1  \\
\lim_{ x \to 1^{+} } f'(x) & = \lim_{ x \to 1^{+} } m = m
\end{align}
$$
$$
\implies m = 1 \implies b = 5 - 1 = 4
$$
Hence, for $f(x)$ to satisfy the MVT hypothesis we need $a=3,\ m=1,\ b=4$.

### Exercises

##### Exercise 12
Suppose that $f''$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and that $f$ has three zeros in the interval. Show that $f''$ has at least one zero in $(a,b)$. Generalise this result.

**Sol.** $f''$ continuous on $[a,b]$ $\implies$ $f'$ is differentiable on $[a,b]$ $\implies$ $f'$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ $\implies$ $f$ is differentiable on $[a,b]$ $\implies$ $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$.

- If $f$ has 3 roots, there exist $x_1<x_2<x_3\in[a,b]$ such that $f(x_1)=f(x_2)=f(x_3)=0$.
- We have two disjoint intervals $[x_1,x_2]$ and $[x_2,x_3]$; applying Rolle's theorem on both, there exist $d_1$ and $d_2$ in those such that $f'(d_1)=f'(d_2)=0$.
- Again applying Rolle's theorem on $[d_1,d_2]$ (to $f'$) we get a point $c$ such that $f''(c)=0$.

**Generalisation.** In general, if $f$ has $n$ distinct roots, then $f'$ has at least $n-1$ roots and $f''$ has at least $n-2$ roots.

##### Exercise 13
Show that if $f''>0$ throughout an interval $[a,b]$, then $f'$ has at most one zero in $[a,b]$. What if $f''<0$ throughout $[a,b]$ instead?

**Sol.** If $f''>0$, then for any $x_1<x_2$ in $[a,b]$ we will have $f'(x_1)<f'(x_2)$. There cannot be 2 roots, otherwise we would get $f'(x_1)=f'(x_2)$ and applying Rolle's theorem we can prove that $f''(x) = 0$ for some $x \in (x_{1},x_{2})$. Similarly, if $f''<0$, then also there will be at most one root.

##### Exercise 17
Suppose that $f$ is differentiable on $[0,1]$, and that its derivative is never zero. Show that $f(0)\ne f(1)$.

# 3.3 The First Derivative Test for Local Extreme Values

> [!abstract] Theorem 5
> The following test applies to a continuous function $f(x)$.
> 
> **At a critical point $c$:**
> 
> 1. If $f'$ changes from positive to negative at $c$ ($f'>0$ for $x<c$ and $f'<0$ for $x>c$), then $f$ has a **local maximum** value at $c$.
> ![](Pasted%20image%2020260922181805.png)
> 2. If $f'$ changes from negative to positive at $c$ ($f'<0$ for $x<c$ and $f'>0$ for $x>c$), then $f$ has a **local minimum** value at $c$.
> ![](Pasted%20image%2020260922181827.png)
> 3. If $f'$ does not change sign at $c$ ($f'$ has the same sign on both sides of $c$), then $f$ has **no local extreme value** at $c$.
> ![](Pasted%20image%2020260922181855.png)
> **At a left endpoint $a$:** if $f'>0$ (or $f'<0$) for $x>a$, then $f$ has a local minimum (or maximum) value at $a$.
> ![](Pasted%20image%2020260922181906.png)
> **At a right endpoint $b$:** if $f'>0$ (or $f'<0$) for $x<b$, then $f$ has a local maximum (or minimum) value at $b$.
> ![](Pasted%20image%2020260922181917.png)

> [!example] Example
> Find the critical points for $f(x)=x^{1/3}(x-4)$ and also find the local and global extrema.

**Solution.** 
$$
f(x)=x^{1/3}(x-4)=x^{4/3}-4x^{1/3}
$$
 
$$
\implies f'(x)=\frac43x^{1/3}-\frac43x^{-2/3}=\frac43,\frac{(x-1)}{x^{2/3}}
$$
 Hence the critical points are $x=1$ and $x=0$.
![](Pasted%20image%2020260922182122.png)
From the equation of $f'(x)=\dfrac43\dfrac{(x-1)}{x^{2/3}}$ we see that $x^{2/3}$ is always $>0$ and $(x-1)$ changes sign at $x=1$. Hence $f'(x)$ changes sign only at $x=1$: 
$$
\begin{align}
f'(x)\ge0,\ x\ge1 \qquad f'(x)<0,\ x<1\ &\ x\ne0
\end{align}
$$
The sign of $f'(x)$ changes from $-$ve to $+$ve, which gives that $f$ has a **local minimum at $x=1$** and **no local extremum at $x=0$**.

The function has no endpoints here, and as $x\to\infty$ or $x\to-\infty$ we get $f(x)\to\infty$; hence $f(x)$ has **no absolute maximum** and $f(x)$ has an **absolute minimum at $x=1$** (value $f(1)=-3$).

# 3.4 Graphing with $y'$ and $y''$

### Concavity

> [!abstract] Definition
> The graph of a differentiable function $y=f(x)$ is **concave up** on an interval where $y'$ is increasing and **concave down** on an interval where $y'$ is decreasing.

**Example:** The curve $y=x^3$ has derivative $y'=3x^2$; the derivative decreases on the interval $(-\infty,0)$ and increases on $(0,\infty)$. Hence, in the graph of $y=x^3$ we see that the concavity is **down** in $(-\infty,0)$ and **up** in $(0,\infty)$.
![](Pasted%20image%2020260922200718.png)
On concave down the graph turns from left to right, and on concave up the graph turns from right to left.

### The Second Derivative Test for Concavity

> [!abstract] Theorem
> Let $y=f(x)$ be twice differentiable on an interval $I$.
> 1. If $y''>0$ on $I$, the graph of $f$ over $I$ is concave up.
> 2. If $y''<0$ on $I$, the graph of $f$ over $I$ is concave down.

> [!example]
> Apply this test on $y=x^3$.

**Solution.** $y=x^3\implies y'=3x^2\implies y''=6x$.

- For $x\in(-\infty,0)$ we have $y''=6x<0$, hence $y$ is concave down.
- For $x\in(0,\infty)$ we have $y''=6x>0$, hence $y$ is concave up.

### Points of Inflection

> [!abstract] Definition
> A point where the graph of a function has a tangent and where the concavity changes is called a **point of inflection**.

On the graph of a twice-differentiable function, $y''=0$ at a point of inflection.

> [!example] Example Find the points of inflection of the graph $y=2+\cos x$.

**Solution.** $y'=-\sin x\implies y''=-\cos x$, hence the points of inflection are 
$$
x=\frac{(2n+1)\pi}{2},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}.
$$
If $y$ gives the position of a particle in simple harmonic motion, then $y'$ gives the velocity and $y''$ gives the acceleration. Points of inflection in this case give the exact point where the acceleration changes direction. Hence, the acceleration changes direction at the centre position of the motion.

> [!note]
> We said that $y''=0$ at a point of inflection, but the converse may not be true, i.e. if $y''=0$ at a point, then that point may or may not be a point of inflection; for it to be a point of inflection, $y''$ must change sign around that point.

> [!example]
> An inflection point where $y''$ does not exist The curve $y=x^{1/3}$ has a point of inflection at $x=0$, but $y''$ does not exist there.
> $$
> y''=\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\left(x^{1/3}\right)=\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac13x^{-2/3}\right)=-\frac29x^{-5/3}
> $$
> As $x\to0^-$ we have $y''\to\infty$ and as $x\to0^+$, $y''\to-\infty$; hence $y''$ changes sign at $x=0$ but is undefined at $x=0$.

> [!example]
> No inflection where $y''=0$ The curve $y=x^4$ has no inflection at $x=0$ even though we have $y''=12x^2=0$ at $x=0$.

The reason is that $y''\ge0$, i.e. the sign of $y''$ does not change at $x=0$.

### The Second Derivative Test for Local Extreme Values

> [!abstract] Theorem
> 
> 1. If $f'(c)=0$ and $f''(c)<0$, then $f$ has a local maximum at $x=c$.
> 2. If $f'(c)=0$ and $f''(c)>0$, then $f$ has a local minimum at $x=c$.

> [!note]
> The test is easy to apply, which makes it good, but the test gives an inconclusive result when $y''=0$ or $y''$ does not exist.

### Strategy for Graphing $y=f(x)$

1. Find $y'$ and $y''$.
2. Find the rise and fall of the curve.
3. Determine the concavity of the curve.
4. Make a summary and show the curve's general shape.
5. Plot specific points and sketch the curve.

> [!abstract] Definition — Cusp
> The graph of a continuous function $y=f(x)$ has a **cusp** at a point $x=c$ if the concavity is the same on both sides of $c$ and either
> 1. $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c^-}f'(x)=\infty$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c^+}f'(x)=-\infty$, or
> 2. $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c^-}f'(x)=-\infty$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c^+}f'(x)=\infty$.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260922203556.png)

## 3.5 Limits as $x\to\pm\infty$, Asymptotes, and Dominant Terms

> [!abstract] Definition
> 1. We say that $f(x)$ has the limit $L$ as $x$ approaches infinity and write 
> $$
> \lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=L
> $$
> if, for every number $\varepsilon>0$, there exists a corresponding number $M$ such that for all $x$
> $$
> x>M\implies|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon
> $$
> 2. We say that $f(x)$ has the limit $L$ as $x$ approaches minus infinity and write
> $$
> \lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x)=L
> $$
> if, for every number $\varepsilon>0$, there exists a corresponding number $N$ such that for all $x$
> $$
> x<N\implies|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon
> $$

> [!abstract] Theorem 6 — Properties of Limits as $x\to\pm\infty$
> The following rules hold if $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x)=L$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}g(x)=M$ ($L$ and $M$ are real numbers).
> 1. **Sum Rule:** $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\big(f(x)+g(x)\big)=L+M$
> 2. **Difference Rule:** $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\big(f(x)-g(x)\big)=L-M$
> 3. **Product Rule:** $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x)\cdot g(x)=L\cdot M$
> 4. **Constant Multiple Rule:** $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}k\cdot f(x)=kL\ \ \forall k\in\mathbb{R}$
> 5. **Quotient Rule:** $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\frac{L}{M}$, if $M\ne0$
> 6. **Power Rule:** if $m$ and $n$ are integers, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\big[f(x)\big]^{m/n}=L^{m/n}$, provided $L^{m/n}$ is a real number.

> [!example]
> Evaluate the following limits:
> **(a)** $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(5+\frac1x\right)$
> **(b)** $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{\pi\sqrt3}{x^2}$

**Solution.**

**(a)** $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(5+\frac1x\right)=\lim_{x\to\infty}5+\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac1x=5+0=5$

**(b)** $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{\pi\sqrt3}{x^2}=\lim_{x\to-\infty}\pi\sqrt3\cdot\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac1{x^2}=\pi\sqrt3\cdot0=0$

### Limits of Rational Functions as $x\to\pm\infty$

For $\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}$ with $a_n$, $b_n$ the leading coefficients of $f$ and $g$:

1. If $\deg(f)=\deg(g)$, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\frac{a_n}{b_n}$, the ratio of the leading coefficients of $f$ and $g$.
2. If $\deg(f)<\deg(g)$, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=0$.
3. If $\deg(f)>\deg(g)$, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\pm\infty$ depending on the signs of numerator and denominator.

> [!example] Example Evaluate the following limits:
> **(a)** $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{5x^2+8x-3}{3x^2+2}$
> **(b)** $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{11x+2}{2x^3-1}$
> **(c)** $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x^2-3}{7x+4}$

**Solution.**

**(a)** $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{5x^2+8x-3}{3x^2+2}=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{5+\frac8x-\frac3{x^2}}{3+\frac2{x^2}}=\frac{5+0-0}{3+0}=\frac53$

**(b)** $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{11x+2}{2x^3-1}=\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{\frac{11}{x^2}+\frac{2}{x^3}}{2-\frac1{x^3}}=0$

**(c)** $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x^2-3}{7x+4}=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x-\frac3x}{7+\frac4x}=\infty$

### Horizontal and Vertical Asymptotes

> [!abstract] Definition
> Asymptote If the graph of a function is such that the distance between the graph and a fixed line approaches $0$ as the graph moves increasingly away from the origin, then we say the graph approaches the line **asymptotically** and the line is an **asymptote** of the graph.

> [!abstract] Definition — Horizontal Asymptote
> A line $y=b$ is called a **horizontal asymptote** of the graph of a function $y=f(x)$ if either 
> $$
> \lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=b\quad\text{or}\quad\lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x)=b
> $$

> [!abstract] Definition — Vertical Asymptote
> A line $x=a$ is a **vertical asymptote** of the graph of a function $y=f(x)$ if either 
> $$
> \lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=\pm\infty\quad\text{or}\quad\lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)=\pm\infty
> $$

> [!example] Example Do the curves $y=\sec x$ and $y=\tan x$ have asymptotes?

**Sol.** Both $y=\sec x$ and $y=\tan x$ have asymptotes at $x=\dfrac{(2n+1)\pi}{2}$; this is because both have $\cos x$ in their denominator.
![](Pasted%20image%2020260922205544.png)
> [!example]
> Find the asymptotes of the curve $y=\dfrac{x+3}{x+2}$.

**Sol.** We know that as $x\to\pm\infty$, $y=\dfrac{x+3}{x+2}\to1$. But for $x>-2$ the graph approaches from above, and for $x<-2$ the graph approaches from below. The graph also has an asymptote at $x=-2$. 
$$
y=\frac{x+3}{x+2}=1+\frac1{x+2}
$$
![](Pasted%20image%2020260922205646.png)

> [!example]
> Find the asymptotes of the curve $y=\dfrac{-8}{x^2-4}$.

**Sol.** The limits we are interested in are $x\to\pm\infty,\ \pm2$. Also, since the function is even we know it is symmetrical about the y-axis, so we only need the behaviour at $x=\infty,,2$.

- As $x\to\infty$ we have $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{-8}{x^2-4}=0$, and at $x=2$ we have $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to2^+}\frac{-8}{x^2-4}=-\infty$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to2^-}\frac{-8}{x^2-4}=\infty$.
- Hence, the graph of the function is:

> [!note]
> It feels like functions have vertical asymptotes at points where their denominators are $0$, but this is not always true. What is true is: functions have vertical asymptotes at points where their denominators are $0$ **if they are in most reduced form**.

**Example:** $y=\dfrac{x^3-1}{x^2-1}$ only has a vertical asymptote at $x=-1$; at $x=1$ we have $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}y=\frac32$ and not $\pm\infty$.

> [!example]
> Using the sandwich theorem, find the asymptotes of the curve $y=2+\dfrac{\sin x}{x}$.

**Sol.** At $x\to0$ we have $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}y=2+\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=3$, hence no vertical asymptote.
As $x\to\pm\infty$ we have $\left|\dfrac{\sin x}{x}\right|\le\left|\dfrac1x\right|$
$$
\implies -\frac1{|x|}\le\frac{\sin x}{x}\le\frac1{|x|},
$$
and since $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac1{|x|}=0$, by the sandwich theorem we have $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{\sin x}{x}=0$, which makes $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}y=2$. Hence, the curve has a horizontal asymptote $y=2$ as $x\to\pm\infty$.

### Oblique Asymptotes

If the degree of the numerator of a rational function is higher than that of the denominator, then the graph has an **oblique asymptote**, that is a linear asymptote that is neither vertical nor horizontal.

> [!example] Example Find the asymptotes of the graph $y=\dfrac{x^2-3}{2x-4}$.

**Sol.** The limits of interest in this are $x\to\pm\infty$ and $x\to2$. First we divide the numerator $x^2-3$ by $2x-4$ and get quotient and remainder: 
$$
x^2-3=\left(\frac x2+1\right)(2x-4)+1\impliesy=\frac x2+1+\frac1{2x-4}
$$
> [!todo] FIGURE: Long-division layout: $2x-4,\overline{),x^2-3}$ giving quotient $\frac x2+1$ (steps $x^2-2x$, then $2x-3$, then $2x-4$, remainder $1$).

Hence $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to2^+}y=\infty$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to2^-}y=-\infty$, and as $x\to\pm\infty$ we have $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac1{2x-4}=0$, hence the function gets closer and closer to $\dfrac x2+1$: 
$$
\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}y=\frac x2+1.
$$
Hence the graph has a vertical asymptote at $x=2$ and an oblique asymptote $y=\dfrac x2+1$ as $x\to\pm\infty$.

### Graphing with Asymptotes and Dominant Terms

- In the last example we say that the term $\dfrac x2+1$ **dominates** when $x$ is numerically large and the term $\dfrac1{2x-4}$ dominates when $x$ is close to $2$.
- These dominant terms help us in graphing functions.

### Checklist for Graphing a Function $y=f(x)$

1. Look for symmetry: is the function even or odd?
2. Is the function a shift of a known function?
3. Analyse dominant terms. Divide rational functions into polynomial + remainder.
4. Check for asymptotes and removable discontinuity. Is there a $0$ denominator at any point? What happens when $x\to\pm\infty$?
5. Compute $f'$ and solve $f'=0$. Identify critical points and intervals of rise and fall.
6. Compute $f''$ to determine concavity and inflection points.
7. Sketch the graph's general shape.
8. Evaluate $f$ at special values (endpoints, critical points, intercepts).
9. Graph $f$, using dominant terms, general shape, and special points for guidance.

Suppose an odd function is known to be increasing on the interval $x>0$. What can be said about its behaviour on the interval $x<0$? What if the function is even?

#### Exercise 83

How many horizontal asymptotes can the graph of a given rational function have? Give reasons for your answer.

**Sol.** A function can have at most 2 horizontal asymptotes: one at $x\to\infty$ and the other at $x\to-\infty$.

#### Exercise 86 — A Puzzle

Suppose we want to identify the oblique asymptote of a curve $y=\dfrac{x^2+3x+7}{x+2}$. If we recast the rational function as a polynomial plus remainder we find 
$$
y=\frac{x^2+3x+7}{x+2}=x+1+\frac5{x+2},
$$
 hence the oblique asymptote must be the line $y=x+1$. But suppose, instead, that we divide both numerator and denominator by $x$, to obtain 
$$
y=\frac{x^2+3x+7}{x+2}=\frac{x+3+\frac7x}{1+\frac2x}.
$$
 For large $x$ the value is approximately $x+3$, so the asymptote should be the line $y=x+3$. Which line is the real asymptote?

$$
 The reason is that we ignored how the small $\dfrac2x$ in the denominator interacts with $x$ in the numerator to give a constant result and not $0$.

**Reason 2.** The distance between $f(x)$ and the asymptote must approach $0$ as $x\to\infty$. 
$$
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)-(x+1)=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac5{x+2}=0
$$
 
$$
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)-(x+3)=\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(-2+\frac5{x+2}\right)=-2\ne0.
$$
 Hence, $f(x)$ approaches $x+1$ and not $x+3$ as $x\to\infty$.

## 3.6 Optimization

### Strategy for Solving Max-Min Problems

1. **Read the problem** until you understand it. What is unknown? What is given? What is sought?
2. **Draw a picture.** Label any part that may be important to the problem.
3. **Introduce variables.** List every relation in the picture and in the problem as an equation or algebraic expression.
4. **Identify the unknown.** Write an equation for it. If you can, express the unknown as a function of a single variable (or in two equations in two unknowns). This may require considerable manipulation.
5. **Test the critical points and endpoints.** Use what you know about the shape of the function's graph and the physics of the problem. Use the first and second derivatives to identify and classify critical points (where $f'=0$ or does not exist).

> [!example] Example — Open-top box An open-top box is to be made by cutting congruent squares from the corners of a $12$ by $12$ inch sheet of tin and bending up the sides. How large should the squares be to hold the maximum volume in the resulting box?

> [!todo] FIGURE: A $12\times12$ cross-shaped sheet: square corners of side $x$ cut out at each of the four corners; $12$ marked along the top and left edges.

**Solution.** 
$$
V=x(12-2x)^2=x(144-48x+4x^2)\impliesV=144x-48x^2+4x^3
$$
 
$$
\implies\frac{dV}{dx}=144-96x+12x^2=12(12-8x+x^2)=12(x-2)(x-6)
$$
The domain of $V(x)$ is $0\le x\le6$, hence the critical points (and endpoints) are $0,,2,,6$. 
$$
V(0)=0,\quad V(2)=128,\quad V(6)=0
$$
 Hence $x=2$ gives the **maximum volume** ($128\ \text{in}^3$).

> [!example] Example — 1-L oil can You have been asked to design a $1$-L oil can shaped like a right circular cylinder. What dimensions will you use for the can to use the least material?

**Solution.** Volume: $\pi r^2h=1000\ \text{cm}^3$.

To minimise material use, we need to minimise the total surface area: 
$$
A=\underbrace{2(\pi r^2)}_{\text{2 lids}}+\underbrace{2\pi rh}_{\text{can walls}}
$$
We need $A$ in terms of either $r$ or $h$, so we use the first relation and put $h=\dfrac{1000}{\pi r^2}$: 
$$
A=2\pi r^2+\frac{2000}{r}\implies\frac{dA}{dr}=4\pi r-\frac{2000}{r^2}
$$
 
$$
\frac{dA}{dr}=0\implies4\pi r-\frac{2000}{r^2}=0\implies r=\left(\frac{500}{\pi}\right)^{1/3}
$$
 
$$
\frac{d^2A}{dr^2}=4\pi+\frac{4000}{r^3},
$$
 which for the domain of $r$, $(0,\infty)$, is always $>0$.

Hence we have a **min $A$** at $r=\left(\dfrac{500}{\pi}\right)^{1/3}$, $\ h=2\left(\dfrac{500}{\pi}\right)^{1/3}=2r$.

> [!example] Example — Two numbers Find two positive numbers whose sum is $20$ and whose product is as large as possible.

**Solution.** $P=xy$ and $x+y=20\implies P=x(20-x)=20x-x^2$. 
$$
\frac{dP}{dx}=20-2x=0\implies x=10,\qquad\frac{d^2P}{dx^2}=-2<0,
$$
 hence there is a max at $x=10$ (so both numbers are $10$).

> [!example] Example — Rectangle in a semicircle A rectangle is to be inscribed in a semicircle of radius $2$. What is the largest area the rectangle can have, and what are its dimensions?

> [!todo] FIGURE: Semicircle with an inscribed rectangle; radius $r$ drawn from the centre to the top-right corner making angle $\theta$ with the base; height $x$ and half-width $y$ labelled.

**Solution.** Height $=x$, width $=2y$, radius $r$ (here $r=2$): 
$$
r^2=x^2+y^2\impliesx=\sqrt{r^2-y^2}
$$
 
$$
\implies\text{Area}=x\cdot2y=2y\sqrt{r^2-y^2}
$$
 Here the domain of $x$ and $y$ is $[0,r]$. 
$$
\frac{dA}{dy}=2\sqrt{r^2-y^2}+2y\cdot\frac1{2\sqrt{r^2-y^2}}\cdot(-2y)=\frac{2,(r^2-y^2-y^2)}{\sqrt{r^2-y^2}}=0
$$
 
$$
\implies y=\frac r{\sqrt2}\impliesx=\sqrt{r^2-\frac{r^2}2}=\frac r{\sqrt2}
$$
Area at the endpoints is $A(0)=0$ and $A(r)=0$, and area at $\dfrac r{\sqrt2}$ is $A!\left(\dfrac r{\sqrt2}\right)=r^2=4$.

Hence, the **max area $=4$**.

### Fermat's Principle and Snell's Law

> [!abstract] Fermat's Principle Light always travels from one point to another along the quickest route.

> [!example] Example (Snell's law) Find the path that a ray of light will follow in going from a point $A$ in a medium where the speed of light is $c_1$ across a straight boundary to a point $B$ in a medium where the speed of light is $c_2$.

> [!todo] FIGURE: Ray from $A$ (height $a$ above the boundary, horizontal distance $d_1$ total) to point $P$ on the boundary at distance $x$ from the foot of $A$, then on to $B$ (depth $d_2-a$ below). Angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ marked from the normal at $P$; path lengths $p_1$, $p_2$; horizontal distances $x$ and $d_1-x$.

**Solution.** 
$$
t_1=\frac{p_1}{c_1},\quad t_2=\frac{p_2}{c_2},\qquad p_1=\sqrt{x^2+a^2},\quad p_2=\sqrt{(d_1-x)^2+(d_2-a)^2}
$$
 
$$
\implies\text{total time}=t_1+t_2=\frac{\sqrt{x^2+a^2}}{c_1}+\frac{\sqrt{(d_1-x)^2+(d_2-a)^2}}{c_2}
$$
 
$$
\implies\frac{dt}{dx}=\frac{x}{c_1\sqrt{x^2+a^2}}-\frac{d_1-x}{c_2\sqrt{(d_1-x)^2+(d_2-a)^2}}=\frac{\sin\theta_1}{c_1}-\frac{\sin\theta_2}{c_2}
$$
 For $\dfrac{dt}{dx}=0$, we need 
$$
\boxed{\frac{\sin\theta_1}{c_1}=\frac{\sin\theta_2}{c_2}}
$$
When $P$ is closer to $A$, then $\theta_1<\theta_2\implies\sin\theta_1<\sin\theta_2$, i.e. $\dfrac{dt}{dx}$ is $-$ve, and when $P$ is closer to $B$ we get $\dfrac{dt}{dx}>0$ in the same way. Hence, at $\dfrac{\sin\theta_1}{c_1}=\dfrac{\sin\theta_2}{c_2}$ we get **minimum time**.

### Cost and Revenue in Economics
$$
r(x)=\text{the revenue from selling }x\text{ items}
$$
 
$$
c(x)=\text{the cost of producing }x\text{ items}
$$
 
$$
p(x)=r(x)-c(x)=\text{profit on selling }x\text{ items}
$$
 
$$
\frac{dr}{dx}=\text{marginal revenue},\qquad\frac{dc}{dx}=\text{marginal cost}
$$
> [!abstract] Theorem 7 Maximum profit (if any) occurs at a production level at which marginal revenue equals marginal cost.

**Proof.** If we assume $r(x)$ and $c(x)$ to be differentiable functions in $x$, then $p(x)$ is also a differentiable function in $x$, and hence it will achieve its max/min when $p'(x)=0$: 
$$
p'(x)=r'(x)-c'(x)=0\implies\boxed{r'(x)=c'(x)}
$$
> [!note] Note Theorem 7 does not say that $p'(x)=0$ guarantees max profit; all it says is that max profit will occur at a point where $p'(x)=0$. Hence we must examine profit at all those points where $p'(x)=0$ to find global maxima.

> [!abstract] Theorem 8 The production level (if any) at which average cost is smallest is a level at which the average cost equals the marginal cost.

**Proof.** Average cost $=\dfrac{c(x)}{x}$. If $c(x)$ is a differentiable function of $x$, then average cost is also differentiable, and hence min/max will occur at a point where $\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(\dfrac{c(x)}{x}\right)=0$: 
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{c(x)}x\right)=0\implies\frac{x,c'(x)-c(x)}{x^2}=0\implies\boxed{c'(x)=\frac{c(x)}x}
$$
 Hence proved. $\blacksquare$

> [!note] Note Again, this theorem does not tell where the min average cost is, but it tells where to look for it.

## 3.7 Linearisation and Differentials

> [!abstract] Definition — Linearisation If $f$ is differentiable at $x=a$, then the approximating function 
$$
L(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)
$$
 is the **linearisation** of $f$ at $a$. The approximation 
$$
f(x)\approx L(x)
$$
 of $f$ by $L$ is the **standard linear approximation** of $f$ at $a$. The point $x=a$ is the **centre** of the approximation.

> [!example] Example Find the linear approximation of $y=\sqrt{1+x}$ at $x=0$.

**Sol.** $y'=\dfrac1{2\sqrt{1+x}}\implies y'\big|_{x=0}=\dfrac12$ and $y(0)=1$ 
$$
\implies L=1+\frac12(x-0)=1+\frac x2.
$$
Let's do some trial runs to see how accurate $L$ is:

|Value|Approximation|Result|Accuracy|
|---|---|---|---|
|$\sqrt{1.2}$|$1+\dfrac{0.2}{2}$|$1.10$|to 2 decimals|
|$\sqrt{1.05}$|$1+\dfrac{0.05}{2}$|$1.025$|to 3 decimals|

### Differentials

> [!abstract] Definition Let $y=f(x)$ be a differentiable function. The differential $dx$ is an independent variable. The differential $dy$ is 
$$
dy=f'(x),dx.
$$
> [!note] Notes
> 
> 1. If we think about it, then $dy$ is actually the change in $y$ along the tangent at $f(x)$ if you took a step of $dx$.
> 2. **The meaning:** in most contexts, the differential $dx$ of the independent variable is its change $\Delta x$, but we do not impose this rule/restriction on the definition. Unlike the independent variable $dx$, the variable $dy$ is always a dependent variable. It depends on both $x$ and $dx$.
> 3. If $dx\ne0$, then we can divide by $dx$ on both sides and get $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=f'(x)$, i.e. the derivative is the ratio of differentials. But here $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ is not treated as a single symbol; rather it is seen as the quotient of 2 variables $dy$ & $dx$.
> 4. We sometimes write $df=f'(x),dx$ in place of $dy=f'(x),dx$ and call $df$ the differential of $f$.
> 5. Every differentiation formula like $\dfrac{d(u+v)}{dx}=\dfrac{du}{dx}+\dfrac{dv}{dx}$ has a corresponding differential form like $d(u+v)=du+dv$. Below are a few with proof.

**Sum rule:** $\boxed{d(u+v)=du+dv}$

_Proof._ Let $f(x)=u+v\implies f'(x)=u'+v'$ 
$$
\implies df=f'(x),dx\implies d(u+v)=(u'+v'),dx=u',dx+v',dx.
$$
 Now $u$ & $v$ are differentiable functions, hence we have $du=u',dx$ & $dv=v',dx$ 
$$
\implies d(u+v)=du+dv.\ \blacksquare
$$
**Product rule:** $\boxed{d(uv)=u,dv+v,du}$

_Proof._ $f=uv\implies f'=uv'+vu'\implies df=f',dx\implies d(uv)=(uv'+vu'),dx\implies d(uv)=u,dv+v,du.\ \blacksquare$

> [!note] Note Similarly we can prove 
$$
\boxed{d!\left(\frac uv\right)=\frac{v,du-u,dv}{v^2}}\quad&\quad\boxed{d(u^n)=nu^{n-1},du}
$$
### Estimating Change with Differentials

Let $f(x)$ be differentiable at $x=x_0$. The approximate change in the value of $f$ when $x$ changes from $x_0$ to $x_0+dx$ is 
$$
df=f'(x_0),dx.
$$
**Proof.** We know that for small $dx$ the change in $f$, i.e. $\Delta f$, is approximately equal to the change in its linearisation $\Delta L$: 
$$
\Delta f\approx\Delta L=L(x_0+dx)-L(x_0)=f(x_0)+f'(x_0)(x_0+dx-x_0)-f(x_0)=f'(x_0),dx
$$
 (since $f(x_0)=L(x_0)$) 
$$
\implies\boxed{\Delta f\approx\Delta L=f'(x_0),dx=df}
$$
> [!note] Note This also proves the geometric interpretation of $df$ which we mentioned earlier, which is that $df$ is the change in the tangent at $f(x_0)$ if we move by $dx$.

> [!example] Example The radius $r$ of a circle increases from $r_0=10$ m to $10.1$ m. Estimate the increase in the circle's area $A$ by calculating $dA$. Compare this with the true $\Delta A$.

**Sol.** $A=\pi r^2\implies A'(r)=2\pi r$.

Approximate change in $A$: $\Delta A\approx dA=A'(r_0),dr$ 
$$
\implies dA=A'(10)\cdot(0.1)=2\pi\cdot10\cdot0.1=2\pi\ \text{m}^2
$$
 The true change: 
$$
\Delta A=A(r_0+dr)-A(r_0)=\pi\left((10.1)^2-(10)^2\right)=\pi(102.01-100)=\underbrace{2\pi}_{dA}+\underbrace{0.01\pi}_{\text{error}}
$$
> [!example] Example Suppose the earth were a perfect sphere and we determined its radius to be $3959\pm0.1$ miles. What effect would the tolerance of $\pm0.1$ have on our estimate of the earth's surface area?

**Sol.** $S=4\pi r^2\implies S'=8\pi r$, hence at $r=3959$ & $dr=0.1$ we have 
$$
\Delta S\approx dS=S',dr=8\pi(3959)(0.1)\approx9950\ \text{mi}^2
$$
 This is roughly equal to the area of the state of Manipur or Meghalaya. Hence the surface area that we calculate could be off by $9950\ \text{mi}^2$.

> [!example] Example About how accurately should we measure the radius $r$ of a sphere to calculate the surface area $S=4\pi r^2$ within $1%$ of its true value?

**Sol.** If $r$ is the true radius then $S=4\pi r^2$ and the tolerance is 
$$
dS=\frac1{100}\times4\pi r^2=\frac{4\pi r^2}{100}.
$$
 If $dr$ is the max tolerance allowed then we have 
$$
dS=S',dr=8\pi r\cdot dr\implies\frac{4\pi r^2}{100}=8\pi r\cdot dr\impliesdr=\frac r{200}
$$
 
$$
\implies\frac{dr}r\times100=0.5
$$
 Hence we should measure $r$ with an error $dr$ that is no more than $0.5%$ of the true value.

### The Error in the Approximation $\Delta f\approx df$

If $f(x)$ is a differentiable function at $x=x_0$ and $x$ changes from $x_0$ to $x_0+\Delta x$, the change $\Delta y$ in $f$ is given by an equation of the form 
$$
\Delta y=f'(x_0),\Delta x+\varepsilon,\Delta x
$$
 in which $\varepsilon\to0$ as $\Delta x\to0$.

**Proof.** We know that $df=f'(x_0),\Delta x$ is the differential of $f$ which we use to approximate $\Delta f$; the approximation error is 
$$
\text{error}=\Delta f-df=\Delta f-f'(x_0),\Delta x=\big[f(x_0+\Delta x)-f(x_0)\big]-f'(x_0),\Delta x
$$
 
$$
\implies\text{error}=\left[\frac{f(x_0+\Delta x)-f(x_0)}{\Delta x}-f'(x_0)\right]\Delta x=\varepsilon,\Delta x,
$$
 where $\varepsilon=\dfrac{f(x_0+\Delta x)-f(x_0)}{\Delta x}-f'(x_0)$.

Now it is clear that as $\Delta x\to0$ we have $\dfrac{f(x_0+\Delta x)-f(x_0)}{\Delta x}\to f'(x_0)$, hence $\varepsilon\to0$.

The actual change in the value of the function, i.e. $\Delta f$, can now be written as $\Delta f=df+\text{error}$: 
$$
\boxed{\Delta f=f'(x_0),\Delta x+\varepsilon,\Delta x}
$$
### Proof of the Chain Rule

> [!abstract] Statement If $f(u)$ is a differentiable function of $u$ and $u=g(x)$ is a differentiable function of $x$, then the composite $y=f(g(x))$ is a differentiable function of $x$. More precisely, if $g$ is differentiable at $x_0$ and $f$ is differentiable at $g(x_0)$, then the composite is differentiable at $x_0$.

**Proof.** Let $\Delta x$ be the change, i.e. $x_0$ to $x_0+\Delta x$; then let $\Delta y$ and $\Delta u$ be the corresponding changes in those functions.

- We know that $\dfrac{dy}{dx}\Big|_{x=x_0}=\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta x\to0}\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$; we will show that the right-hand limit is equal to $f'(g(x_0))\cdot g'(x_0)$.
- $\Delta y=f'(u_0),\Delta u+\varepsilon_1\Delta u=\big(f'(u_0)+\varepsilon_1\big)\Delta u$
- $\Delta u=g'(x_0),\Delta x+\varepsilon_2\Delta x=\big(g'(x_0)+\varepsilon_2\big)\Delta x$
- As $\Delta x\to0$, we have $\varepsilon_2\to0$, $\Delta u\to0$ and so $\varepsilon_1\to0$.
- Now, 
$$
\Delta y=\big(f'(g(x_0))+\varepsilon_1\big)\Delta u=\big(f'(g(x_0))+\varepsilon_1\big)\big(g'(x_0)+\varepsilon_2\big)\Delta x
$$
 
$$
=\big[f'(g(x_0))\cdot g'(x_0)+\varepsilon_2f'(g(x_0))+\varepsilon_1g'(x_0)+\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2\big]\Delta x
$$
 
$$
\implies\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=f'(g(x_0))\cdot g'(x_0)+\varepsilon_2f'(g(x_0))+\varepsilon_1g'(x_0)+\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2
$$
 
$$
\implies\lim_{\Delta x\to0}\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=f'(g(x_0))\cdot g'(x_0)
$$
 
$$
\implies\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=f'(g(x_0))\cdot g'(x_0)}\ \blacksquare
$$
### Derivative (vs) Differentials of Composite Functions

When we look at the definition of derivative and differential of composite functions, there is a difference in the restrictions on $g$:

1. **Derivative of $f(g(x))$:** the derivative of $f(g(x))$ with respect to $g(x)$ is defined as 
$$
\frac{d,(f(g(x)))}{d,g(x)}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(g(x)+h)-f(g(x))}{h}.
$$
 For the derivative to exist we need $g(x)$ to exist and be in the domain of $f$ where $f$ is differentiable.
2. **Differential of $f(g(x))$:** if we have $y=f(g(x))$ then the differential of $y$ is defined as 
$$
dy=f'(g(x)),dg.
$$
 And since $dg$ itself is not an independent variable, we know $dg=g'(x),dx$. Hence, for the differential of a composite function we need $g(x)$ to also be differentiable, along with the previous conditions.

### Some Missed Concepts

#### Absolute, Relative, and Percentage Change

1. **Absolute change:** $\Delta f=f(x_0+dx)-f(x_0)$ is the true value of absolute change, and its estimate is $df=f'(x_0),dx$.
2. **Relative change:** the true value of relative change is defined as $\dfrac{\Delta f}{f(x_0)}$ and its estimate is given by the formula $\dfrac{df}{f(x_0)}$.
3. **Percentage change:** true value $=\dfrac{\Delta f}{f(x_0)}\times100$ and its estimated value $=\dfrac{df}{f(x_0)}\times100$.

#### Sensitivity

The equation $df=f'(x),dx$ tells us how sensitive the output of $f$ is to a change in input at different values of $x$. The larger the value of $f'$ at $x$, the greater is the effect of a given change $dx$.

#### The Conversion of Mass to Energy

Newton's second law states that force is equal to the change in momentum: 
$$
F=\frac{d}{dt}(mv)=m\frac{dv}{dt}=ma
$$
- Here we assumed that the mass of an object remains constant, but that is not actually the case in reality.
- Einstein gave an equation for the mass of a body in motion: 
$$
m=\frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}}},\qquad m_0\text{ is the rest mass of the body (i.e. at }v=0).
$$
- In normal motion the velocity $v$ of a body is very small in comparison to the speed of light $c\implies\dfrac{v^2}{c^2}\approx0$.
- Hence, we can use the formula $(1+x)^k\approx1+kx$ at $x\approx0$ by choosing $x=-\dfrac{v^2}{c^2}$ & $k=-\dfrac12$: 
$$
m=m_0\left(1+\left(-\frac12\right)\left(-\frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)\right)=m_0+\frac12m_0v^2\left(\frac1{c^2}\right)
$$
 
$$
\implies(m-m_0)c^2=\frac12m_0v^2=\frac12m_0\left(v^2-0^2\right)\quad(\text{considering the object was at rest, i.e. }v=0)
$$
 
$$
\implies\boxed{\Delta m,c^2=\Delta K.E.}
$$
- Hence, a small change in mass can lead to a huge amount of energy release. In an atomic bomb, the $\Delta m$ before and after the reaction is $\approx1$ g (for a 20-kiloton atomic bomb), which is what is causing all that damage.

## 3.8 Newton's Method

- We know formulas to calculate the roots of cubic and quadratic equations. For a long time it was believed that such formulas would eventually be found for quintic and higher degree polynomials too.
- But Niels Henrik Abel proved that no such formula exists for quintic or higher degree polynomials. This was later proved more elegantly by Galois using group theory (not known by that name formally at that time).
- Since exact formulas don't exist for such polynomials, we turn to numerical methods to estimate the roots.
- One of these techniques is the **Newton–Raphson method**; the idea is to use linearisation iteratively to get closer and closer to the root of the polynomial.

**The process is as follows.**

1. Guess a first approximation to a root of the equation $f(x)=0$. A graph of $y=f(x)$ will help.
2. Use the first approximation to get a second, the second to get a third, and so on, using the formula 
$$
x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}\qquad(f'(x_n)\ne0)
$$
 where $f'(x_n)$ is the derivative of $f$ at $x_n$.

**Proof of the formula.**

- Given $x_n$, to get $x_{n+1}$ we find the equation of the tangent at $(x_n,f(x_n))$, which is 
$$
y-f(x_n)=f'(x_n)(x-x_n).
$$
- Where this tangent crosses the x-axis, that is the point which we call $x_{n+1}$, hence 
$$
(0-f(x_n))=f'(x_n)(x_{n+1}-x_n)\implies\boxed{x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}}
$$
**Geometric visualisation.** Geometrically this is shown in the diagram below; we see that as $n$ increases, $x_n$ gets closer and closer to the actual root.

> [!note] Notes
> 
> 1. Convergence is usually assured when using Newton's method for finding roots, but things can go wrong.
>     - If $f'(x)=0$ then the process stops and we need to start again with a different starting point.
>     - If the roots of $f(x)=0$ & $f'(x)=0$ coincide then the method will fail, but for this we can first find the roots of $f'(x)=0$ and check if they are also roots of $f(x)=0$.
>     - Sometimes the $x_n$ do not converge; instead they oscillate between two values forever. For example, consider the function 
$$
f(x)=\begin{cases}-\sqrt{r-x}, & x<r\ \sqrt{x-r}, & x\ge r\end{cases}
$$
 In this, if we start with $x_0=r-h$, then we get $x_1=r+h$, $x_2=x_0$, $x_3=x_1$, and so on.
> 2. For more information read pages 281, 282, 283 from the textbook.

Suppose that $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and that $c$ is an interior point of the interval. Show that if $f'(x)\le0$ on $[a,c)$ and $f'(x)\ge0$ on $(c,b]$, then $f(x)$ is never less than $f(c)$ on $[a,b]$, i.e. $f(x)\ge f(c)\ \forall x\in[a,b]$.

 $\implies f(x)\ge f(c)\ \ \forall x\in[a,b]$. Hence proved. $\blacksquare$

#### Exercise 17 — A max-min problem with a variable answer

Sometimes the solution of a max-min problem depends on the proportions of the shapes involved. As a case in point, suppose that a right circular cylinder of radius $r$ and height $h$ is inscribed in a right circular cone of radius $R$ and height $H$, as shown here. Find the value of $r$ (in terms of $R$ and $H$) that maximises the total surface area of the cylinder (including top and bottom).

> [!todo] FIGURE: Right triangle cross-section of the cone (base $R$, height $H$, base angle $\theta$) with an inscribed rectangle of width $r$ and height $h$ touching the slanted side.

**Sol.** Whatever $h$ we have, $r$ must be such that the top touches the cone, to maximise area for that height.

From the figure we see that 
$$
\tan\theta=\frac HR=\frac h{R-r}=\frac{H-h}r\impliesHR-Hr=hR\impliesh=\frac{H(R-r)}R
$$
 
$$
\text{Total surface area }A=2\pi r^2+2\pi rh=2\pi r^2+2\pi r\cdot\frac HR(R-r)\impliesA=2\pi r^2+\frac{2\pi H}R\left(Rr-r^2\right)
$$
 
$$
\implies\frac{dA}{dr}=4\pi r+\frac{2\pi H}R(R-2r)=0
$$
 
$$
\implies(4\pi R)r+2\pi HR-(4\pi H)r=0\impliesr=\frac{2\pi HR}{4\pi H-4\pi R}=\frac{HR}{2(H-R)}
$$
This assumes that $H\ne R$. If $H=R$, then $h=R-r$: 
$$
A=2\pi r^2+2\pi r(R-r)=(2\pi R),r,
$$
 so in this case max area is when $r$ is max, i.e. $r=R$ & $h=0$.

Going back to the case $H\ne R$, we have $\dfrac{dA}{dr}=0$ at $r=\dfrac{HR}{2(H-R)}$: 
$$
\frac{d^2A}{dr^2}=4\pi+\frac{2\pi H}R(-2)\implies\frac{d^2A}{dr^2}=\frac{4\pi(R-H)}R
$$
The domain of $r$ is $[0,R]$. From the formulas that we get from $\dfrac{dA}{dr}$ & $\dfrac{d^2A}{dr^2}$ we have the following cases.

**Case I: $H<R$.**

- $\dfrac{d^2A}{dr^2}>0$; the graph is a parabola that is concave up, the vertex $\dfrac{HR}{2(H-R)}<0$ is out of the domain, and the domain is to the right of the vertex where the graph is increasing.
- In this case we get max $A$ at the endpoint of the domain, $r=R$: $\ A=2\pi R^2$.

**Case II: $H>R$.**

- In this case the graph is concave down and the vertex will have the maximum $A$. The location of the vertex depends on the value of $H$; let us find the condition so that the vertex is inside the domain: 
$$
\frac{HR}{2(H-R)}\le R\impliesH\le2H-2R\implies\boxed{H\ge2R}
$$
- Hence, if $H\ge2R$ we have max area at $r=\dfrac{HR}{2(H-R)}$. 
$$
A=2\pi\left(1-\frac HR\right)r^2+2\pi Hr
$$
 
$$
=2\pi\left(1-\frac HR\right)\frac{H^2R^2}{4(H-R)^2}+2\pi H\left(\frac{HR}{2(H-R)}\right)
$$
 
$$
=2\pi\left(\frac{H^2R^2}{4(H-R)^2}-\frac{H^3R}{4(H-R)^2}+\frac{2H^2R(H-R)}{4(H-R)^2}\right)
$$
 
$$
=2\pi\left[\frac{H^2R^2-H^3R+2H^3R-2H^2R^2}{4(H-R)^2}\right]=\frac\pi2,\frac{H^3R-H^2R^2}{(H-R)^2}=\frac\pi2,\frac{H^2R(H-R)}{(H-R)^2}
$$
 
$$
\boxed{A=\frac{\pi H^2R}{2(H-R)}}
$$
**Case III: $R<H<2R$.**

- From the last case we know that in this case the vertex is outside the domain of $r$; the domain is to the left of it.
- Hence, since the graph is concave down, $A$ is increasing in the domain and attains its max value at $r=R$.

**Final verdict.** The final verdict from our analysis is that the max surface area and the corresponding value of $r$ depend on the values of $H$ & $R$:

- If $H<2R$, then $A_{\max}=2\pi R^2$ at $r=R$.
- If $H\ge2R$, then $A_{\max}=\dfrac{\pi H^2R}{2(H-R)}$ at $r=\dfrac{HR}{2(H-R)}$.

### Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem Proof

> [!abstract] Statement Every bounded sequence of real numbers has a convergent subsequence.

**Proof (Bisection Method).**

- Let $x_n$ be a sequence such that $x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n,\dots$ all lie inside the interval $[a,b]$.
- Divide the interval into two halves $\left[a,\frac{a+b}{2}\right]$ and $\left[\frac{a+b}{2},b\right]$. At least one of these halves needs to have infinitely many terms; let that be $I_1$.
- Similarly we divide $I_1$ into two halves such that at least one of them has infinitely many terms, and call that $I_2$.
- We keep repeating the process and get a nested set of intervals $I_1\supseteq I_2\supseteq I_3\supseteq I_4\supseteq\cdots$. The size of $I_k$ is $\dfrac{b-a}{2^k}$, i.e. it is shrinking exponentially.
- The **Nested Interval Theorem** (below) says that there will be exactly one number $L$ common to all of these intervals.
- Now we choose a number $x_{n_1}$ from $I_1$, then we choose a number $x_{n_2}$ from $I_2$ such that $n_2>n_1$. Notice that since $I_2$ has infinitely many terms, we will always find many candidates for $x_{n_2}$.
- We continue choosing $x_{n_3}$ from $I_3$, $x_{n_4}$ from $I_4$ and so on.
- The distance between $x_{n_k}$ and $L$ in $I_k$ satisfies the inequality 
$$
|x_{n_k}-L|<\frac{b-a}{2^k}.
$$
- Hence, as $k\to\infty$ we get $x_{n_k}\to L$, i.e. the subsequence $x_{n_1},x_{n_2},x_{n_3},\dots,x_{n_k},\dots$ converges to $L$. $\blacksquare$

### The Nested Interval Theorem

> [!abstract] Statement
> Let $I_n=[a_n,b_n]$ be a sequence of closed, bounded intervals such that they are nested:
> $$
> I_1\supseteq I_2\supseteq I_3\supseteq\cdots
> $$
> Then there is at least one real number $x$ such that $x\in I_n\ \forall n\in\mathbb{N}$. Furthermore, if the lengths of the intervals $(b_n-a_n)$ approach $0$ as $n\to\infty$, then $x$ is unique.

**Proof.**

- Consider the set of all $a$'s, i.e. $S={a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n,\dots}$. Here we have the relation $a_1\le a_2\le a_3\le\cdots$
- Also consider the set $T={b_1,b_2,b_3,\dots}$; in this we have the relation $b_1\ge b_2\ge b_3\ge\cdots$
- Now, each element of set $S$ is $\le$ every element of set $T$, that is $s_i\le t_j\ \ \forall s_i\in S,\ t_j\in T$.
- This gives us that the set $S$ is non-empty and bounded from above, and hence by the completeness axiom there is a supremum. Let $\sup S=x$; then 
$$
\begin{align}
a_i\le x\ \ \forall a_i\in S\quad&\quad x\le b_i\ \ \forall b_i\in T\ \ (\text{since each }b_i\text{ is an upper bound})
\end{align}
$$
- Hence, $x$ is in every interval $[a_n,b_n]$.
- **Uniqueness.** Suppose the lengths of the intervals approach $0$ and $x$ and $y$ are both in all of the intervals. Then $|x-y|\le b_n-a_n$ for every $n$, but $b_n-a_n\to0$, so $|x-y|=0$. Hence there can be only one element common to every interval.
- Hence, the theorem is proved. $\blacksquare$
