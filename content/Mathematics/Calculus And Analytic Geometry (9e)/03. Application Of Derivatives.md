# 3.1 Extreme Values of Functions

One of the crowning achievements of differential calculus is our ability to optimize functions—finding where a quantity reaches its highest peak or lowest valley. Before we develop the derivative tools to hunt down these peaks and valleys, we need to ask a more fundamental question: *when is a function guaranteed to have a highest and lowest value in the first place?*

## The Max-Min Theorem for Continuous Functions

Imagine drawing a continuous curve on a closed interval $[a, b]$ without lifting your pencil. Intuitively, because the interval has fixed, included endpoints and the curve never breaks or jumps to infinity, your pencil must reach some highest height $M$ and some lowest height $m$ somewhere along that path. This geometric guarantee is formalized by the Extreme Value Theorem.

> [!theorem] Theorem 1: The Max-Min Theorem (Extreme Value Theorem)
> If $f$ is continuous at every point of a closed interval $I = [a, b]$, then $f$ assumes both an absolute maximum value $M$ and an absolute minimum value $m$ somewhere in $I$. That is, there exist numbers $x_1$ and $x_2$ in $I$ with $f(x_1) = m$ and $f(x_2) = M$ such that
> $$
> m \le f(x) \le M
> $$
> for every $x$ in $I$.

**Proof:** We divide the proof into two natural stages. First, we show that a continuous function on $[a, b]$ cannot blow up to infinity—its values must be bounded. Second, we show that $f$ actually reaches its least upper bound ($M$) and greatest lower bound ($m$) at specific points inside $[a, b]$.

### Part I: $f$ is Bounded on $[a, b]$

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that $f$ is **not** bounded from above on $[a, b]$. What would that mean? It would mean that no matter how large an integer $n$ we pick, we can always find some input $x_n \in [a, b]$ whose output exceeds $n$, so that $f(x_n) > n$. Repeating this for $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ generates an infinite sequence of points
$$
x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n, \dots \quad \text{where } x_i \in [a, b]
$$
Because every term of this sequence is trapped inside the closed, finite interval $[a, b]$, the sequence $(x_n)$ is bounded. By the [Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem](#Bolzano–Weierstrass%20Theorem%20Proof), every bounded infinite sequence of real numbers has a convergent subsequence. Let $(x_{n_k})$ be a subsequence of $(x_n)$ that converges to some limit point $x_0$. Since every $x_{n_k}$ lies in the closed interval $[a, b]$, the limit $x_0$ must also belong to $[a, b]$.

Now, because $f$ is continuous at $x_0$, the function values along this subsequence must converge to $f(x_0)$:
$$
\lim_{k \to \infty} f(x_{n_k}) = \lim_{x \to x_0} f(x) = f(x_0)
$$
However, by our construction of the sequence, as $k \to \infty$, the index $n_k \to \infty$, which forces $f(x_{n_k}) > n_k \to \infty$. A sequence of real numbers cannot simultaneously approach the finite number $f(x_0)$ and diverge to $\infty$. This contradiction shows that our initial assumption was false; therefore, $f$ must be bounded from above on $[a, b]$. By an entirely symmetric argument, $f$ is also bounded from below on $[a, b]$.

### Part II: $f$ Attains Its Bounds in $[a, b]$

Since the set of values $\{f(x) : x \in [a, b]\}$ is non-empty and bounded from above, the completeness property of the real numbers guarantees that it has a least upper bound (supremum). Let us call this supremum $M$:
$$
M = \sup \{f(x) : x \in [a, b]\}
$$
We want to show that there is some point $d \in [a, b]$ where $f(d) = M$. Because $M$ is the *least* upper bound, nudging $M$ down by any tiny positive amount $1/n$ (for $n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$) produces a number $M - 1/n$ that is no longer an upper bound. Thus, for every positive integer $n$, there exists some point $x_n \in [a, b]$ whose function value falls strictly between $M - 1/n$ and $M$:
$$
M - \frac{1}{n} < f(x_n) \le M
$$
Once again, $(x_n)$ is a sequence trapped inside $[a, b]$, so the Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem hands us a convergent subsequence $(x_{n_k})$ that approaches some limit $d \in [a, b]$:
$$
\lim_{k \to \infty} n_k = \infty, \qquad \lim_{k \to \infty} x_{n_k} = d
$$
Evaluating our inequality along the subsequence $(x_{n_k})$, we have
$$
M - \frac{1}{n_k} < f(x_{n_k}) \le M
$$
As $k \to \infty$, the left-hand side $\lim_{k \to \infty} (M - 1/n_k) = M$, and the right-hand side is constantly $M$. By the Sandwich Theorem, $f(x_{n_k})$ is squeezed to $M$. Using the continuity of $f$ at $d$, we conclude:
$$
\lim_{k \to \infty} f(x_{n_k}) = \lim_{x \to d} f(x) = f(d) = M
$$
Thus, $f$ attains its absolute maximum value $M$ at the point $d \in [a, b]$. By applying the exact same reasoning to the greatest lower bound (infimum) $m = \inf \{f(x) : x \in [a, b]\}$, we find a point $c \in [a, b]$ where $f(c) = m$. Consequently,
$$
f(c) \le f(x) \le f(d) \quad \forall x \in [a, b]
$$
as required. $\blacksquare$

> [!note] Remarks on the Extreme Value Theorem
> 1. **Terminology:** Theorem 1 is widely known as the **Extreme Value Theorem**.
> 2. **Background Proof:** You can review the full proof of the [Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem](#Bolzano–Weierstrass%20Theorem%20Proof) at the end of these notes.
> 3. **Why Continuity and Closedness Matter:** Both hypotheses—that the interval is closed and that $f$ is continuous at *every* point—are essential. If a function fails to be continuous even at a single point, or if the interval is missing its endpoints, the function may fail to have a maximum or minimum value, as illustrated in the graphs below.

## Local vs. Absolute (Global) Extrema

When studying the terrain of a function's graph, we distinguish between the highest peak in the *entire domain* (an absolute or global maximum) and a point that is simply the highest peak in its *immediate neighborhood* (a local maximum). Let us state both concepts precisely.

> [!abstract] Definition: Absolute Extreme Values
> Let $f$ be a function with domain $D$. Then $f$ has an **absolute maximum** value on $D$ at a point $c$ if
> $$
> f(x) \le f(c) \quad \forall x \in D
> $$
> and an **absolute minimum** value on $D$ at $c$ if
> $$
> f(x) \ge f(c) \quad \forall x \in D
> $$

Absolute maximum and minimum values are collectively called **absolute extrema** (the plural of the Latin *extremum*) or **global extrema**. Notice how sensitive global extrema are to the choice of domain $D$: two functions with the exact same algebraic rule can have completely different extrema when restricted to different domains. For instance, the parabola $y = x^2$ on the entire real line $(-\infty, \infty)$ has no absolute maximum (since $x^2$ grows without bound) but has an absolute minimum value of $0$ at $x = 0$. However, that same rule $y = x^2$ restricted to the half-open interval $(0, 2]$ has no absolute minimum value (as $x \to 0^+$, $x^2$ gets arbitrarily close to $0$ without ever reaching it) and attains an absolute maximum value of $4$ at $x = 2$.

Now let us zoom in from the entire domain $D$ to small neighborhoods around a single point.

> [!abstract] Definition: Local Extreme Values
> A function $f$ has a **local maximum** value at an interior point $c$ of its domain if
> $$
> f(x) \le f(c) \quad \text{for all } x \text{ in some open interval containing } c
> $$
> A function $f$ has a **local minimum** value at an interior point $c$ of its domain if
> $$
> f(x) \ge f(c) \quad \text{for all } x \text{ in some open interval containing } c
> $$

> [!note] Extending Local Extrema to Endpoints
> 1. **Endpoint Local Extrema:** We can extend the definition of local extrema to the endpoints of a domain interval: $f$ has a local maximum (or minimum) at an endpoint $c$ if the corresponding inequality holds for all $x$ in some half-open interval in its domain containing $c$.
> 2. **Global Implies Local:** Because any inequality that holds across the *entire* domain $D$ automatically holds on a small sub-interval around $c$, every **absolute maximum is also a local maximum**, and every **absolute minimum is also a local minimum**. Thus, a complete list of all local extrema automatically contains the global extrema (if any exist).

## Finding Extrema

If a function is defined on an interval containing infinitely many points, how can we possibly check where its extreme values occur? Here is where derivatives come to our rescue. Imagine walking along a smooth curve: as you climb toward a peak, your slope is positive; as you descend on the other side, your slope is negative. Right at the top of a smooth interior peak (or the bottom of a smooth interior valley), the tangent line must level off horizontally!

> [!theorem] Theorem 2: The First Derivative Theorem for Local Extreme Values
> If $f$ has a local maximum or minimum value at an interior point $c$ of its domain, and if $f'$ is defined at $c$, then
> $$
> f'(c) = 0
> $$

**Proof:** We will show that at an interior local extremum $c$, the derivative $f'(c)$ cannot be positive and cannot be negative, leaving $f'(c) = 0$ as the only possibility.

Suppose $f$ has a local maximum at an interior point $c$. By definition, $f(x) \le f(c)$ for all $x$ in some open interval around $c$. Since $c$ is an interior point and $f'(c)$ is defined, both the right-hand and left-hand difference quotient limits must exist and must equal $f'(c)$:
$$
\text{(i)} \ \lim_{x \to c^+} \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} = f'(c), \qquad \text{(ii)} \ \lim_{x \to c^-} \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} = f'(c)
$$
Let us examine the signs of these two difference quotients for $x$ sufficiently close to $c$:
- For $x$ slightly to the right of $c$ ($x \to c^+$), we have $f(x) - f(c) \le 0$ and $x - c > 0$. Dividing a non-positive number by a positive number gives a non-positive quotient, so taking the limit yields:
  $$
  f'(c) = \lim_{x \to c^+} \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} \le 0
  $$
- For $x$ slightly to the left of $c$ ($x \to c^-$), we still have $f(x) - f(c) \le 0$, but now $x - c < 0$. Dividing a non-positive number by a negative number gives a non-negative quotient, so taking the limit yields:
  $$
  f'(c) = \lim_{x \to c^-} \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} \ge 0
  $$

Since $f'(c)$ is simultaneously $\le 0$ and $\ge 0$, we have $0 \le f'(c) \le 0$, which forces
$$
\lim_{x \to c^+} \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} = \lim_{x \to c^-} \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} = 0 \implies f'(c) = 0
$$
Thus, the derivative of a function (when defined) must vanish at any interior local maximum. The proof for an interior local minimum proceeds identically by reversing the inequality to $f(x) - f(c) \ge 0$. $\blacksquare$

> [!warning] Caution: The Converse of Theorem 2 Is False!
> Finding $f'(c) = 0$ at an interior point does **not** guarantee that $f$ has a local extremum there. For example, consider the cubic curve $f(x) = x^3$. Its derivative is $f'(x) = 3x^2$, which equals $0$ at $x = 0$: $f'(0) = 3(0)^2 = 0$. Yet the curve simply flattens out momentarily at the origin and keeps climbing—it has neither a local maximum nor a local minimum at $x = 0$.
>

What does Theorem 2 tell us about our search for extreme values? By logical contrapositive, if $c$ is an interior point where $f'(c)$ exists and $f'(c) \neq 0$, then $f$ *cannot* have a local extremum at $c$. This narrows down the entire infinite interval to just three possible hiding places for extrema:
1. Interior points where $f'(x) = 0$,
2. Interior points where $f'(x)$ is undefined,
3. Endpoints of the domain of $f$.

Because the first two types of interior points are so central to optimization, we give them a special name.

> [!abstract] Definition: Critical Point
> An interior point of the domain of a function $f$ where $f'$ is zero or undefined is a **critical point** of $f$.

With this terminology in hand, we can summarize the core takeaway of Theorem 2: **the only domain points where a function can assume extreme values are critical points and endpoints**.

> [!summary] Procedure: How to Find the Absolute Extrema of a Continuous Function on a Closed Interval
> To find the absolute maximum and minimum values of a continuous function $f$ on a closed interval $[a, b]$:
> 1. **Find all critical points** of $f$ in the open interval $(a, b)$ (usually there are only a few), and list them alongside the endpoints $a$ and $b$.
> 2. **Evaluate $f$** at all critical points and at both endpoints.
> 3. **Compare the values:** The largest of these outputs is the absolute maximum value, and the smallest is the absolute minimum value.

Let us put this three-step procedure to work on a function whose derivative is undefined at an interior point.

> [!example] Example: Finding Absolute Extrema on a Closed Interval
> Find the absolute maximum and minimum values of $h(x) = x^{2/3}$ on the interval $[-2, 3]$.

**Solution:** First, we differentiate $h(x)$ to locate any critical points inside $(-2, 3)$:
$$
h'(x) = \frac{2}{3}x^{-1/3} = \frac{2}{3x^{1/3}}
$$
Notice that $h'(x)$ is never zero, because the numerator is constantly $2$. However, $h'(x)$ is undefined at the interior point $x = 0$, making $x = 0$ the sole critical point of $h$.

Next, we evaluate $h(x)$ at the critical point $x = 0$ and at the two endpoints $x = -2$ and $x = 3$:
$$
h(0) = 0, \qquad h(-2) = (-2)^{2/3} = 4^{1/3}, \qquad h(3) = (3)^{2/3} = 9^{1/3}
$$
Comparing these three values, we see that $h$ attains its **absolute minimum value of $0$** at the interior critical point $x = 0$, and its **absolute maximum value of $9^{1/3}$** at the right endpoint $x = 3$. $\blacksquare$

> [!warning] Caution: An Undefined Derivative Does Not Guarantee an Extremum
> Just as $f'(c) = 0$ does not guarantee an extremum, an interior critical point where $f'(c)$ is undefined also does not automatically produce a local extremum. For example, consider $f(x) = x^{1/3}$. Its derivative
> $$
> f'(x) = \frac{1}{3}x^{-2/3} = \frac{1}{3x^{2/3}}
> $$
> is undefined at $x = 0$, so $x = 0$ is a critical point. Yet as the graph below shows, the curve passes vertically through the origin and keeps increasing from $-\infty$ to $\infty$, assuming no extreme value at $x = 0$.

## 3.1 Exercises

### Problem 30: Failure of Theorem 2 at Endpoints

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Why can't the conclusion of Theorem 2 ($f'(c) = 0$) be expected to hold if $c$ is an endpoint of the function's domain?

**Solution:** The proof of Theorem 2 relies on approaching $c$ from *both* the left ($x \to c^-$) and the right ($x \to c^+$) so that the two one-sided difference quotients have opposite signs, squeezing $f'(c)$ between $\le 0$ and $\ge 0$. At an endpoint of the domain, the function is only defined on one side of $c$, so only a single one-sided derivative exists. Without the opposing inequality from the other side to trap the slope at $0$, the one-sided derivative at an endpoint can take any real value (for instance, $f(x) = x$ on $[0, 1]$ has a minimum at $x = 0$ and a maximum at $x = 1$, yet its slope is $1$ at both endpoints). $\blacksquare$

### Problems 31 & 32: Symmetry of Local Extrema for Even and Odd Functions

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> If an even function $f$ has a local maximum value at $x = c$, can we say anything about the value of $f$ at $x = -c$? What if $f$ is an odd function instead? Give reasons for your answers.

**Solution:**
1. **Even Function ($f(-x) = f(x)$):** Since $f$ has a local maximum at $x = c$, there exists some open interval $(a, b)$ containing $c$ such that
   $$
   f(u) \le f(c) \quad \forall u \in (a, b)
   $$
   Now consider the reflected open interval $(-b, -a)$, which contains $-c$. For any $x \in (-b, -a)$, its negation $u = -x$ belongs to $(a, b)$. Using the even symmetry $f(x) = f(-x)$ and $f(c) = f(-c)$, we obtain
   $$
   f(x) = f(-x) \le f(c) = f(-c) \implies f(x) \le f(-c) \quad \forall x \in (-b, -a)
   $$
   Therefore, an even function also has a **local maximum value** at $x = -c$.

2. **Odd Function ($f(-x) = -f(x)$):** Again, for any $x \in (-b, -a)$, we have $-x \in (a, b)$, so $f(-x) \le f(c)$. Because $f$ is odd, $f(-x) = -f(x)$ and $f(c) = -f(-c)$. Substituting these into the inequality gives
   $$
   -f(x) \le -f(-c) \implies f(x) \ge f(-c) \quad \forall x \in (-b, -a)
   $$
   Multiplying by $-1$ reverses the inequality, showing that an odd function has a **local minimum value** at $x = -c$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 33: Functions Without Critical Points or Endpoints

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> We know how to find the extreme values of a continuous function $f(x)$ by investigating its values at critical points and endpoints. But what if there are no critical points or endpoints? What happens then? Do such functions really exist? Give reasons for your answer.

**Solution:** If a function has neither critical points nor endpoints in its domain, then **it cannot have any extreme values (local or absolute) on that domain**.

To see why, suppose $f$ did attain an extreme value at some point $c$ in its domain. Exhausting all logical possibilities for $c$:
1. $c$ is an endpoint of the domain.
2. $c$ is an interior point and $f'(c)$ exists—in which case Theorem 2 forces $f'(c) = 0$, making $c$ a critical point.
3. $c$ is an interior point and $f'(c)$ does not exist—which by definition also makes $c$ a critical point.

Thus, every extremum must occur at either an endpoint or a critical point; if neither exists, no extremum can exist.

Such functions certainly do exist! A simple example is the straight line $f(x) = x$ defined on the open domain $(-\infty, \infty)$. Because the domain is all real numbers, there are no endpoints; and because $f'(x) = 1 \neq 0$ everywhere, there are no critical points. As expected, $f(x) = x$ rises steadily from $-\infty$ to $\infty$ without ever reaching a maximum or minimum value. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 34: An Endpoint Without a Local Extremum

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Give an example of a function defined on $[0, 1]$ that has neither a local maximum nor a local minimum value at $x = 0$.

**Solution:** Consider the oscillating function defined on $[0, 1]$ by
$$
f(x) = \begin{cases}
x \sin\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right), & \text{if } 0 < x \le 1 \\[6pt]
0, & \text{if } x = 0
\end{cases}
$$
At the left endpoint $x = 0$, the function has the value $f(0) = 0$. However, in every half-open interval $[0, \delta)$ (no matter how small $\delta > 0$ is), $\sin(1/x)$ oscillates infinitely often between $+1$ and $-1$. Thus, every neighborhood of $x = 0$ contains points where $f(x) > 0 = f(0)$ as well as points where $f(x) < 0 = f(0)$. Because $f(0)$ is neither the largest nor the smallest value in any interval $[0, \delta)$, $f$ has neither a local maximum nor a local minimum at $x = 0$. $\blacksquare$

# 3.2 The Mean Value Theorem

If you drive 60 miles in exactly one hour, your *average* velocity for the trip is $60\text{ mph}$. Does your speedometer have to read *exactly* $60\text{ mph}$ at least once during the trip? Intuitively, yes: you cannot average $60\text{ mph}$ if you drive slower than $60\text{ mph}$ the whole time, nor if you drive faster than $60\text{ mph}$ the whole time, and since velocity changes continuously, you must pass through $60\text{ mph}$ at some instant. This bridge between *average rate of change* over an interval and *instantaneous rate of change* at an interior point is the **Mean Value Theorem**—arguably the most important theoretical workhorse in differential calculus.

## Rolle's Theorem

To prove the Mean Value Theorem, we first establish a special horizontal version discovered by Michel Rolle: if a smooth curve starts and ends at height $0$ on a closed interval $[a, b]$, then somewhere strictly between $a$ and $b$, the curve must have a horizontal tangent.

> [!theorem] Theorem 3: Rolle's Theorem
> Suppose that $y = f(x)$ is continuous at every point of the closed interval $[a, b]$ and differentiable at every point of its interior $(a, b)$. If
> $$
> f(a) = f(b) = 0
> $$
> then there is at least one number $c$ in $(a, b)$ at which
> $$
> f'(c) = 0
> $$

**Proof:** Because $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$, the Extreme Value Theorem (Theorem 1) guarantees that $f$ attains both an absolute maximum value $M$ and an absolute minimum value $m$ somewhere on $[a, b]$. By Theorem 2, these extreme values can occur in only three places:
1. At an endpoint ($a$ or $b$),
2. At an interior point $c \in (a, b)$ where $f'(c) = 0$,
3. At an interior point where $f'$ is undefined.

However, the hypothesis of Rolle's Theorem states that $f$ is differentiable at *every* point of $(a, b)$, which completely rules out Option 3. We are left with two cases:
- **Case 1:** Either the maximum $M$ or the minimum $m$ occurs at some interior point $c \in (a, b)$. Then by Theorem 2, $f'(c) = 0$, and we have found our point $c$.
- **Case 2:** Both the absolute maximum $M$ and the absolute minimum $m$ occur at the endpoints $a$ and $b$. Since $f(a) = f(b) = 0$, this forces $m = M = 0$. In other words, $0 \le f(x) \le 0$ for all $x \in [a, b]$, meaning $f(x) = 0$ is constant throughout $[a, b]$. Then $f'(c) = 0$ at *every* interior point $c \in (a, b)$.

In either case, there is at least one interior point $c \in (a, b)$ where $f'(c) = 0$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note] Geometric Meaning of Rolle's Theorem
> Geometrically, Rolle's Theorem guarantees that any continuous curve on $[a, b]$ that is smooth on $(a, b)$ and crosses (or touches) the $x$-axis at both endpoints must have at least one horizontal tangent line over the interior $(a, b)$. (In fact, the exact same proof works whenever $f(a) = f(b)$, even if that common height is not $0$!)

## The Mean Value Theorem (MVT)

Now imagine tilting the horizontal chord of Rolle's Theorem so that the endpoints $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$ sit at arbitrary heights. The Mean Value Theorem states that there is still at least one interior point $c \in (a, b)$ where the tangent line to the curve is **parallel to the secant line** joining the endpoints.

> [!theorem] Theorem 4: The Mean Value Theorem
> Suppose $y = f(x)$ is continuous on a closed interval $[a, b]$ and differentiable on the interval's interior $(a, b)$. Then there is at least one point $c$ in $(a, b)$ at which
> $$
> \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a} = f'(c)
> $$

**Proof:** How can we reduce this tilted picture to Rolle's Theorem? We measure the vertical distance between the curve $y = f(x)$ and the secant line joining $(a, f(a))$ to $(b, f(b))$!

The secant line passes through $(a, f(a))$ with slope $\dfrac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$, so by the point-slope formula its equation is
$$
g(x) = f(a) + \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}(x - a)
$$
Now define the vertical difference function $h(x) = f(x) - g(x)$, which measures how far the curve $f(x)$ sits above or below the secant line $g(x)$ at any point $x$:
$$
h(x) = f(x) - f(a) - \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}(x - a)
$$
Notice that $h(x)$ satisfies all three hypotheses of Rolle's Theorem on $[a, b]$:
1. $h$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ because both $f$ and the linear function $g$ are continuous on $[a, b]$.
2. $h$ is differentiable on $(a, b)$ because both $f$ and $g$ are differentiable on $(a, b)$, with derivative $h'(x) = f'(x) - g'(x) = f'(x) - \dfrac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$.
3. At both endpoints, the curve and the secant line meet, so $h(a) = 0$ and $h(b) = 0$.

Applying Rolle's Theorem to $h(x)$, there must exist at least one point $c \in (a, b)$ where $h'(c) = 0$. Substituting into our expression for $h'(c)$, we obtain
$$
h'(c) = 0 \implies f'(c) - g'(c) = 0 \implies \boxed{f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}}
$$
which completes the proof. $\blacksquare$

> [!note] Remarks on the Mean Value Theorem
> 1. **Existence vs. Construction:** Like the Extreme Value Theorem, the MVT is an *existence theorem*—it guarantees that at least one such point $c \in (a, b)$ exists, without giving a general formula for $c$. For specific functions, however, we can often find $c$ explicitly by solving the algebraic equation $f'(c) = \dfrac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$.
> 2. **Physical Interpretation:** If $f(t)$ represents the position of a moving object at time $t$, then the difference quotient $\dfrac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$ is the **average velocity** (or average rate of change) over the time interval $[a, b]$, while $f'(c)$ is the **instantaneous velocity** (instantaneous rate of change) at time $t = c$. The MVT states that at some instant during the trip, the instantaneous velocity must equal the average velocity.

## Mathematical Consequences (Corollaries of the MVT)

Why do mathematicians care so deeply about the Mean Value Theorem? Because it lets us deduce global properties of a function $f$ across an entire interval just from knowing its derivative $f'$! Let us prove three foundational corollaries that pave the way for integral calculus.

### Corollary 1: Functions with Zero Derivative Are Constant

We already know that if a function is constant, its derivative is zero everywhere. What about the converse? Could a non-constant function somehow have $f'(x) = 0$ at every point of an interval? The Mean Value Theorem says no.

> [!theorem] Corollary 1: Zero Derivative Implies Constant Function
> If $f'(x) = 0$ at each point of an interval $I$, then $f(x) = C$ for all $x$ in $I$, where $C$ is a constant.

**Proof:** Pick any two distinct points $x_1$ and $x_2$ in the interval $I$ with $x_1 < x_2$. Since $f$ is differentiable at every point of $I$, it is automatically continuous on the closed sub-interval $[x_1, x_2]$ and differentiable on the open sub-interval $(x_1, x_2)$.

By the Mean Value Theorem, there exists some point $c \in (x_1, x_2)$ such that
$$
\frac{f(x_2) - f(x_1)}{x_2 - x_1} = f'(c)
$$
Because $c$ lies in $I$ and $f'$ is zero throughout $I$, we have $f'(c) = 0$. Therefore,
$$
\frac{f(x_2) - f(x_1)}{x_2 - x_1} = 0 \implies f(x_2) - f(x_1) = 0 \implies f(x_2) = f(x_1)
$$
Since $x_1$ and $x_2$ were arbitrary points in $I$, $f$ takes the exact same value $C$ at every point of $I$. $\blacksquare$

### Corollary 2: Functions with the Same Derivative Differ by a Constant

If two functions have the exact same rate of change at every point of an interval, how can their graphs be related? Corollary 1 immediately implies that one graph must simply be a vertical shift of the other.

> [!theorem] Corollary 2: Identical Derivatives Imply Constant Difference
> If $f'(x) = g'(x)$ at each point of an interval $I$, then there exists a constant $C$ such that $f(x) = g(x) + C$ for all $x$ in $I$.

**Proof:** Define the difference function $h(x) = f(x) - g(x)$ on the interval $I$. Differentiating $h(x)$ at any point $x \in I$ gives
$$
h'(x) = f'(x) - g'(x) = 0 \quad \forall x \in I
$$
Since $h'(x) = 0$ throughout the interval $I$, Corollary 1 tells us that $h(x) = C$ for some constant $C$ on $I$. Thus,
$$
f(x) - g(x) = C \implies f(x) = g(x) + C \quad \forall x \in I
$$
as claimed. $\blacksquare$

### Application: Finding Velocity and Position from Acceleration

Corollary 2 allows us to work backward from derivatives to recover original functions—a process that forms the heart of differential equations and integration. Let us see how this works for a free-falling body near Earth's surface.

> [!example] Example: Recovering Velocity and Position from Constant Gravity
> Suppose a body falls from rest ($v(0) = 0$ and $s(0) = 0$) with a constant downward acceleration of $9.8\text{ m/s}^2$. Find its velocity $v(t)$ and position $s(t)$ as functions of time $t$.

**Solution:**
1. **Finding Velocity $v(t)$:** Since acceleration is the derivative of velocity, we know that $v'(t) = 9.8$. We also know by inspection that the function $g(t) = 9.8t$ has the exact same derivative, $g'(t) = 9.8$. By Corollary 2, $v(t)$ and $g(t)$ can differ only by a constant $C_1$:
   $$
   v(t) = g(t) + C_1 = 9.8t + C_1
   $$
   Using the initial condition that the body starts from rest at $t = 0$ ($v(0) = 0$), we find $9.8(0) + C_1 = 0 \implies C_1 = 0$. Therefore,
   $$
   \boxed{v(t) = 9.8t}
   $$
2. **Finding Position $s(t)$:** Since velocity is the derivative of position, we now have $s'(t) = v(t) = 9.8t$. By inspection, the quadratic function $h(t) = 4.9t^2$ also has the derivative $h'(t) = 9.8t$. Applying Corollary 2 a second time gives
   $$
   s(t) = h(t) + C_2 = 4.9t^2 + C_2
   $$
   Using the initial position $s(0) = 0$ at $t = 0$, we obtain $4.9(0)^2 + C_2 = 0 \implies C_2 = 0$, which yields
   $$
   \boxed{s(t) = 4.9t^2}
   $$
   Thus, Corollary 2 guarantees that these are the *only* possible velocity and position functions for the falling body. $\blacksquare$

## Increasing Functions and Decreasing Functions

Intuitively, a positive slope means a graph is rising from left to right, and a negative slope means it is falling. Using the Mean Value Theorem, we can turn this geometric intuition into a rigorous theorem.

> [!theorem] Corollary 3: The First Derivative Test for Increasing and Decreasing Functions
> Suppose that $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$.
> - If $f' > 0$ at each point of $(a, b)$, then $f$ increases on $[a, b]$.
> - If $f' < 0$ at each point of $(a, b)$, then $f$ decreases on $[a, b]$.

**Proof:** Let $x_1$ and $x_2$ be any two points in $[a, b]$ with $x_1 < x_2$. Because $f$ is continuous on $[x_1, x_2]$ and differentiable on $(x_1, x_2)$, the Mean Value Theorem guarantees that there exists some point $c \in (x_1, x_2)$ such that
$$
\frac{f(x_2) - f(x_1)}{x_2 - x_1} = f'(c) \implies f(x_2) - f(x_1) = f'(c)(x_2 - x_1)
$$
Since $x_1 < x_2$, the factor $(x_2 - x_1)$ is strictly positive. Therefore, the sign of $f(x_2) - f(x_1)$ is completely determined by the sign of $f'(c)$:
- If $f' > 0$ throughout $(a, b)$, then $f'(c) > 0$, so $f(x_2) - f(x_1) > 0 \implies f(x_2) > f(x_1)$, meaning $f$ is **increasing** on $[a, b]$.
- If $f' < 0$ throughout $(a, b)$, then $f'(c) < 0$, so $f(x_2) - f(x_1) < 0 \implies f(x_2) < f(x_1)$, meaning $f$ is **decreasing** on $[a, b]$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note] Local Interpretation
> If $f'(x) > 0$ (or $f'(x) < 0$) throughout some open neighborhood around a point $x$, then $f$ is increasing (or decreasing) on that open interval.

## Theoretical Subtleties of the MVT and Differentiability

Let us pause to explore three rich conceptual questions about how derivatives and the Mean Value Theorem behave at the boundaries of their hypotheses.

### 1. Why Does the MVT Require Differentiability Only on the Interior $(a, b)$?

Notice that the Mean Value Theorem requires $f$ to be continuous on the *closed* interval $[a, b]$, but differentiable only on the *open* interior $(a, b)$. Why don't we require $f$ to be differentiable on the closed interval $[a, b]$?

Mathematical theorems always strive for the **weakest possible hypotheses that yield the strongest possible conclusion**. If a function happens to be differentiable at the endpoints too, the MVT still applies—endpoint differentiability is simply not *necessary* because the point $c$ guaranteed by the theorem always lives strictly inside $(a, b)$.

By requiring differentiability only on $(a, b)$, we allow the MVT to apply to important families of functions that are continuous on $[a, b]$ yet fail to be differentiable at one or both endpoints:
1. **Vertical Tangents at Endpoints:** The upper semicircle $y = \sqrt{1 - x^2}$ is continuous on $[-1, 1]$ and differentiable on $(-1, 1)$, but at the endpoints $x = \pm 1$ its tangent lines become vertical ($y' \to \mp\infty$).
2. **Infinite Oscillation at an Endpoint:** Consider the damped oscillating function on $[0, 1]$:
   $$
   y = \begin{cases}
   x \sin\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right), & x \neq 0 \\[6pt]
   0, & x = 0
   \end{cases}
   $$
   This function is continuous at $x = 0$ (since $|x \sin(1/x)| \le |x| \to 0$) and differentiable at every interior point $x \in (0, 1)$, but at $x = 0$ the difference quotient $\dfrac{y(x) - y(0)}{x - 0} = \sin\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right)$ oscillates forever between $-1$ and $1$ without converging.

Because the MVT only needs derivatives in the interior $(a, b)$, both of these functions still obey the Mean Value Theorem on their closed intervals!

### 2. The Derivative at a Point vs. the Limit of the Derivative

It is easy to confuse two subtly different limits near a point $x_0$:
1. **The derivative at $x_0$**, $f'(x_0)$, which is the limit of the **slope of the secant line** $PQ$ connecting $P(x_0, f(x_0))$ to a nearby point $Q(x_0 + h, f(x_0 + h))$ (left figure below):
   $$
   f'(x_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h}
   $$
2. **The limit of the derivative as $x \to x_0$**, $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to x_0} f'(x)$, which is the limiting value of the **slope of the tangent line** at points $x$ approaching $x_0$ (right figure below).

![](Pasted%20image%2020260922114848.webp)

For $f$ to be differentiable at $x_0$, **only the first limit ($f'(x_0)$) needs to exist**. The second limit ($\lim_{x \to x_0} f'(x)$) may not exist at all! Let us see a classic example of this phenomenon.

> [!example] Example: A Differentiable Function Whose Derivative Is Discontinuous
> Show that the function
> $$
> y = f(x) = \begin{cases}
> x^2 \sin\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right), & x \neq 0 \\[6pt]
> 0, & x = 0
> \end{cases}
> $$
> is differentiable at $x = 0$, even though $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} f'(x)$ does not exist.

**Solution:** First, let us compute $f'(0)$ directly from the limit definition of the derivative (the limiting slope of secants through the origin):
$$
\left. y' \right|_{x=0} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{f(x) - f(0)}{x - 0} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x^2 \sin\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right) - 0}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} x \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) = 0
$$
Because this limit exists and equals $0$, $f$ is differentiable at $x = 0$ with $f'(0) = 0$.

Now let us compute $f'(x)$ for $x \neq 0$ using the product and chain rules:
$$
y' = \begin{cases}
2x \sin\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right) - \cos\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right), & x \neq 0 \\[6pt]
0, & x = 0
\end{cases}
$$
As $x \to 0$, the first term $2x \sin(1/x) \to 0$, but the second term $-\cos(1/x)$ oscillates wildly between $-1$ and $+1$ without approaching any single number. Thus, $\lim_{x \to 0} y'$ **does not exist**, even though $y'(0) = 0$ is perfectly well-defined!

Geometrically, because the wave $x^2 \sin(1/x)$ is pinched between the parabolas $y = x^2$ and $y = -x^2$ (both of which are flat at the origin), any secant line drawn from $(0, 0)$ to a nearby point on the curve gets squeezed toward slope $0$. Yet as you ride along the rapid ripples near the origin, the tangent line to the ripples keeps tilting up and down between slopes $-1$ and $+1$. $\blacksquare$

![](Screenshot%20from%202026-09-22%2011-55-14.webp)![](Screenshot%20from%202026-09-22%2011-57-38.webp)

> [!note] When Do the Two Limits Agree?
> If the derivative function $f'$ is itself continuous at $c$, then $\lim_{x \to c} f'(x) = f'(c)$, meaning the limiting slope of the tangent lines matches the limiting slope of the secant lines. Even better, as our next theorem shows, if $\lim_{x \to c} f'(x)$ exists for a continuous function $f$, then $f'(c)$ is *guaranteed* to exist and equal that limit!

### 3. The Limit of the Derivative Theorem

When checking whether a piecewise function is differentiable at a join point $x = c$, do we always have to compute the difference quotient limit $\lim_{x \to c} \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c}$ from scratch, or can we simply take the left- and right-hand limits of the derivative formulas on either side of $c$? Thanks to the Mean Value Theorem, we can do the latter!

> [!theorem] Theorem: The Limit of the Derivative Theorem
> Let $f$ be a function that is:
> 1. continuous at a point $c$, and
> 2. differentiable on an open interval containing $c$ (except possibly at $c$ itself).
> 
> If the limit $L = \displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} f'(x)$ exists and is finite, then $f$ is differentiable at $c$ and $f'(c) = L$.

**Proof:** Let $(c - \delta, c + \delta)$ be the open interval around $c$ on which $f$ is differentiable (except possibly at $c$). For any point $x \in (c - \delta, c + \delta)$ with $x \neq c$, consider the closed interval $I$ between $x$ and $c$ (that is, $[x, c]$ if $x < c$, or $[c, x]$ if $x > c$).

Because $f$ is continuous at $c$ and differentiable at every other point of $I$, $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $I$ and differentiable on its interior. By the Mean Value Theorem, there exists a point $y$ strictly between $x$ and $c$ such that
$$
\frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} = f'(y)
$$
Now let $x \to c$. Since $y$ is trapped between $x$ and $c$, $y$ is forced to approach $c$ as well ($y \to c$ with $y \neq c$). Taking the limit as $x \to c$, we obtain
$$
f'(c) = \lim_{x \to c} \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} = \lim_{y \to c} f'(y) = L
$$
Thus, $f$ is differentiable at $c$ and $f'(c) = L$. $\blacksquare$

Let us see how much algebraic effort this theorem saves when piecing functions together smoothly.

> [!example] Example: Satisfying the MVT Hypotheses for a Piecewise Function
> For what values of $a$, $m$, and $b$ does the function
> $$
> f(x) = \begin{cases}
> 3, & x = 0 \\
> -x^2 + 3x + a, & 0 < x < 1 \\
> mx + b, & 1 \le x \le 2
> \end{cases}
> $$
> satisfy the hypotheses of the Mean Value Theorem on the interval $[0, 2]$?

**Solution:** For $f$ to satisfy the hypotheses of the MVT on $[0, 2]$, it must be **continuous on $[0, 2]$** and **differentiable on $(0, 2)$**. Since the pieces $-x^2 + 3x + a$ and $mx + b$ are polynomials, $f$ is automatically continuous and differentiable everywhere except possibly at the join points $x = 0$ and $x = 1$.

1. **Continuity at the Left Endpoint $x = 0$:** We need the right-hand limit as $x \to 0^+$ to match $f(0) = 3$:
   $$
   \lim_{x \to 0^+} f(x) = \lim_{x \to 0^+} (-x^2 + 3x + a) = a = f(0) = 3 \implies \boxed{a = 3}
   $$
2. **Continuity at the Interior Join Point $x = 1$:** With $a = 3$, the left-hand and right-hand limits at $x = 1$ must agree:
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x) &= -(1)^2 + 3(1) + a = 2 + 3 = 5 \\
   \lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x) &= m(1) + b = m + b \\
   \implies m + b &= 5
   \end{aligned}
   $$
3. **Differentiability at the Interior Join Point $x = 1$:** By the Limit of the Derivative Theorem, since $f$ is continuous at $x = 1$ and polynomial on either side, $f$ is differentiable at $x = 1$ if and only if the left- and right-hand limits of $f'(x)$ at $x = 1$ are equal:
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \lim_{x \to 1^-} f'(x) &= \lim_{x \to 1^-} (-2x + 3) = 1 \\
   \lim_{x \to 1^+} f'(x) &= \lim_{x \to 1^+} m = m
   \end{aligned}
   $$
   Equating these two slopes gives $\boxed{m = 1}$, and substituting $m = 1$ into $m + b = 5$ gives $b = 5 - 1 \implies \boxed{b = 4}$.

Therefore, $f(x)$ satisfies the hypotheses of the Mean Value Theorem on $[0, 2]$ when $a = 3$, $m = 1$, and $b = 4$. $\blacksquare$

## 3.2 Exercises

### Problem 12: Zeros of Higher Derivatives via Rolle's Theorem

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Suppose that $f''$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and that $f$ has three distinct zeros in the interval. Show that $f''$ has at least one zero in $(a, b)$. Generalize this result.

**Solution:** First, let us trace the chain of smoothness guaranteed by the hypothesis: since $f''$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, $f'$ is differentiable (and therefore continuous) on $[a, b]$, which in turn implies that $f$ is differentiable (and therefore continuous) on $[a, b]$.

Now suppose $f$ has three distinct roots $x_1 < x_2 < x_3$ in $[a, b]$, so that
$$
f(x_1) = f(x_2) = f(x_3) = 0
$$
Applying Rolle's Theorem to $f$ on the two adjacent closed sub-intervals $[x_1, x_2]$ and $[x_2, x_3]$, we obtain:
- A point $d_1 \in (x_1, x_2)$ where $f'(d_1) = 0$, and
- A point $d_2 \in (x_2, x_3)$ where $f'(d_2) = 0$.

Since $x_1 < d_1 < x_2 < d_2 < x_3$, we have $d_1 < d_2$. Now apply Rolle's Theorem a second time, this time to the derivative function $f'$ on the closed interval $[d_1, d_2] \subset (a, b)$. Since $f'$ is continuous on $[d_1, d_2]$, differentiable on $(d_1, d_2)$, and satisfies $f'(d_1) = f'(d_2) = 0$, there exists at least one point $c \in (d_1, d_2) \subset (a, b)$ such that $f''(c) = 0$.

**Generalization:** By repeating this argument inductively, if $f$ is sufficiently smooth and has $n$ distinct roots in $[a, b]$, then $f'$ has at least $n - 1$ distinct roots in $(a, b)$, $f''$ has at least $n - 2$ distinct roots, and in general the $k$-th derivative $f^{(k)}$ (for $k < n$) has at least $n - k$ distinct roots in $(a, b)$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 13: Uniqueness of Zeros of $f'$ When $f''$ Has Constant Sign

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Show that if $f'' > 0$ throughout an interval $[a, b]$, then $f'$ has at most one zero in $[a, b]$. What if $f'' < 0$ throughout $[a, b]$ instead?

**Solution:** We can prove this in two equivalent ways:
1. **By Monotonicity (Corollary 3):** Since $f'' = (f')' > 0$ throughout $[a, b]$, Corollary 3 tells us that $f'$ is strictly increasing on $[a, b]$. That is, for any $x_1 < x_2$ in $[a, b]$, we have $f'(x_1) < f'(x_2)$. A strictly increasing function can cross $0$ at most once.
2. **By Rolle's Theorem (Contradiction):** Suppose $f'$ had two distinct zeros $x_1 < x_2$ in $[a, b]$, so $f'(x_1) = f'(x_2) = 0$. Applying Rolle's Theorem to $f'$ on $[x_1, x_2]$ would produce a point $c \in (x_1, x_2)$ where $f''(c) = 0$, directly contradicting the hypothesis that $f'' > 0$ everywhere on $[a, b]$.

By the exact same reasoning, if $f'' < 0$ throughout $[a, b]$, then $f'$ is strictly decreasing on $[a, b]$ and likewise can have **at most one zero** in $[a, b]$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 17: Non-Zero Derivative Implies Distinct Endpoint Values

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Suppose that $f$ is differentiable on $[0, 1]$ and that its derivative is never zero. Show that $f(0) \neq f(1)$.

**Solution:** We can see this directly from Rolle's Theorem (or the Mean Value Theorem): if $f(0)$ were equal to $f(1)$, then by the Mean Value Theorem on $[0, 1]$ there would exist some $c \in (0, 1)$ where
$$
f'(c) = \frac{f(1) - f(0)}{1 - 0} = 0
$$
contradicting the hypothesis that $f'$ is never zero on $[0, 1]$. (Equivalently, by the Intermediate Value Property for derivatives, since $f'$ is never zero on $[0, 1]$, $f'$ must be either strictly positive everywhere or strictly negative everywhere on $[0, 1]$, meaning $f$ is strictly increasing or strictly decreasing on $[0, 1]$, and hence $f(0) \neq f(1)$.) $\blacksquare$

### Problem 18: Lipschitz Inequality for the Sine Function

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Show that $|\sin b - \sin a| \le |b - a|$ for any $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$.

**Solution:** Without loss of generality, assume $b \ge a$ (if $a = b$, both sides are $0$). Let us compare $\sin x$ with $x$ by constructing two auxiliary functions and examining their derivatives via Corollary 3:
1. Consider $y_1(x) = \sin x + x$. Differentiating gives
   $$
   y_1'(x) = \cos x + 1 \ge 0 \quad \forall x \in \mathbb{R}
   $$
   Because $y_1'(x) \ge 0$, $y_1(x)$ is non-decreasing, so for $b \ge a$ we have
   $$
   \sin a + a \le \sin b + b \implies -(b - a) \le \sin b - \sin a
   $$
2. Similarly, consider $y_2(x) = \sin x - x$. Differentiating gives
   $$
   y_2'(x) = \cos x - 1 \le 0 \quad \forall x \in \mathbb{R}
   $$
   Because $y_2'(x) \le 0$, $y_2(x)$ is non-increasing, so for $b \ge a$ we have
   $$
   \sin a - a \ge \sin b - b \implies \sin b - \sin a \le b - a
   $$

Combining both inequalities yields
$$
-(b - a) \le \sin b - \sin a \le b - a \implies \boxed{|\sin b - \sin a| \le |b - a|}
$$
for all $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 20: Parallel Tangents Between Intersecting Curves

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Suppose that $f$ and $g$ are differentiable on $[a, b]$ and that $f(a) = g(a)$ and $f(b) = g(b)$. Show that there is at least one point between $a$ and $b$ where the tangents to the graphs of $f$ and $g$ are parallel.

**Solution:** Consider the vertical separation function $h(x) = f(x) - g(x)$ on $[a, b]$. Since $f$ and $g$ are differentiable on $[a, b]$, $h$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$. Furthermore, at the endpoints we have
$$
h(a) = f(a) - g(a) = 0, \qquad h(b) = f(b) - g(b) = 0
$$
By Rolle's Theorem, there exists at least one point $c \in (a, b)$ where $h'(c) = 0$. Since $h'(c) = f'(c) - g'(c)$, this gives
$$
f'(c) - g'(c) = 0 \implies f'(c) = g'(c)
$$
Because the two derivatives are equal at $x = c$, the tangent lines to the graphs of $f$ and $g$ at $x = c$ have the exact same slope and are therefore parallel. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 21: Exact Midpoint Property of the MVT for Quadratic Functions

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Let $f(x) = px^2 + qx + r$ ($p \neq 0$) be a quadratic function defined on a closed interval $[a, b]$. Show that there is **exactly one** point $c$ in $(a, b)$ at which $f$ satisfies the conclusion of the Mean Value Theorem.

**Solution:** Let us compute the secant slope of $f(x) = px^2 + qx + r$ across $[a, b]$ and set it equal to the derivative $f'(c) = 2pc + q$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a} &= \frac{(pb^2 + qb + r) - (pa^2 + qa + r)}{b - a} \\
  &= \frac{p(b^2 - a^2) + q(b - a)}{b - a} \\
  &= p(b + a) + q
\end{aligned}
$$
Equating $f'(c) = 2pc + q$ to this average rate of change gives
$$
2pc + q = p(b + a) + q \implies 2pc = p(b + a) \implies c = \frac{a + b}{2}
$$
Because $c = \dfrac{a + b}{2}$ is the unique solution to this equation and the midpoint of $[a, b]$ always lies strictly inside $(a, b)$, there is **exactly one** point $c \in (a, b)$ satisfying the conclusion of the Mean Value Theorem for any parabola! $\blacksquare$

### Problem 24: Functions Starting Together with Equal Rates of Change

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> If the graphs of two functions $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ start at the same point in the plane and the functions have the same rate of change at every point, do the graphs have to be identical? Give reasons for your answer.

**Solution:** Yes, their graphs must be identical. Suppose $f$ and $g$ are defined on an interval $I$ starting at $x = a$, so that $f(a) = g(a)$ (they start at the same point) and $f'(x) = g'(x)$ for all $x \in I$ (they have the same rate of change everywhere).

Let $h(x) = f(x) - g(x)$. Since $h'(x) = f'(x) - g'(x) = 0$ for all $x \in I$, Corollary 1 implies that $h(x) = C$ is constant on $I$. Evaluating at the starting point $x = a$ gives
$$
C = h(a) = f(a) - g(a) = 0
$$
Therefore, $h(x) = f(x) - g(x) = 0$ at every point $x \in I$, meaning $f(x) = g(x)$ throughout the entire interval. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 26: Bounding the Average Rate of Change Between $\min f'$ and $\max f'$

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Let $f$ be a function defined on an interval $[a, b]$. What conditions could you place on $f$ to guarantee that
> $$
> \min f' \le \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a} \le \max f'
> $$
> where $\min f'$ and $\max f'$ refer to the minimum and maximum values of $f'$ on $[a, b]$? Give reasons for your answer.

**Solution:** To guarantee this inequality, we need two things: first, $\min f'$ and $\max f'$ must actually exist on $[a, b]$, and second, the Mean Value Theorem must apply to $f$ on $[a, b]$. Both are guaranteed if we require **$f'$ to be continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$** (that is, $f$ is continuously differentiable on $[a, b]$):
1. Since $f'$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$, the Extreme Value Theorem (Theorem 1) guarantees that $f'$ attains an absolute minimum value $\min f'$ and an absolute maximum value $\max f'$ on $[a, b]$.
2. Since $f'$ exists on $[a, b]$, $f$ is differentiable (and hence continuous) on $[a, b]$. By the Mean Value Theorem, there exists some $c \in (a, b)$ such that
   $$
   \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a} = f'(c)
   $$
3. Because $c \in [a, b]$, the value $f'(c)$ is bounded between the minimum and maximum values of $f'$ on $[a, b]$:
   $$
   \min f' \le f'(c) \le \max f' \implies \min f' \le \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a} \le \max f'
   $$
as desired. $\blacksquare$

# 3.3 The First Derivative Test for Local Extreme Values

In Section 3.1 we learned that interior local extrema can only occur at critical points (where $f'(c) = 0$ or $f'(c)$ is undefined), and in Section 3.2 we proved Corollary 3, which tells us that $f$ rises where $f' > 0$ and falls where $f' < 0$. What happens when we combine these two ideas?

Imagine walking along the graph of a continuous function $f(x)$ from left to right as you pass through a critical point $x = c$:
- If you are climbing ($f' > 0$) to the left of $c$ and descending ($f' < 0$) to the right of $c$, then $x = c$ must be the crest of a hill—a **local maximum**!
- If you are descending ($f' < 0$) to the left of $c$ and climbing ($f' > 0$) to the right of $c$, then $x = c$ must be the bottom of a valley—a **local minimum**!
- If you keep climbing on both sides of $c$ (or keep descending on both sides of $c$), then $x = c$ is merely a momentary pause or vertical inflection—**not a local extremum**.

This simple sign-change criterion is called the **First Derivative Test for Local Extreme Values**.

## The First Derivative Test

> [!theorem] Theorem 5: The First Derivative Test for Local Extreme Values
> Suppose $f$ is a continuous function on an interval containing a critical point $c$ (or endpoint $a$ or $b$) and differentiable on the interior of the interval (except possibly at $c$ itself).
> 
> **At an interior critical point $c$:**
> 1. If $f'$ changes from positive to negative at $c$ ($f' > 0$ for $x < c$ and $f' < 0$ for $x > c$ on some open intervals abutting $c$), then $f$ has a **local maximum** value at $c$.
>    ![](Pasted%20image%2020260922181805.webp)
> 2. If $f'$ changes from negative to positive at $c$ ($f' < 0$ for $x < c$ and $f' > 0$ for $x > c$ on some open intervals abutting $c$), then $f$ has a **local minimum** value at $c$.
>    ![](Pasted%20image%2020260922181827.webp)
> 3. If $f'$ does not change sign at $c$ ($f'$ has the same sign on both sides of $c$), then $f$ has **no local extreme value** at $c$.
>    ![](Pasted%20image%2020260922181855.webp)
> 
> **At a left endpoint $a$:** If $f' > 0$ (or $f' < 0$) for $x > a$ near $a$, then $f$ has a **local minimum** (or **local maximum**) value at $a$.
> ![](Pasted%20image%2020260922181906.webp)
> 
> **At a right endpoint $b$:** If $f' > 0$ (or $f' < 0$) for $x < b$ near $b$, then $f$ has a **local maximum** (or **local minimum**) value at $b$.
> ![](Pasted%20image%2020260922181917.webp)

Let us see how the First Derivative Test effortlessly distinguishes between critical points that produce extrema and critical points that do not.

> [!example] Example: Applying the First Derivative Test to Locate Local and Global Extrema
> Find the critical points of $f(x) = x^{1/3}(x - 4)$, and identify all local and global extreme values of the function.

**Solution:** First, expand $f(x)$ into a sum of fractional powers so we can differentiate term by term:
$$
f(x) = x^{1/3}(x - 4) = x^{4/3} - 4x^{1/3}
$$
Differentiating with respect to $x$ and factoring over a common denominator gives
$$
f'(x) = \frac{4}{3}x^{1/3} - \frac{4}{3}x^{-2/3} = \frac{4}{3}\frac{x - 1}{x^{2/3}}
$$
From this factored form, we see that $f'(x) = 0$ at $x = 1$ and $f'(x)$ is undefined at $x = 0$ (where $f(0) = 0$ is still defined and continuous). Thus, the critical points are $x = 0$ and $x = 1$.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260922182122.webp)

Next, let us examine the sign of $f'(x) = \dfrac{4}{3}\dfrac{x - 1}{x^{2/3}}$ across the sub-intervals $(-\infty, 0)$, $(0, 1)$, and $(1, \infty)$:
- Notice that the denominator $x^{2/3} = (x^{1/3})^2$ is strictly positive for all $x \neq 0$.
- Consequently, the sign of $f'(x)$ is controlled entirely by the numerator factor $(x - 1)$:
  $$
  f'(x) < 0 \quad \text{for } x < 1 \ (x \neq 0), \qquad f'(x) > 0 \quad \text{for } x > 1
  $$
- **At $x = 0$:** As $x$ passes through $0$, $f'(x)$ remains negative on both sides ($-$ to $-$). By the First Derivative Test, $f$ has **no local extremum at $x = 0$** (the graph has a vertical tangent there and keeps decreasing).
- **At $x = 1$:** As $x$ passes through $1$, $f'(x)$ changes sign from negative to positive ($-$ to $+$). By the First Derivative Test, $f$ has a **local minimum at $x = 1$** with value $f(1) = 1^{1/3}(1 - 4) = -3$.

Finally, what about global extrema on $(-\infty, \infty)$? The domain has no endpoints, and as $x \to \infty$ or $x \to -\infty$, the leading term $x^{4/3} \to \infty$, so $f(x) \to \infty$. Therefore, $f(x)$ has **no absolute maximum**, and its local minimum at $x = 1$ ($f(1) = -3$) is also its **absolute minimum**. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 48: Intervals of Increase and Decrease of a General Quadratic Function

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Find the intervals on which the quadratic function $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ ($a \neq 0$) is increasing and decreasing. Give reasons for your answer.

**Solution:** Differentiating $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ gives the linear derivative
$$
f'(x) = 2ax + b
$$
Setting $f'(x) = 0$ yields the unique critical point at the vertex $x = -\dfrac{b}{2a}$. The sign of $f'(x) = 2a\left(x + \dfrac{b}{2a}\right)$ on either side of $x = -\dfrac{b}{2a}$ depends on the sign of the leading coefficient $a$:
1. **Case 1 ($a > 0$):** We have $f'(x) = 2ax + b > 0 \iff x > -\dfrac{b}{2a}$, and $f'(x) < 0 \iff x < -\dfrac{b}{2a}$. By Corollary 3, $f$ is **decreasing** on $\left(-\infty, -\dfrac{b}{2a}\right]$ and **increasing** on $\left[-\dfrac{b}{2a}, \infty\right)$.
2. **Case 2 ($a < 0$):** Dividing by $2a < 0$ reverses the inequality, so $f'(x) > 0 \iff x < -\dfrac{b}{2a}$, and $f'(x) < 0 \iff x > -\dfrac{b}{2a}$. By Corollary 3, $f$ is **increasing** on $\left(-\infty, -\dfrac{b}{2a}\right]$ and **decreasing** on $\left[-\dfrac{b}{2a}, \infty\right)$. $\blacksquare$

# 3.4 Graphing with $y'$ and $y''$

Knowing where a curve rises and falls ($y' > 0$ vs. $y' < 0$) gives us a rough stick-figure sketch of a function, but it does not tell us how the curve *bends*. Does an increasing curve bend upward like an accelerating rocket, or bend downward like a leveling-off hill? To capture the curvature or **concavity** of a graph, we bring in the second derivative $y''$.

## Concavity and the Second Derivative Test for Concavity

Imagine driving along a curve $y = f(x)$ from left to right. If the tangent line rotates counterclockwise as you move forward—so that the slope $y'$ is *increasing*—the curve bends upward and holds water like a cup; we say it is **concave up**. If the tangent line rotates clockwise—so that the slope $y'$ is *decreasing*—the curve bends downward and spills water like an umbrella; we say it is **concave down**.

> [!abstract] Definition: Concavity
> The graph of a differentiable function $y = f(x)$ is:
> - **concave up** on an open interval $I$ if $y'$ is increasing on $I$;
> - **concave down** on an open interval $I$ if $y'$ is decreasing on $I$.

For example, consider the cubic curve $y = x^3$, whose first derivative is $y' = 3x^2$. On the negative half-line $(-\infty, 0)$, $y' = 3x^2$ decreases as $x$ increases toward $0$, so $y = x^3$ is **concave down** on $(-\infty, 0)$ (turning clockwise from left to right). On the positive half-line $(0, \infty)$, $y' = 3x^2$ increases as $x$ grows, so $y = x^3$ is **concave up** on $(0, \infty)$ (turning counterclockwise from right to left).

![](Pasted%20image%2020260922200718.webp)

How can we test whether $y'$ is increasing or decreasing without graphing $y'$ directly? Since $y'' = (y')'$ is the derivative of $y'$, Corollary 3 from Section 3.2 immediately tells us that $y'$ increases where $y'' > 0$ and decreases where $y'' < 0$!

> [!theorem] Theorem: The Second Derivative Test for Concavity
> Let $y = f(x)$ be twice differentiable on an interval $I$.
> 1. If $y'' > 0$ on $I$, the graph of $f$ over $I$ is **concave up**.
> 2. If $y'' < 0$ on $I$, the graph of $f$ over $I$ is **concave down**.

> [!example] Example: Testing Concavity of $y = x^3$ Using $y''$
> Use the Second Derivative Test for Concavity to determine the concavity of $y = x^3$.

**Solution:** Differentiating $y = x^3$ twice gives
$$
y = x^3 \implies y' = 3x^2 \implies y'' = 6x
$$
- For $x \in (-\infty, 0)$, we have $y'' = 6x < 0$, so the graph of $y = x^3$ is **concave down** on $(-\infty, 0)$.
- For $x \in (0, \infty)$, we have $y'' = 6x > 0$, so the graph of $y = x^3$ is **concave up** on $(0, \infty)$. $\blacksquare$

## Points of Inflection

Notice that at $x = 0$ on the curve $y = x^3$, the graph transitions smoothly from being concave down on the left to being concave up on the right. Points where a curve has a tangent line and switches concavity are important geometric landmarks called **points of inflection**.

> [!abstract] Definition: Point of Inflection
> A point where the graph of a function has a tangent line and where the concavity changes (from concave up to concave down, or vice versa) is called a **point of inflection**.

Why must a tangent line exist, and how do we hunt for inflection points using $y''$? If $f$ is twice differentiable at an inflection point $x = c$, then $y''$ must change sign as $x$ passes through $c$. Therefore, at any point of inflection where $y''$ exists, we must have **$y'' = 0$** (alternatively, an inflection point can occur where $y'$ has a vertical tangent so that $y''$ is undefined).

> [!example] Example: Inflection Points of a Sinusoidal Curve and Simple Harmonic Motion
> Find the points of inflection of the curve $y = 2 + \cos x$, and interpret them physically if $y(x)$ represents the position of a particle in simple harmonic motion.

**Solution:** Differentiating $y = 2 + \cos x$ twice yields
$$
y' = -\sin x \implies y'' = -\cos x
$$
Notice that $y'' = -\cos x$ changes sign whenever $\cos x$ passes through $0$, which occurs at every odd multiple of $\pi/2$:
$$
x = \frac{(2n + 1)\pi}{2}, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z}
$$
At each of these points, the concavity of $y = 2 + \cos x$ switches between concave up and concave down, so the points of inflection are $\left(\dfrac{(2n+1)\pi}{2}, 2\right)$ for $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

**Physical Interpretation:** If $y$ gives the position of a particle oscillating in simple harmonic motion around the equilibrium line $y = 2$, then $y'$ is the velocity and $y''$ is the acceleration. Because a point of inflection is precisely where $y''$ changes sign, the points of inflection mark the exact instants (as the particle passes through the center position $y = 2$) where the **acceleration reverses direction** and the speed reaches an extremum! $\blacksquare$

> [!warning] Caution: $y'' = 0$ Does Not Guarantee a Point of Inflection, and $y''$ Need Not Exist!
> Just as with first derivatives and critical points, there are two crucial subtleties to keep in mind when looking for points of inflection:
> 1. **An inflection point can occur where $y''$ is undefined** (provided the graph still has a vertical tangent line there).
> 2. **Having $y''(c) = 0$ is NOT sufficient for an inflection point**—the second derivative $y''$ must actually **change sign** across $x = c$.

Let us look at a concrete example of each subtlety.

> [!example] Example: A Point of Inflection Where $y''$ Does Not Exist
> Show that the curve $y = x^{1/3}$ has a point of inflection at $x = 0$, even though $y''(0)$ does not exist.

**Solution:** Differentiating $y = x^{1/3}$ twice for $x \neq 0$ gives
$$
y' = \frac{1}{3}x^{-2/3}, \qquad y'' = \frac{d^2}{dx^2}\left(x^{1/3}\right) = \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{1}{3}x^{-2/3}\right) = -\frac{2}{9}x^{-5/3} = -\frac{2}{9x^{5/3}}
$$
At $x = 0$, the curve is continuous and has a vertical tangent line (since $\lim_{x \to 0} y' = \infty$), while $y''(0)$ is undefined. However, look at the sign of $y''$ on either side of $0$:
- As $x \to 0^-$ ($x < 0$), $x^{5/3} < 0$, so $y'' > 0$ ($y'' \to +\infty$, concave up).
- As $x \to 0^+$ ($x > 0$), $x^{5/3} > 0$, so $y'' < 0$ ($y'' \to -\infty$, concave down).

Because the graph has a (vertical) tangent line at $(0, 0)$ and $y''$ changes sign across $x = 0$, the origin $(0, 0)$ is a genuine **point of inflection** despite $y''(0)$ being undefined! $\blacksquare$

> [!example] Example: No Point of Inflection Even Though $y'' = 0$
> Show that the curve $y = x^4$ has no point of inflection at $x = 0$, even though $y''(0) = 0$.

**Solution:** Computing the second derivative of $y = x^4$ gives
$$
y' = 4x^3 \implies y'' = 12x^2
$$
At $x = 0$, we indeed have $y''(0) = 12(0)^2 = 0$. However, for all $x \neq 0$, $y''(x) = 12x^2 > 0$. Because $y''$ is positive on *both* sides of $x = 0$, the curve remains concave up everywhere and never changes concavity—in fact, $x = 0$ is an absolute minimum of $y = x^4$, not an inflection point! $\blacksquare$

## The Second Derivative Test for Local Extreme Values

Instead of checking how $f'$ changes sign across a critical point $c$, we can often use the concavity $f''(c)$ right at $x = c$ to classify a horizontal tangent! If $f'(c) = 0$ (the curve is flat at $c$) and $f''(c) < 0$ (the curve is concave down, bending like an upside-down bowl), then $c$ must sit at the top of a local peak. Conversely, if $f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) > 0$ (concave up, bending like an upright bowl), then $c$ must sit at the bottom of a local valley.

> [!theorem] Theorem: The Second Derivative Test for Local Extreme Values
> Suppose $f''$ is continuous on an open interval that contains $x = c$.
> 1. If $f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) < 0$, then $f$ has a **local maximum** at $x = c$.
> 2. If $f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) > 0$, then $f$ has a **local minimum** at $x = c$.
> 3. If $f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) = 0$, the test **fails** (it is inconclusive): $f$ may have a local maximum, a local minimum, or neither at $x = c$.

> [!warning] Caution: Inconclusive Cases and Direction of Implication
> 1. **When the Test Fails:** The Second Derivative Test is convenient because it only requires evaluating $f''$ at a single point $c$ rather than testing signs on intervals, but it is **inconclusive** whenever $f''(c) = 0$ or $f''(c)$ does not exist. In those cases, you must fall back on the First Derivative Test!
> 2. **Sufficient, Not Necessary:** The conditions $f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) < 0$ are *sufficient* conditions for a local maximum, **not necessary** conditions. That is, $(f'(c) = 0 \text{ and } f''(c) < 0) \implies \text{local max}$, **not** the other way around! For instance, $y = -x^4$ has a local (and global) maximum at $x = 0$, yet $y''(0) = -12(0)^2 = 0$, not $< 0$.

## Strategy for Graphing $y = f(x)$ and Cusps

Before we put $y'$ and $y''$ together to sketch a complete curve, let us define one more sharp geometric feature that can occur where $y'$ blows up to $\pm\infty$: a **cusp**.

> [!abstract] Definition: Cusp
> The graph of a continuous function $y = f(x)$ has a **cusp** at a point $x = c$ if the concavity is the same on both sides of $c$ and either:
> 1. $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c^-} f'(x) = \infty$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c^+} f'(x) = -\infty$ (an upward-pointing sharp peak), or
> 2. $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c^-} f'(x) = -\infty$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c^+} f'(x) = \infty$ (a downward-pointing sharp beak).

![](Pasted%20image%2020260922203556.webp)

> [!summary] Procedure: Strategy for Graphing $y = f(x)$ Using $y'$ and $y''$
> 1. **Compute $y'$ and $y''$** and factor both expressions as completely as possible.
> 2. **Find the rise and fall of the curve:** Identify all critical points (where $y' = 0$ or is undefined) and test the sign of $y'$ on the resulting intervals to find where the curve increases, where it decreases, and where local extrema occur.
> 3. **Determine the concavity of the curve:** Identify all points where $y'' = 0$ or is undefined, and test the sign of $y''$ to locate intervals of upward/downward concavity and points of inflection.
> 4. **Synthesize the general shape:** Combine the sign charts for $y'$ and $y''$ (and check for vertical tangents or cusps) to sketch the qualitative shape on each sub-interval.
> 5. **Plot key anchor points and sketch the curve:** Evaluate $y = f(x)$ at critical points, inflection points, and intercepts to draw an accurate, properly scaled graph.

Let us walk through this entire five-step graphing procedure on a fractional-power curve that exhibits a cusp, a smooth minimum, and an inflection point!

> [!example] Example: Complete Curve Sketching of $y = x^{5/3} - 5x^{2/3}$
> Graph the function $y = x^{5/3} - 5x^{2/3}$ by analyzing its first and second derivatives.

**Solution:**
1. **First Derivative and Rise/Fall ($y'$):** Differentiating $y = x^{5/3} - 5x^{2/3}$ gives
   $$
   y' = \frac{5}{3}x^{2/3} - \frac{10}{3}x^{-1/3} = \frac{5}{3}\frac{x - 2}{x^{1/3}}
   $$
   Setting $y' = 0$ gives $x = 2$, and $y'$ is undefined at $x = 0$. Since $y$ is defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$, the critical points are $x = 0$ and $x = 2$. Testing the sign of $y'$ on $(-\infty, 0)$, $(0, 2)$, and $(2, \infty)$:
   ![](Pasted%20image%2020260922204221.webp)
   Since $y'$ changes from $+$ to $-$ at $x = 0$ and from $-$ to $+$ at $x = 2$, the function has a **local maximum at $x = 0$** and a **local minimum at $x = 2$**.

2. **Second Derivative and Concavity ($y''$):** Differentiating $y' = \frac{5}{3}x^{2/3} - \frac{10}{3}x^{-1/3}$ gives
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   y'' &= \frac{10}{9}x^{-1/3} + \frac{10}{9}x^{-4/3} \\
       &= \frac{10}{9}\left(\frac{1}{x^{1/3}} + \frac{1}{x^{4/3}}\right) \\
       &= \frac{10}{9}\frac{x + 1}{x^{4/3}}
   \end{aligned}
   $$
   Notice that $y'' = 0$ at $x = -1$ and $y''$ is undefined at $x = 0$, so our candidate points for concavity changes are $x = -1$ and $x = 0$. Since the denominator $x^{4/3} = (x^{1/3})^4$ is strictly positive for all $x \neq 0$, the sign of $y''$ is governed entirely by $(x + 1)$:
   - For $x < -1$, $y'' < 0$ (concave down).
   - For $-1 < x < 0$ and for $x > 0$, $y'' > 0$ (concave up).

3. **Classifying Features at Special Points:**
   - **At $x = -1$:** Concavity changes from concave down ($y'' < 0$) to concave up ($y'' > 0$), and $y'(-1)$ is finite, so $(-1, -6)$ is a **point of inflection**.
   - **At $x = 0$:** Notice that $\lim_{x \to 0^-} y' = +\infty$ and $\lim_{x \to 0^+} y' = -\infty$, while the curve is concave up ($y'' > 0$) on both sides of $0$. Thus, the graph has a sharp **cusp** (and local maximum) at $(0, 0)$.
   - **At $x = 2$:** We have $y'(2) = 0$ and $y''(2) = \dfrac{10}{9} \cdot \dfrac{3}{2^{4/3}} = \dfrac{10}{3 \cdot 2^{4/3}} > 0$, which confirms via the Second Derivative Test that $x = 2$ is a smooth **local minimum**.

4. **General Shape Summary:**
   - On $(-\infty, -1)$: increasing and concave down.
   - On $(-1, 0)$: increasing and concave up, rising into a sharp cusp at $(0, 0)$.
   - On $(0, 2)$: decreasing and concave up, bottoming out at $x = 2$.
   - On $(2, \infty)$: increasing and concave up.
   ![](Pasted%20image%2020260922204810.webp)

5. **Plotting Key Coordinates and Final Graph:** Evaluating $y = x^{2/3}(x - 5)$ at $x = -1, 0, 2, 5$ gives the anchor points

# 3.5 Limits as $x \to \pm\infty$, Asymptotes, and Dominant Terms

Up to this point, we have analyzed the behavior of functions on finite intervals or near specific points $x = c$. To complete our graphing toolkit, we need to understand what happens to $f(x)$ in the far distance as $x$ grows arbitrarily large positively ($x \to \infty$) or negatively ($x \to -\infty$), as well as near points where $f(x)$ blows up vertically ($f(x) \to \pm\infty$).

## Rigorous Definitions and Properties of Limits as $x \to \pm\infty$

How do we make the statement $\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L$ mathematically precise? Instead of requiring $x$ to lie within a small $\delta$-neighborhood of a finite point $c$, we require $x$ to lie beyond a sufficiently large threshold $M$ (or to the left of a sufficiently negative threshold $N$).

> [!abstract] Definition: Limits as $x$ Approaches $\infty$ or $-\infty$
> 1. We say that $f(x)$ has the **limit $L$ as $x$ approaches infinity** and write
>    $$
>    \lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L
>    $$
>    if, for every number $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a corresponding number $M$ such that for all $x$,
>    $$
>    x > M \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon
>    $$
> 2. We say that $f(x)$ has the **limit $L$ as $x$ approaches minus infinity** and write
>    $$
>    \lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = L
>    $$
>    if, for every number $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a corresponding number $N$ such that for all $x$,
>    $$
>    x < N \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon
>    $$

Just like finite limits as $x \to c$, limits at infinity obey all the standard algebraic limit laws whenever the individual component limits are finite real numbers.

> [!theorem] Theorem 6: Properties of Limits as $x \to \pm\infty$
> The following rules hold if $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} f(x) = L$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} g(x) = M$, where $L$ and $M$ are real numbers:
> 1. **Sum Rule:** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \big(f(x) + g(x)\big) = L + M$
> 2. **Difference Rule:** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \big(f(x) - g(x)\big) = L - M$
> 3. **Product Rule:** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} f(x) \cdot g(x) = L \cdot M$
> 4. **Constant Multiple Rule:** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} k \cdot f(x) = kL \quad \forall k \in \mathbb{R}$
> 5. **Quotient Rule:** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{L}{M}$, provided $M \neq 0$
> 6. **Power Rule:** If $m$ and $n$ are integers, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \big[f(x)\big]^{m/n} = L^{m/n}$, provided $L^{m/n}$ is a real number (and if $n$ is even, we assume $L > 0$).

Using the fundamental building block $\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{1}{x} = 0$ along with Theorem 6, we can evaluate many limits at infinity immediately.

> [!example] Example: Applying Limit Laws as $x \to \pm\infty$
> Evaluate the following limits:
> **(a)** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \left(5 + \frac{1}{x}\right)$
> **(b)** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{\pi\sqrt{3}}{x^2}$

**Solution:**
- **(a)** Using the Sum Rule:
  $$
  \lim_{x \to \infty} \left(5 + \frac{1}{x}\right) = \lim_{x \to \infty} 5 + \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{1}{x} = 5 + 0 = 5
  $$
- **(b)** Using the Constant Multiple and Product Rules:
  $$
  \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{\pi\sqrt{3}}{x^2} = \pi\sqrt{3} \cdot \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{1}{x} \cdot \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{1}{x} = \pi\sqrt{3} \cdot 0 = 0
  $$
  $\blacksquare$

## Limits of Rational Functions as $x \to \pm\infty$

What happens as $x \to \pm\infty$ in a rational function $\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}$, where $f(x) = a_n x^n + \dots + a_0$ and $g(x) = b_m x^m + \dots + b_0$ are polynomials? For huge values of $|x|$, the highest-degree (leading) term in each polynomial dominates all lower-degree terms. By dividing both numerator and denominator by the highest power of $x$ in the denominator, we obtain three clean rules based on comparing $\deg(f) = n$ and $\deg(g) = m$:
1. **Equal Degrees ($\deg(f) = \deg(g)$):** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{a_n}{b_n}$, the ratio of the leading coefficients.
2. **Bottom-Heavy ($\deg(f) < \deg(g)$):** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = 0$.
3. **Top-Heavy ($\deg(f) > \deg(g)$):** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \pm\infty$, with the sign determined by the leading terms of the numerator and denominator as $x \to \infty$ or $x \to -\infty$.

> [!example] Example: Limits of Rational Functions in All Three Degree Cases
> Evaluate the following limits:
> **(a)** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{5x^2 + 8x - 3}{3x^2 + 2}$
> **(b)** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{11x + 2}{2x^3 - 1}$
> **(c)** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{2x^2 - 3}{7x + 4}$

**Solution:** In each case, we divide numerator and denominator by the highest power of $x$ appearing in the denominator:
- **(a) Equal degrees ($\deg = 2$):** Divide numerator and denominator by $x^2$:
  $$
  \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{5x^2 + 8x - 3}{3x^2 + 2} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{5 + \dfrac{8}{x} - \dfrac{3}{x^2}}{3 + \dfrac{2}{x^2}} = \frac{5 + 0 - 0}{3 + 0} = \frac{5}{3}
  $$
- **(b) Bottom-heavy ($\deg(f) = 1 < \deg(g) = 3$):** Divide numerator and denominator by $x^3$:
  $$
  \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{11x + 2}{2x^3 - 1} = \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{\dfrac{11}{x^2} + \dfrac{2}{x^3}}{2 - \dfrac{1}{x^3}} = \frac{0 + 0}{2 - 0} = 0
  $$
- **(c) Top-heavy ($\deg(f) = 2 > \deg(g) = 1$):** Divide numerator and denominator by $x$:
  $$
  \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{2x^2 - 3}{7x + 4} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{2x - \dfrac{3}{x}}{7 + \dfrac{4}{x}} = \infty
  $$
  since the numerator $2x - 3/x \to \infty$ while the denominator approaches $7 > 0$. $\blacksquare$

## Horizontal, Vertical, and Oblique Asymptotes

When a curve flattens out toward a straight line as it travels far away from the origin—either horizontally as $x \to \pm\infty$, vertically as $y \to \pm\infty$ near $x = a$, or along a slanted line—that guiding line is called an **asymptote**.

> [!abstract] Definition: Asymptote, Horizontal Asymptote, and Vertical Asymptote
> - **Asymptote:** If the distance between the graph of a function and some fixed line approaches $0$ as a point on the graph moves increasingly far from the origin, we say that the graph approaches the line **asymptotically** and that the line is an **asymptote** of the graph.
> - **Horizontal Asymptote:** A line $y = b$ is a **horizontal asymptote** of the graph of $y = f(x)$ if either
>   $$
>   \lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = b \quad \text{or} \quad \lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = b
>   $$
> - **Vertical Asymptote:** A line $x = a$ is a **vertical asymptote** of the graph of $y = f(x)$ if either
>   $$
>   \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = \pm\infty \quad \text{or} \quad \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = \pm\infty
>   $$

Let us examine several classic examples of horizontal and vertical asymptotes.

> [!example] Example: Vertical Asymptotes of $y = \sec x$ and $y = \tan x$
> Do the curves $y = \sec x$ and $y = \tan x$ have asymptotes?

**Solution:** Yes! Because both $\sec x = \dfrac{1}{\cos x}$ and $\tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}$ have $\cos x$ in their denominator (while their numerators are $\pm 1 \neq 0$ whenever $\cos x = 0$), both functions blow up to $\pm\infty$ and therefore have **vertical asymptotes** at every odd multiple of $\pi/2$:
$$
x = \frac{(2n + 1)\pi}{2}, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z}
$$
Neither curve has a horizontal asymptote, since both are periodic and oscillate forever as $x \to \pm\infty$. $\blacksquare$

![](Pasted%20image%2020260922205544.webp)

> [!example] Example: Finding Horizontal and Vertical Asymptotes of $y = \dfrac{x + 3}{x + 2}$
> Find the asymptotes of the curve $y = \dfrac{x + 3}{x + 2}$.

**Solution:** Dividing $x + 3 = (x + 2) + 1$ by $x + 2$ allows us to rewrite the function in shifted reciprocal form:
$$
y = \frac{x + 3}{x + 2} = 1 + \frac{1}{x + 2}
$$
From this form, we can immediately read off both asymptotes and the direction of approach:
1. **Horizontal Asymptote ($y = 1$):** As $x \to \pm\infty$, $\dfrac{1}{x + 2} \to 0$, so $y \to 1$. More specifically, for $x > -2$ the fraction $\dfrac{1}{x + 2}$ is positive, so the curve approaches $y = 1$ from **above** as $x \to \infty$; for $x < -2$ the fraction $\dfrac{1}{x + 2}$ is negative, so the curve approaches $y = 1$ from **below** as $x \to -\infty$.
2. **Vertical Asymptote ($x = -2$):** As $x \to -2^+$, $\dfrac{1}{x + 2} \to +\infty \implies y \to +\infty$; as $x \to -2^-$, $\dfrac{1}{x + 2} \to -\infty \implies y \to -\infty$. Thus, $x = -2$ is a vertical asymptote. $\blacksquare$

![](Pasted%20image%2020260922205646.webp)

> [!example] Example: Using Symmetry to Find Asymptotes of $y = -\dfrac{8}{x^2 - 4}$
> Find the asymptotes of the curve $y = -\dfrac{8}{x^2 - 4}$.

**Solution:** Notice first that $f(-x) = -\dfrac{8}{(-x)^2 - 4} = f(x)$, so the function is **even** and its graph is symmetric about the $y$-axis. Thus, if we analyze its behavior as $x \to \infty$ and near $x = 2$, symmetry automatically gives us the behavior as $x \to -\infty$ and near $x = -2$!
- **Horizontal Asymptote ($y = 0$):** As $x \to \infty$, we have $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{-8}{x^2 - 4} = 0$ (approaching $0$ from below since $x^2 - 4 > 0$ for $x > 2$). By even symmetry, $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{-8}{x^2 - 4} = 0$ as well, so $y = 0$ (the $x$-axis) is a horizontal asymptote in both directions.
- **Vertical Asymptotes ($x = 2$ and $x = -2$):** Near $x = 2$, we have
  $$
  \lim_{x \to 2^+} \frac{-8}{x^2 - 4} = -\infty, \qquad \lim_{x \to 2^-} \frac{-8}{x^2 - 4} = +\infty
  $$
  By symmetry across the $y$-axis, $\lim_{x \to -2^-} y = -\infty$ and $\lim_{x \to -2^+} y = +\infty$. Thus, the vertical lines $x = 2$ and $x = -2$ are vertical asymptotes. $\blacksquare$

![](Pasted%20image%2020260922205728.webp)

> [!warning] Caution: Zero Denominators vs. Vertical Asymptotes (Reduce First!)
> It is tempting to assume that a rational function has a vertical asymptote at every point where its denominator equals $0$, but that is **only true after all common factors between numerator and denominator have been canceled (i.e., in lowest reduced form)**!
> 
> For example, consider
> $$
> y = \frac{x^3 - 1}{x^2 - 1} = \frac{(x - 1)(x^2 + x + 1)}{(x - 1)(x + 1)}
> $$
> Although the denominator vanishes at both $x = 1$ and $x = -1$, the curve only has a vertical asymptote at **$x = -1$**. At $x = 1$, the $(x - 1)$ factor cancels out, leaving a finite limit (a removable discontinuity / hole in the graph):
> $$
> \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^3 - 1}{x^2 - 1} = \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^2 + x + 1}{x + 1} = \frac{3}{2} \neq \pm\infty
> $$

Next, let us see how the Sandwich Theorem helps us find horizontal asymptotes of oscillating functions, and dispel the myth that a curve can never cross its asymptote!

> [!example] Example: Finding Asymptotes via the Sandwich Theorem for $y = 2 + \dfrac{\sin x}{x}$
> Using the Sandwich Theorem, find the asymptotes of the curve $y = 2 + \dfrac{\sin x}{x}$.

**Solution:** First, let us check $x = 0$, the only point where the denominator vanishes. Because $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$, we have
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} y = 2 + \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 2 + 1 = 3
$$
Since this limit is finite, there is **no vertical asymptote** at $x = 0$ (only a removable discontinuity at $(0, 3)$).

Next, let us investigate $x \to \pm\infty$. Because $|\sin x| \le 1$ for all real $x$, dividing by $|x| > 0$ gives
$$
\left|\frac{\sin x}{x}\right| \le \frac{1}{|x|} \implies -\frac{1}{|x|} \le \frac{\sin x}{x} \le \frac{1}{|x|}
$$
Since $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \left(-\frac{1}{|x|}\right) = 0$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{1}{|x|} = 0$, the Sandwich Theorem forces $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 0$. Therefore,
$$
\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \left(2 + \frac{\sin x}{x}\right) = 2 + 0 = 2
$$
Hence, the line **$y = 2$ is a horizontal asymptote** as $x \to \pm\infty$ (and notice that the curve crosses this asymptote infinitely many times, at every $x = n\pi$!). $\blacksquare$

### Oblique (Slant) Asymptotes

What happens when the degree of the numerator of a rational function is **exactly one higher** than the degree of the denominator ($\deg(f) = \deg(g) + 1$)? When we perform polynomial long division, the quotient is a linear polynomial $mx + b$ ($m \neq 0$) plus a proper rational remainder $\dfrac{r(x)}{g(x)}$ that vanishes as $x \to \pm\infty$. This means the graph approaches the slanted line $y = mx + b$, called an **oblique asymptote** (a linear asymptote that is neither horizontal nor vertical).

> [!example] Example: Finding an Oblique Asymptote by Polynomial Long Division
> Find all asymptotes of the graph of $y = \dfrac{x^2 - 3}{2x - 4}$.

**Solution:** We are interested in the behavior near the zero of the denominator ($x = 2$) and as $x \to \pm\infty$. Since the numerator has degree $2$ and the denominator has degree $1$, we divide $x^2 - 3$ by $2x - 4$:
$$
\begin{array}{r}
    \frac{1}{2}x + 1 \\[1ex]
    2x - 4 \enclose{longdiv}{x^2 + 0x - 3} \\[1ex]
    \underline{-(x^2 - 2x)\phantom{-3}} \\[1ex]
    2x - 3 \\[1ex]
    \underline{-(2x - 4)} \\[1ex]
    1
\end{array}
$$
This division tells us that $x^2 - 3 = \left(\dfrac{x}{2} + 1\right)(2x - 4) + 1$. Dividing both sides by $2x - 4$ decomposes $y$ into a linear part plus a remainder fraction:
$$
y = \underbrace{\frac{x}{2} + 1}_{\text{linear part}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{2x - 4}}_{\text{remainder}}
$$
Now both asymptotes are transparent:
1. **Vertical Asymptote ($x = 2$):** As $x \to 2^+$, $\dfrac{1}{2x - 4} \to +\infty \implies y \to +\infty$; as $x \to 2^-$, $\dfrac{1}{2x - 4} \to -\infty \implies y \to -\infty$. Thus, $x = 2$ is a vertical asymptote.
2. **Oblique Asymptote ($y = \frac{1}{2}x + 1$):** As $x \to \pm\infty$, the remainder $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{1}{2x - 4} = 0$, so the vertical distance between the curve $y$ and the line $y = \dfrac{x}{2} + 1$ shrinks to $0$ (approaching the line from above for $x > 2$ and from below for $x < 2$). Therefore, $y = \dfrac{x}{2} + 1$ is an **oblique asymptote** as $x \to \pm\infty$. $\blacksquare$

## Graphing with Asymptotes and Dominant Terms

Look again at the decomposition from our last example:
$$
y = \left(\frac{x}{2} + 1\right) + \frac{1}{2x - 4}
$$
Notice how the two pieces take turns controlling the graph in different regions of the domain:
- When $|x|$ is **numerically large** ($x \to \pm\infty$), the fraction $\dfrac{1}{2x - 4}$ is tiny, so the linear term $\dfrac{x}{2} + 1$ **dominates**.
- When $x$ is **very close to $2$** ($x \to 2$), the fraction $\dfrac{1}{2x - 4}$ blows up, so $\dfrac{1}{2x - 4}$ **dominates**.

Identifying these **dominant terms** gives us an immediate physical feel for how a rational function behaves both near its singularities and out near infinity. Let us assemble a master 9-step checklist for graphing any function $y = f(x)$.

> [!summary] Checklist for Graphing a Function $y = f(x)$
> 1. **Check for symmetry:** Is the function even ($f(-x) = f(x)$) or odd ($f(-x) = -f(x)$)?
> 2. **Check for shifts:** Is the function a simple horizontal/vertical shift or scaling of a known curve?
> 3. **Analyze dominant terms:** For rational functions, reduce common factors and use polynomial division to write $f(x)$ as a polynomial plus a proper remainder.
> 4. **Find asymptotes and removable discontinuities:** Check where the denominator is $0$ (vertical asymptotes vs. holes) and evaluate limits as $x \to \pm\infty$ (horizontal or oblique asymptotes).
> 5. **Compute $f'$ and solve $f' = 0$:** Identify critical points and intervals of rise and fall.
> 6. **Compute $f''$:** Determine intervals of upward/downward concavity and inflection points.
> 7. **Sketch the graph's general shape:** Combine asymptotes, dominant terms, rise/fall, and concavity.
> 8. **Evaluate $f$ at special values:** Compute coordinates of endpoints, critical points, inflection points, holes, and intercepts.
> 9. **Draw the final graph:** Use the asymptotes and dominant terms as a frame, and thread the curve through the special points with the proper slope and concavity.

Let us apply all nine steps of this checklist to a comprehensive rational graphing problem.

> [!example] Example: Complete Analysis and Graph of $y = \dfrac{x^3 - 3x^2 + 3x - 1}{x^2 + x - 2}$
> Graph the rational function $y = \dfrac{x^3 - 3x^2 + 3x - 1}{x^2 + x - 2}$ using the 9-step graphing checklist.

**Solution:** First, let us factor the numerator $(x - 1)^3 = x^3 - 3x^2 + 3x - 1$ and the denominator $(x - 1)(x + 2)$ to reduce the rational function (remembering that the original function is **undefined at $x = 1$**):
$$
y = \frac{(x^3 - 1) - 3x^2 + 3x}{(x - 1)(x + 2)} = \frac{(x - 1)\big[(x^2 + x + 1) - 3x\big]}{(x - 1)(x + 2)} = \frac{x^2 - 2x + 1}{x + 2}, \quad x \neq 1
$$
Now perform polynomial division on the reduced fraction: since $x^2 - 2x + 1 = (x - 4)(x + 2) + 9$, we have
$$
y = (x - 4) + \frac{9}{x + 2}, \quad x \neq 1
$$
Let us walk through the 9-step checklist:
1. **Symmetry:** There is no even or odd symmetry (because the vertical asymptote is at $x = -2$).
2. **Shifts:** While not a trivial shift of $x^2$ or $1/x$, it is the sum of a line and a shifted hyperbola.
3. **Dominant Terms:** When $|x|$ is numerically large, $y \approx x - 4$ dominates; when $x \to -2$, $y \approx \dfrac{9}{x + 2}$ dominates.
4. **Asymptotes and Holes:**
   - **Oblique asymptote:** $y = x - 4$ as $x \to \pm\infty$.
   - **Vertical asymptote:** $x = -2$ (with $y \to +\infty$ as $x \to -2^+$ and $y \to -\infty$ as $x \to -2^-$).
   - **Removable discontinuity (hole):** At $x = 1$, the original denominator is $0$, so there is a punctured hole at $(1, 0)$.
5. **First Derivative ($y'$):** Differentiating the decomposed form $y = (x - 4) + 9(x + 2)^{-1}$ gives
   $$
   y' = 1 - \frac{9}{(x + 2)^2} = \frac{(x + 2)^2 - 9}{(x + 2)^2} = \frac{x^2 + 4x - 5}{(x + 2)^2} = \frac{(x + 5)(x - 1)}{(x + 2)^2}
   $$
   Setting $y' = 0$ gives $(x + 2)^2 = 9 \implies x + 2 = \pm 3 \implies x = -5$ and $x = 1$ (note that $x = -2$ is outside the domain, so it is not a critical point, and $x = 1$ is a hole on the original curve).
   ![](Pasted%20image%2020260924170548.webp)
   From the sign chart of $y'$, the reduced curve increases on $(-\infty, -5)$, has a **local maximum at $x = -5$**, decreases on $(-5, -2)$ and $(-2, 1)$, reaches a **local minimum at the hole $x = 1$**, and increases on $(1, \infty)$.
6. **Second Derivative ($y''$):** Differentiating $y' = 1 - 9(x + 2)^{-2}$ yields
   $$
   y'' = \frac{18}{(x + 2)^3}
   $$
   Thus, $y'' < 0$ (concave down) for $x < -2$, and $y'' > 0$ (concave up) for $x > -2$. Concavity changes across the vertical asymptote $x = -2$ (there are no points of inflection since $x = -2$ is not on the graph).
7. **General Shape:** Combining the asymptotes $x = -2$ and $y = x - 4$ with the dominant terms gives the frame below:
   ![](Screenshot%20from%202026-09-24%2017-10-56.webp)
8. **Special Values:** At $x = -5$, $y(-5) = (-5 - 4) + \frac{9}{-3} = -12$ (local maximum). At $x = 1$, the reduced function has value $y = (1 - 4) + \frac{9}{3} = 0$, marking an open circle (hole) at $(1, 0)$. At $x = 0$, the $y$-intercept is $y(0) = -4 + \frac{9}{2} = \frac{1}{2}$.
9. **Final Graph:** Plotting the asymptotes, the local maximum $(-5, -12)$, and the hole at $(1, 0)$ yields the complete graph below. $\blacksquare$

## 3.5 Exercises

### Problem 77: Surjectivity of a Rational Function via Limits at Infinity and IVT

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Let $f(x) = \dfrac{x^3 + x^2}{x^2 + 1}$. Show that there is a value of $c$ for which $f(c)$ equals:
> **(a)** $-2$
> **(b)** $\cos 3$
> **(c)** $5 \times 10^6$

**Solution:** Notice first that the denominator $x^2 + 1 \ge 1$ is never zero, so $f(x)$ is continuous on the entire real line $(-\infty, \infty)$. Next, examine the end behavior of $f(x)$ as $x \to \pm\infty$: since the numerator has degree $3$ (odd) and the denominator has degree $2$ (even) with positive leading coefficients, we have
$$
\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{x^3 + x^2}{x^2 + 1} = +\infty, \qquad \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{x^3 + x^2}{x^2 + 1} = -\infty
$$
Because $f$ is continuous everywhere and grows arbitrarily large positively as $x \to \infty$ and negatively as $x \to -\infty$, the **Intermediate Value Theorem** guarantees that the range of $f$ is all of $\mathbb{R} = (-\infty, \infty)$. Consequently, for *every* real target value $y_0$—including **(a)** $-2$, **(b)** $\cos 3$, and **(c)** $5 \times 10^6$—there exists some real number $c$ such that $f(c) = y_0$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 78: Indeterminate Form $\infty - \infty$ with Radicals

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Find the limit
> $$
> \lim_{x \to \infty} \left(\sqrt{x^2 + x} - \sqrt{x^2 - x}\right)
> $$

**Solution:** As $x \to \infty$, both square roots approach $\infty$, giving an indeterminate difference of the form $\infty - \infty$. To resolve this, we multiply and divide by the conjugate $\sqrt{x^2 + x} + \sqrt{x^2 - x}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= \sqrt{x^2 + x} - \sqrt{x^2 - x} \\
     &= \frac{(x^2 + x) - (x^2 - x)}{\sqrt{x^2 + x} + \sqrt{x^2 - x}} \\
     &= \frac{2x}{\sqrt{x^2 + x} + \sqrt{x^2 - x}}
\end{aligned}
$$
For $x > 0$, we can factor $\sqrt{x^2} = x$ out of each radical in the denominator and cancel $x$:
$$
\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{2x}{x\sqrt{1 + \dfrac{1}{x}} + x\sqrt{1 - \dfrac{1}{x}}} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{2}{\sqrt{1 + \dfrac{1}{x}} + \sqrt{1 - \dfrac{1}{x}}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{1 + 0} + \sqrt{1 - 0}} = \frac{2}{1 + 1} = 1
$$
Thus,
$$
\boxed{\lim_{x \to \infty} \left(\sqrt{x^2 + x} - \sqrt{x^2 - x}\right) = 1}
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problems 79 & 80: Monotonicity of Odd and Even Functions on $x < 0$ vs. $x > 0$

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Suppose an odd function is known to be increasing on the interval $x > 0$. What can be said about its behavior on the interval $x < 0$? What if the function is even instead?

**Solution:** Assuming $f$ is differentiable (or directly using the definition of increasing/decreasing with $x_1 < x_2 < 0 \iff 0 < -x_2 < -x_1$):
1. **Odd Function ($f(-x) = -f(x)$):** Differentiating both sides of $f(-x) = -f(x)$ via the Chain Rule gives $-f'(-x) = -f'(x) \implies f'(-x) = f'(x)$, meaning the derivative of an odd function is **even**. Since $f$ is increasing on $x > 0$, we have $f'(x) > 0$ for all $x > 0$, which implies $f'(-x) = f'(x) > 0$ for all $-x < 0$. Therefore, $f$ is **also increasing on $x < 0$**.
2. **Even Function ($f(-x) = f(x)$):** Differentiating both sides of $f(-x) = f(x)$ via the Chain Rule gives $-f'(-x) = f'(x) \implies f'(-x) = -f'(x)$, meaning the derivative of an even function is **odd**. Since $f'(x) > 0$ for all $x > 0$, we have $f'(-x) = -f'(x) < 0$ for all $-x < 0$. Therefore, $f$ is **decreasing on $x < 0$**. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 81: Symmetry of Horizontal Asymptotes for Rational Functions

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Suppose that $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ are polynomials in $x$ and that $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = 2$. Can you conclude anything about $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$? Give reasons for your answer.

**Solution:** Yes, we can conclude with certainty that $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = 2$ as well!

Why? Because $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ are polynomials, the only way their quotient $\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}$ can approach a non-zero finite limit ($2$) as $x \to \infty$ is if $f$ and $g$ have the **exact same degree** $n$, in which case the limit is the ratio of their leading coefficients $\dfrac{a_n}{b_n} = 2$. When we take the limit as $x \to -\infty$, the highest power $x^n$ still cancels identically between numerator and denominator, leaving the exact same ratio of leading coefficients:
$$
\lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{a_n}{b_n} = 2
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 82: Asymptotes of Rational Functions Whose Denominator Never Vanishes

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Suppose that $\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}$ is a rational function with $f$ and $g$ polynomials. Can the graph of $\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}$ have an asymptote if $g(x)$ is never $0$? Give reasons for your answer.

**Solution:** Yes! Although $\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}$ cannot have any *vertical* asymptotes if $g(x)$ is never $0$, it can still have a **horizontal asymptote** (if $\deg(f) \le \deg(g)$, such as $y = \dfrac{1}{x^2 + 1}$ with horizontal asymptote $y = 0$) or an **oblique asymptote** (if $\deg(f) = \deg(g) + 1$, such as $y = \dfrac{x^3}{x^2 + 1} = x - \dfrac{x}{x^2 + 1}$ with oblique asymptote $y = x$). $\blacksquare$

### Problem 83: Maximum Number of Horizontal Asymptotes

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> How many horizontal asymptotes can the graph of a given rational function have? What about a general function? Give reasons for your answer.

**Solution:**
1. **For a Rational Function:** The graph of a rational function can have **at most $1$ horizontal asymptote**. As $x \to \infty$ and $x \to -\infty$, the limit of $\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}$ is governed by the degrees of the polynomials $f$ and $g$: it is either $0$ in both directions (if $\deg(f) < \deg(g)$), $\dfrac{a_n}{b_n}$ in both directions (if $\deg(f) = \deg(g)$), or $\pm\infty$ in both directions (if $\deg(f) > \deg(g)$). Thus, a rational function cannot approach two different finite limits as $x \to \infty$ and $x \to -\infty$.
2. **For a General Function:** An arbitrary function $y = f(x)$ can have **at most $2$ horizontal asymptotes**—one as $x \to \infty$ (if $\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L_1$) and another as $x \to -\infty$ (if $\lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = L_2$ with $L_1 \neq L_2$, such as $y = \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + 1}}$, which approaches $y = 1$ as $x \to \infty$ and $y = -1$ as $x \to -\infty$). $\blacksquare$

### Problem 84: Number of Vertical Asymptotes of a Rational Function

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> How many vertical asymptotes can the graph of a rational function have? Give reasons for your answer.

**Solution:** A rational function can have **any finite non-negative integer number** ($0, 1, 2, 3, \dots, n$) of vertical asymptotes. Specifically, after canceling all common factors between numerator and denominator, the graph has one vertical asymptote at each distinct real root of the reduced denominator polynomial $g(x)$. If $g(x)$ has degree $n$, it can have anywhere from $0$ up to $n$ distinct real roots, where $n$ can be any natural number. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 85: Slope Behavior Along a Horizontal Asymptote

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> **(a)** The word *asymptote* is derived from an old Greek word for "never touching." In practice, however, a curve may cross one of its asymptotes a finite number of times or even an infinite number of times, as does the curve $y = 2 + \dfrac{\sin x}{x}$. Show that the slope of the curve $y = 2 + \dfrac{\sin x}{x}$ nevertheless approaches the slope of the asymptote as $x \to \infty$.
> 
> **(b)** Give an example of a function $f(x)$ with the following three properties:
> 1. $f$ is differentiable for $x > 0$,
> 2. $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = 2$,
> 3. $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} f'(x)$ does not exist.

**Solution:**
**(a)** Differentiating $f(x) = 2 + \dfrac{\sin x}{x}$ using the Quotient Rule gives
$$
f'(x) = \frac{x \cos x - \sin x}{x^2} = \frac{\cos x}{x} - \frac{\sin x}{x^2}
$$
Since $|\cos x| \le 1$ and $|\sin x| \le 1$, both $\dfrac{\cos x}{x} \to 0$ and $\dfrac{\sin x}{x^2} \to 0$ as $x \to \infty$ by the Sandwich Theorem. Therefore,
$$
\lim_{x \to \infty} f'(x) = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{x \cos x - \sin x}{x^2} = 0
$$
which equals the slope ($0$) of the horizontal asymptote $y = 2$.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260924172517.webp)

**(b)** To make $f(x) \to 2$ while preventing $f'(x)$ from converging, we can make the curve oscillate with decaying amplitude $1/x$ but *increasing frequency* as $x \to \infty$! Consider
$$
f(x) = 2 + \frac{\sin(x^2)}{x}, \quad x > 0
$$
Let us verify all three required properties:
1. **Differentiability for $x > 0$:** $f(x)$ is a quotient and composition of differentiable functions with non-zero denominator $x$ on $(0, \infty)$.
2. **Horizontal Asymptote $y = 2$:** Since $\left|\dfrac{\sin(x^2)}{x}\right| \le \dfrac{1}{x} \to 0$ as $x \to \infty$, the Sandwich Theorem gives $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \left(2 + \frac{\sin(x^2)}{x}\right) = 2$.
3. **Non-existent Limit of $f'(x)$:** Differentiating $f(x)$ yields
   $$
   f'(x) = \frac{x \cdot 2x \cos(x^2) - \sin(x^2)}{x^2} = 2\cos(x^2) - \frac{\sin(x^2)}{x^2}
   $$
   As $x \to \infty$, the second term $\dfrac{\sin(x^2)}{x^2} \to 0$, but the first term $2\cos(x^2)$ oscillates endlessly between $-2$ and $+2$. Thus, $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} f'(x)$ **does not exist**! $\blacksquare$

### Problem 86: A Puzzle on Finding Oblique Asymptotes

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Suppose we want to identify the oblique asymptote of the curve
> $$
> y = \frac{x^2 + 3x + 7}{x + 2}
> $$
> If we recast the rational function as a polynomial plus remainder using long division, we find
> $$
> y = \frac{x^2 + 3x + 7}{x + 2} = x + 1 + \frac{5}{x + 2}
> $$
> so the oblique asymptote must be the line $y = x + 1$. But suppose, instead, that we divide both numerator and denominator by $x$ to obtain
> $$
> y = \frac{x^2 + 3x + 7}{x + 2} = \frac{x + 3 + \dfrac{7}{x}}{1 + \dfrac{2}{x}}
> $$
> For large $x$, the value of the numerator is approximately $x + 3$ and the denominator is approximately $1$, suggesting that the asymptote should be the line $y = x + 3$. Which line is the real asymptote, and what went wrong with the other argument?

**Solution:** The true oblique asymptote of $y = \dfrac{x^2 + 3x + 7}{x + 2}$ is **$y = x + 1$**, not $y = x + 3$. We can see why in two complementary ways:

1. **Why the Second Argument Fails (Hidden $O(1)$ Interaction):** In the expression $\dfrac{x + 3 + 7/x}{1 + 2/x}$, replacing the denominator $1 + \dfrac{2}{x}$ by $1$ introduces a small relative error of size $\approx -\dfrac{2}{x}$. When that small error $-\dfrac{2}{x}$ is multiplied by the huge term $x$ in the numerator, their product is $x \cdot \left(-\dfrac{2}{x}\right) = -2$, a non-zero constant! Explicitly, if we subtract $x + 3$ from the fraction, the limit is $-2$, not $0$:
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \lim_{x \to \infty} \left(\frac{x + 3 + \dfrac{7}{x}}{1 + \dfrac{2}{x}} - (x + 3)\right) &= \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\left(x + 3 + \dfrac{7}{x}\right) - (x + 3)\left(1 + \dfrac{2}{x}\right)}{1 + \dfrac{2}{x}} \\
     &= \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{x + 3 + \dfrac{7}{x} - x - 3 - 2 - \dfrac{6}{x}}{1 + \dfrac{2}{x}} \\
     &= \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{-2 + \dfrac{1}{x}}{1 + \dfrac{2}{x}} = -2 \neq 0
   \end{aligned}
   $$
2. **Definition of an Asymptote (Vanishing Vertical Distance):** For a line $y = mx + b$ to be an asymptote of $y = f(x)$ as $x \to \infty$, the vertical distance $f(x) - (mx + b)$ must approach $0$ as $x \to \infty$. Checking both lines directly:
   $$
   \lim_{x \to \infty} \big[f(x) - (x + 1)\big] = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{5}{x + 2} = 0
   $$
   whereas
   $$
   \lim_{x \to \infty} \big[f(x) - (x + 3)\big] = \lim_{x \to \infty} \left(-2 + \frac{5}{x + 2}\right) = -2 \neq 0
   $$
   Thus, the curve approaches $y = x + 1$ (and stays a constant vertical distance of $2$ units below $y = x + 3$) as $x \to \infty$. $\blacksquare$

# 3.6 Optimization

Why did mathematicians invent differential calculus in the first place? One of the strongest historical motivations—from designing lenses and artillery trajectories to maximizing industrial profit and minimizing construction material—was **optimization**: finding the absolute best (maximum or minimum) way to configure a system subject to physical or geometric constraints.

## Strategy for Solving Max-Min Problems

When tackling an applied optimization problem, the hardest step is usually translating a word problem into a single-variable function $f(x)$ defined on a clear domain. Following a structured 5-step workflow makes this translation systematic.

> [!summary] Procedure: Strategy for Solving Applied Max-Min Problems
> 1. **Read the problem carefully** until you understand it completely. Ask yourself: *What is given? What is the unknown quantity to be maximized or minimized? What are the constraints?*
> 2. **Draw a picture** and label every part that plays a role in the problem.
> 3. **Introduce variables:** List every geometric or physical relation in the picture and in the problem statement as an equation or algebraic expression.
> 4. **Write an equation for the unknown quantity to be optimized:** Express this target quantity as a function of a **single independent variable** by using the constraint equations to eliminate all other variables, and determine the physical domain of that variable.
> 5. **Test the critical points and endpoints:** Differentiate the single-variable function, find all critical points where $f' = 0$ or is undefined, and use the First or Second Derivative Test alongside endpoint evaluations to identify the absolute maximum or minimum.

Let us practice this 5-step optimization strategy across geometry, manufacturing, optics, and economics.

> [!example] Example: Maximizing the Volume of an Open-Top Box
> An open-top box is to be made by cutting congruent squares of side length $x$ from the corners of a $12\text{-in.}$ by $12\text{-in.}$ sheet of tin and bending up the sides. How large should the corner squares be to make the box hold as much volume as possible?
> 
> ![](Pasted%20image%2020260924172850.webp)

**Solution:** When we cut a square of side $x$ from each corner and fold up the four flaps, the resulting box has height $x$ and a square base of side length $12 - 2x$. Therefore, the volume $V(x)$ of the box is
$$
V(x) = x(12 - 2x)^2 = x(144 - 48x + 4x^2) = 144x - 48x^2 + 4x^3
$$
What is the physical domain of $x$? Side lengths cannot be negative ($x \ge 0$), and we cannot cut away more than half the $12\text{-in.}$ width ($12 - 2x \ge 0 \implies x \le 6$), so the domain of $V(x)$ is the closed interval $[0, 6]$.

Differentiating $V(x)$ to find the critical points gives
$$
\frac{dV}{dx} = 144 - 96x + 12x^2 = 12(x^2 - 8x + 12) = 12(x - 2)(x - 6)
$$
Setting $\dfrac{dV}{dx} = 0$ yields $x = 2$ and $x = 6$ (where $x = 6$ is already a domain endpoint). Evaluating $V(x)$ at the interior critical point $x = 2$ and at the endpoints $x = 0$ and $x = 6$:
$$
V(0) = 0, \qquad V(2) = 2(12 - 4)^2 = 128\text{ in}^3, \qquad V(6) = 0
$$
Thus, cutting **$2\text{-in.}$ squares** from the corners maximizes the volume of the box, yielding a maximum capacity of **$128\text{ in}^3$**. $\blacksquare$

> [!example] Example: Designing the Most Material-Efficient $1\text{-L}$ Cylindrical Oil Can
> You have been asked to design a $1\text{-L}$ ($1000\text{ cm}^3$) oil can shaped like a right circular cylinder. What dimensions (radius $r$ and height $h$) will use the least material?

**Solution:** Neglecting the thickness of the metal and any seams, minimizing the amount of material means minimizing the **total surface area** $A$ of the cylinder (two circular lids of area $\pi r^2$ plus the cylindrical side wall of area $2\pi rh$), subject to the fixed volume constraint $V = \pi r^2 h = 1000\text{ cm}^3$:
$$
A = \underbrace{2\pi r^2}_{\text{2 lids}} + \underbrace{2\pi rh}_{\text{can wall}}
$$
To express $A$ as a function of $r$ alone on the domain $r \in (0, \infty)$, we solve the volume constraint for $h = \dfrac{1000}{\pi r^2}$ and substitute it into $A$:
$$
A(r) = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r\left(\frac{1000}{\pi r^2}\right) = 2\pi r^2 + \frac{2000}{r}, \quad r > 0
$$
Differentiating $A(r)$ with respect to $r$ and setting $\dfrac{dA}{dr} = 0$ gives
$$
\frac{dA}{dr} = 4\pi r - \frac{2000}{r^2} = 0 \implies 4\pi r^3 = 2000 \implies r = \left(\frac{500}{\pi}\right)^{1/3}
$$
To confirm that this critical point minimizes $A(r)$, we check the second derivative on $(0, \infty)$:
$$
\frac{d^2A}{dr^2} = 4\pi + \frac{4000}{r^3} > 0 \quad \forall r \in (0, \infty)
$$
Because $A''(r) > 0$ throughout $(0, \infty)$, the graph of $A(r)$ is strictly concave up everywhere on its domain, so the unique critical point $r = \left(\dfrac{500}{\pi}\right)^{1/3}\text{ cm} \approx 5.42\text{ cm}$ gives the **absolute minimum surface area**. What is the corresponding height $h$?
$$
h = \frac{1000}{\pi r^2} = \frac{2 \cdot 500}{\pi r^2} = \frac{2(\pi r^3)}{\pi r^2} = 2r = 2\left(\frac{500}{\pi}\right)^{1/3}\text{ cm} \approx 10.84\text{ cm}
$$
Notice the elegant geometric takeaway: the most material-efficient cylindrical can has **height equal to its diameter** ($h = 2r$)! $\blacksquare$

> [!example] Example: Maximizing the Product of Two Positive Numbers with Fixed Sum
> Find two positive numbers whose sum is $20$ and whose product is as large as possible.

**Solution:** Let the two positive numbers be $x$ and $y$, where $x + y = 20 \implies y = 20 - x$. We wish to maximize their product $P$:
$$
P(x) = xy = x(20 - x) = 20x - x^2, \quad 0 < x < 20
$$
Computing the first and second derivatives of $P(x)$ gives
$$
\frac{dP}{dx} = 20 - 2x = 0 \implies x = 10, \qquad \frac{d^2P}{dx^2} = -2 < 0
$$
Since $P''(x) = -2 < 0$ everywhere, $P(x)$ achieves its absolute maximum at $x = 10$ (which gives $y = 20 - 10 = 10$ and a maximum product of $P(10) = 100$). $\blacksquare$

> [!example] Example: Largest Rectangle Inscribed in a Semicircle
> A rectangle is to be inscribed in a semicircle of radius $r = 2$. What is the largest area the rectangle can have, and what are its dimensions?
> 
> ![](Pasted%20image%2020260924173047.webp)

**Solution:** Place the center of the circle at the origin, as shown in the figure. If the upper-right corner of the rectangle has coordinates $(y, x)$ on the semicircle of radius $r$ (with $r = 2$), then the rectangle has height $x$ and total width $2y$. By the Pythagorean theorem,
$$
x^2 + y^2 = r^2 \implies x = \sqrt{r^2 - y^2}
$$
Thus, we can express the area $A$ of the rectangle as a function of $y$ on the closed interval $[0, r]$:
$$
A(y) = x \cdot 2y = 2y\sqrt{r^2 - y^2}, \quad y \in [0, r]
$$
Differentiating $A(y)$ via the Product and Chain Rules and setting $\dfrac{dA}{dy} = 0$ gives
$$
\frac{dA}{dy} = 2\sqrt{r^2 - y^2} + 2y \cdot \frac{-2y}{2\sqrt{r^2 - y^2}} = \frac{2(r^2 - y^2 - y^2)}{\sqrt{r^2 - y^2}} = \frac{2(r^2 - 2y^2)}{\sqrt{r^2 - y^2}} = 0
$$
Solving $r^2 - 2y^2 = 0$ for $y \in [0, r]$ yields
$$
y = \frac{r}{\sqrt{2}} \implies x = \sqrt{r^2 - \frac{r^2}{2}} = \frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}
$$
Evaluating $A(y)$ at the endpoints $y = 0, r$ and at the critical point $y = \dfrac{r}{\sqrt{2}}$:
$$
A(0) = 0, \qquad A(r) = 0, \qquad A\left(\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = 2\left(\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left(\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = r^2
$$
For $r = 2$, the **maximum area is $A_{\max} = 2^2 = 4$**, achieved when the height is $x = \dfrac{2}{\sqrt{2}} = \sqrt{2}$ and the width is $2y = 2\sqrt{2}$. $\blacksquare$

## Fermat's Principle and Snell's Law of Refraction

In physics, optimization principles often govern the fundamental laws of nature. In 1662, Pierre de Fermat proposed a remarkable variational principle for optics:

> [!theorem] Law: Fermat's Principle of Least Time
> Light always travels from one point to another along the path that minimizes the travel time (the quickest route).

Let us use Fermat's Principle and differential calculus to derive **Snell's Law of Refraction** when light passes from one medium (such as air) into another (such as water or glass).

> [!example] Example: Deriving Snell's Law of Refraction from Fermat's Principle
> Find the path that a ray of light will follow in going from a point $A$ in a medium where the speed of light is $c_1$, across a straight boundary line, to a point $B$ in a second medium where the speed of light is $c_2$.
> 
> ![](Pasted%20image%2020260924173220.webp)

**Solution:** Since light travels in straight lines within each uniform medium, the path from $A$ to $B$ consists of two straight line segments meeting at some point $P$ on the boundary line. As labeled in the diagram:
- Let $a$ be the perpendicular distance from $A$ to the boundary, and let $d_2 - a$ be the perpendicular distance from $B$ to the boundary.
- Let $d_1$ be the total horizontal separation between $A$ and $B$ along the boundary, and let $x$ be the horizontal distance from the foot of $A$'s perpendicular to the crossing point $P$ (so that $d_1 - x$ is the remaining horizontal distance from $P$ to the foot of $B$'s perpendicular).

By the Pythagorean theorem, the path lengths $p_1 = AP$ in Medium 1 and $p_2 = PB$ in Medium 2 are
$$
p_1 = \sqrt{x^2 + a^2}, \qquad p_2 = \sqrt{(d_1 - x)^2 + (d_2 - a)^2}
$$
Dividing each distance by the speed of light in that medium gives the travel times $t_1 = \dfrac{p_1}{c_1}$ and $t_2 = \dfrac{p_2}{c_2}$, so the total travel time $t(x)$ as a function of $x$ is
$$
t(x) = t_1 + t_2 = \frac{\sqrt{x^2 + a^2}}{c_1} + \frac{\sqrt{(d_1 - x)^2 + (d_2 - a)^2}}{c_2}
$$
To find the point $P$ that minimizes $t(x)$, we differentiate $t(x)$ with respect to $x$:
$$
\frac{dt}{dx} = \frac{x}{c_1\sqrt{x^2 + a^2}} - \frac{d_1 - x}{c_2\sqrt{(d_1 - x)^2 + (d_2 - a)^2}}
$$
Now look at the right triangles in the figure! The ratio $\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + a^2}}$ is simply $\sin\theta_1$ (where $\theta_1$ is the angle of incidence), and $\dfrac{d_1 - x}{\sqrt{(d_1 - x)^2 + (d_2 - a)^2}}$ is simply $\sin\theta_2$ (where $\theta_2$ is the angle of refraction). Thus, our derivative simplifies beautifully to
$$
\frac{dt}{dx} = \frac{\sin\theta_1}{c_1} - \frac{\sin\theta_2}{c_2}
$$
Setting $\dfrac{dt}{dx} = 0$ yields **Snell's Law of Refraction**:
$$
\boxed{\frac{\sin\theta_1}{c_1} = \frac{\sin\theta_2}{c_2}}
$$
Why does this critical point give the *minimum* travel time? Notice that as $x$ increases from $0$ to $d_1$, $\theta_1$ increases from $0$ toward $\pi/2$ (so $\sin\theta_1$ increases) while $\theta_2$ decreases toward $0$ (so $\sin\theta_2$ decreases). Thus, $\dfrac{dt}{dx}$ is negative to the left of the critical point (when $P$ is closer to $A$) and positive to the right of the critical point (when $P$ is closer to $B$). By the First Derivative Test, Snell's Law gives the unique path of **minimum time**! $\blacksquare$

## Cost, Revenue, and Profit in Economics

In mathematical economics, we often model the production and sale of $x$ items using smooth functions—even when $x$ is an integer—so that we can apply the tools of calculus:
- $r(x) = \text{the revenue from selling } x \text{ items}$
- $c(x) = \text{the cost of producing } x \text{ items}$
- $p(x) = r(x) - c(x) = \text{the profit from producing and selling } x \text{ items}$

In economics, the derivatives of these functions are called **marginal** rates, because $\dfrac{dr}{dx} \approx r(x+1) - r(x)$ and $\dfrac{dc}{dx} \approx c(x+1) - c(x)$ measure the approximate extra revenue or extra cost of producing **one more item** at production level $x$:
$$
\frac{dr}{dx} = \text{marginal revenue}, \qquad \frac{dc}{dx} = \text{marginal cost}
$$

Two fundamental theorems of microeconomics follow immediately from setting derivatives equal to zero.

> [!theorem] Theorem 7: Maximum Profit Principle
> Maximum profit (if any) at an interior production level $x$ occurs at a production level at which **marginal revenue equals marginal cost** ($r'(x) = c'(x)$).

**Proof:** Assume that the revenue function $r(x)$ and cost function $c(x)$ are differentiable functions of $x$. Then the profit function $p(x) = r(x) - c(x)$ is also differentiable, and by Theorem 2 any interior local maximum (or minimum) of $p(x)$ must satisfy $p'(x) = 0$:
$$
p'(x) = r'(x) - c'(x) = 0 \implies \boxed{r'(x) = c'(x)}
$$
$\blacksquare$

> [!warning] Caution: $r'(x) = c'(x)$ Identifies Candidates, Not Guaranteed Maxima!
> Theorem 7 does *not* say that every point where $r'(x) = c'(x)$ yields maximum profit—a solution to $r'(x) = c'(x)$ could just as easily be a local minimum of profit (maximum loss!) or an inflection point. Rather, Theorem 7 tells us *where to look*: we must test all critical points where $r'(x) = c'(x)$ (using the First or Second Derivative Test, or comparing profit values directly) to find the true global maximum profit.

What if a manufacturer wants to minimize the **average cost per item**, $\dfrac{c(x)}{x}$, rather than total cost?

> [!theorem] Theorem 8: Minimizing Average Cost
> The interior production level (if any) at which the **average cost** $\dfrac{c(x)}{x}$ is smallest is a level at which the **average cost equals the marginal cost**:
> $$
> c'(x) = \frac{c(x)}{x}
> $$

**Proof:** For $x > 0$, if $c(x)$ is differentiable, then the average cost function $A(x) = \dfrac{c(x)}{x}$ is also differentiable. By Theorem 2, any interior extremum of $A(x)$ must occur where $\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(\dfrac{c(x)}{x}\right) = 0$. Applying the Quotient Rule gives
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{c(x)}{x}\right) = \frac{x c'(x) - c(x)}{x^2} = 0 \implies x c'(x) - c(x) = 0 \implies \boxed{c'(x) = \frac{c(x)}{x}}
$$
$\blacksquare$

> [!note] Remark on Theorem 8
> Just like Theorem 7, Theorem 8 tells us *where to look* for the minimum average cost by narrowing the search to solutions of $c'(x) = \dfrac{c(x)}{x}$.

### Problem 14: Largest Right Circular Cone Inscribed in a Sphere

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Find the volume of the largest right circular cone that can be inscribed in a sphere of radius $R = 3$.

**Solution:** Let the inscribed cone have base radius $r$ and height $h$. By symmetry, the axis of the cone passes through the center of the sphere of radius $R = 3$. As shown in the cross-sectional diagram below, the distance from the center of the sphere to the flat circular base of the cone is $|h - 3|$ (specifically, $h - 3$ if the cone's height extends past the sphere's center, or $3 - h$ if the base lies above the center).

![](Pasted%20image%2020260924174326.webp)

In either case, applying the Pythagorean theorem to the right triangle formed by the sphere's center, the center of the cone's base, and the rim of the cone's base gives the exact same relation between $r$ and $h$:
$$
r^2 + (h - 3)^2 = 3^2 \implies r^2 = 9 - (h^2 - 6h + 9) \implies \boxed{r^2 = 6h - h^2}
$$
Substituting $r^2 = 6h - h^2$ into the volume formula for a cone, $V = \dfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$, expresses $V$ as a function of $h$ alone on the open interval $h \in (0, 6)$ (since $2R = 6$ is the diameter of the sphere):
$$
V(h) = \frac{1}{3}\pi (6h - h^2)h = \frac{\pi}{3}(6h^2 - h^3), \quad 0 < h < 6
$$
Differentiating $V(h)$ with respect to $h$ and setting $\dfrac{dV}{dh} = 0$:
$$
\frac{dV}{dh} = \frac{\pi}{3}(12h - 3h^2) = \pi h(4 - h) = 0 \implies h = 0 \text{ or } h = 4
$$
Testing the interior critical point $h = 4$ with the second derivative:
$$
\frac{d^2V}{dh^2} = \frac{\pi}{3}(12 - 6h) = 2\pi(2 - h) \implies \left.\frac{d^2V}{dh^2}\right|_{h=4} = 2\pi(2 - 4) = -4\pi < 0
$$
Thus, $V(h)$ attains its **absolute maximum at $h = 4$**, giving a maximum cone volume of
$$
V_{\max} = V(4) = \frac{\pi}{3}\big(6(4)^2 - 4^3\big) = \frac{\pi}{3}(96 - 64) = \boxed{\frac{32\pi}{3}}
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 15: Angle That Maximizes the Area of a Triangle with Two Fixed Sides

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Two sides of a triangle have fixed lengths $a$ and $b$, and the angle between them is $\theta$. What value of $\theta$ will maximize the triangle's area?

![](Pasted%20image%2020260924174427.webp)

**Solution:** First, let us derive the formula for the area of the triangle in terms of $a$, $b$, and the included angle $\theta \in (0, \pi)$ using the altitude $h$ drawn to the third side $c = x + y$, as shown in the figure above. Splitting $\theta = \theta_1 + \theta_2$ into two right triangles, we have $\sin\theta_1 = \dfrac{x}{a}$, $\cos\theta_1 = \dfrac{h}{a}$, $\sin\theta_2 = \dfrac{y}{b}$, and $\cos\theta_2 = \dfrac{h}{b}$. By the angle-addition identity for sine:
$$
\sin\theta = \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2) = \sin\theta_1\cos\theta_2 + \cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2 = \frac{x}{a}\cdot\frac{h}{b} + \frac{h}{a}\cdot\frac{y}{b} = \frac{(x + y)h}{ab} = \frac{ch}{ab}
$$
Solving for the altitude $h = \dfrac{ab\sin\theta}{c}$ and substituting into the standard triangle area formula $A = \dfrac{1}{2}ch$ gives
$$
A(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}c\left(\frac{ab\sin\theta}{c}\right) = \frac{1}{2}ab\sin\theta, \quad \theta \in (0, \pi)
$$
Now differentiate $A(\theta)$ with respect to $\theta$ to find the critical point:
$$
\frac{dA}{d\theta} = \frac{1}{2}ab\cos\theta = 0 \implies \theta = \frac{\pi}{2}, \qquad \left.\frac{d^2A}{d\theta^2}\right|_{\theta=\pi/2} = -\frac{1}{2}ab\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = -\frac{1}{2}ab < 0
$$
Therefore, the area of the triangle is **maximized when $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$** (a right triangle, with maximum area $\dfrac{1}{2}ab$). $\blacksquare$

### Problem 23: Dividing a Wire Between a Square and a Circle to Maximize Total Area

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> A $4\text{-m}$ wire is available for making a circle and a square. How should the wire be distributed between the two shapes to maximize the sum of the enclosed areas?

**Solution:** Let $x$ be the length of wire bent into a square of side $a$, and let $y = 4 - x$ be the remaining length of wire bent into a circle of radius $r$, where $x \in [0, 4]$. Since the perimeter of the square is $4a = x \implies a = \dfrac{x}{4}$ and the circumference of the circle is $2\pi r = y \implies r = \dfrac{y}{2\pi} = \dfrac{4 - x}{2\pi}$, the combined enclosed area $A(x)$ is
$$
A(x) = a^2 + \pi r^2 = \left(\frac{x}{4}\right)^2 + \pi\left(\frac{4 - x}{2\pi}\right)^2 = \frac{x^2}{16} + \frac{(4 - x)^2}{4\pi}, \quad x \in [0, 4]
$$
Differentiating $A(x)$ with respect to $x$ and setting $\dfrac{dA}{dx} = 0$:
$$
\frac{dA}{dx} = \frac{2x}{16} - \frac{2(4 - x)}{4\pi} = \frac{x}{8} - \frac{4 - x}{2\pi} = 0
$$
Clearing denominators by multiplying by $8\pi$:
$$
\pi x - 4(4 - x) = 0 \implies (\pi + 4)x = 16 \implies x = \frac{16}{\pi + 4}
$$
At this interior critical point, $y = 4 - \dfrac{16}{\pi + 4} = \dfrac{4\pi}{\pi + 4}$, and the combined area is
$$
A\left(\frac{16}{\pi + 4}\right) = \frac{16}{(\pi + 4)^2} + \frac{4\pi}{(\pi + 4)^2} = \frac{4(\pi + 4)}{(\pi + 4)^2} = \frac{4}{\pi + 4} \approx 0.56\text{ m}^2
$$
Wait—is this interior critical point a maximum or a minimum? Notice that $A''(x) = \dfrac{1}{8} + \dfrac{1}{2\pi} > 0$, so the interior critical point actually gives the **minimum** combined area! To find the **maximum** combined area on the closed interval $[0, 4]$, we must evaluate $A(x)$ at the two endpoints:
- **At $x = 0$ (all wire given to the circle):** $A(0) = \dfrac{4}{\pi} \approx 1.27\text{ m}^2$.
- **At $x = 4$ (all wire given to the square):** $A(4) = \dfrac{16}{16} = 1\text{ m}^2$.

Comparing $A(0) = \dfrac{4}{\pi} \approx 1.27$, $A\left(\dfrac{16}{\pi+4}\right) \approx 0.56$, and $A(4) = 1$, we see that the **maximum total area is achieved at the endpoint $x = 0, y = 4$**—by putting **all $4\text{ m}$ of the wire into the circle** (and none into the square)! $\blacksquare$

### Problem 25: Norman Window with Tinted Semicircle

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> A window is in the form of a rectangle surmounted by a semicircle. The rectangle is of clear glass, while the semicircle is of tinted glass that transmits only half as much light per unit area as clear glass does. The total perimeter $P$ is fixed. Find the proportions of the window that will admit the most light. Neglect frame thickness.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260924174525.webp)

**Solution:** Let $r$ be the radius of the semicircular top, so that the rectangular base has width $2r$ and height $h$. The total outer perimeter $P$ consists of the bottom width $2r$, the two vertical sides $2h$, and the semicircular arc $\pi r$:
$$
P = 2h + 2r + \pi r \implies h = \frac{P - (2 + \pi)r}{2}
$$
Since clear glass admits $k$ units of light per unit area and the tinted semicircular glass admits $\dfrac{1}{2}k$ units of light per unit area, the total light transmitted $L(r)$ is
$$
L(r) = k\left(\underbrace{2rh}_{\text{rectangle}} + \frac{1}{2}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\pi r^2}_{\text{semicircle}}\right)
$$
Substituting $h = \dfrac{P - (2 + \pi)r}{2}$ expresses $L$ as a function of $r$ alone:
$$
\begin{aligned}
L(r) &= k\left(2r \cdot \frac{P - (2 + \pi)r}{2} + \frac{\pi r^2}{4}\right) \\
     &= k\left(Pr - (2 + \pi)r^2 + \frac{\pi}{4}r^2\right) \\
     &= k\left(Pr - \left(2 + \frac{3\pi}{4}\right)r^2\right) = k\left(Pr - \frac{8 + 3\pi}{4}r^2\right)
\end{aligned}
$$
Differentiating $L(r)$ with respect to $r$ and setting $\dfrac{dL}{dr} = 0$:
$$
\frac{dL}{dr} = k\left(P - \frac{8 + 3\pi}{2}r\right) = 0 \implies \boxed{r = \frac{2P}{8 + 3\pi}}
$$
Since the second derivative $\dfrac{d^2L}{dr^2} = -k\left(\dfrac{8 + 3\pi}{2}\right) < 0$ is strictly negative, this radius $r$ maximizes the transmitted light. What is the corresponding height $h$?
$$
h = \frac{P - (2 + \pi)\dfrac{2P}{8 + 3\pi}}{2} = \frac{(8 + 3\pi)P - (4 + 2\pi)P}{2(8 + 3\pi)} = \frac{(4 + \pi)P}{2(8 + 3\pi)}
$$
Therefore, the optimal ratio of the window's width $2r$ to its rectangular height $h$ is
$$
\frac{\text{width}}{\text{height}} = \frac{2r}{h} = \frac{\dfrac{4P}{8 + 3\pi}}{\dfrac{(4 + \pi)P}{2(8 + 3\pi)}} = \boxed{\frac{8}{4 + \pi}}
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 28: Minimizing the Length of a Paper-Folding Crease

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> A rectangular sheet of $8.5\text{-in.}$ by $11\text{-in.}$ paper is placed on a flat surface. One of the corners is folded across and placed on the opposite longer edge while the other corners remain in their original positions, and the paper is smoothed flat. Find the minimum possible length $L$ of the crease.

**Solution:** Let the original rectangular sheet be $ABCD$ with width $AB = 8.5\text{ in.}$ and length $AD = BC = 11\text{ in.}$. Suppose the lower-left corner $A$ is folded across to touch the opposite long edge $BC$ at point $Q$, creating a crease along segment $RP$ of length $L$, where $P$ lies on the bottom edge $AB$ and $R$ lies on the left edge $AD$.

By the symmetry of folding $\triangle RAP$ onto $\triangle RQP$:
- Let $AP = PQ = x$, and let the crease length be $RP = L$.
- In right triangle $\triangle RAP$, the vertical leg is $AR = QR = \sqrt{L^2 - x^2}$.
- On the bottom edge of width $8.5$, the remaining segment is $PB = 8.5 - x$. Applying the Pythagorean theorem to right triangle $\triangle PBQ$ gives
  $$
  BQ = \sqrt{PQ^2 - PB^2} = \sqrt{x^2 - (8.5 - x)^2} = \sqrt{17x - 8.5^2} = \sqrt{17x - \left(\frac{17}{2}\right)^2}
  $$
- Now drop a horizontal perpendicular from $R$ across the sheet to meet the right edge $BC$ at point $E$, so that $RE = 8.5$ and $CE = DR = 11 - AR = 11 - \sqrt{L^2 - x^2}$.
- In right triangle $\triangle REQ$, the vertical leg $EQ$ along edge $BC$ is
  $$
  EQ = BC - BQ - CE = 11 - \sqrt{17x - 8.5^2} - \left(11 - \sqrt{L^2 - x^2}\right) = \sqrt{L^2 - x^2} - \sqrt{17x - 8.5^2} = AR - BQ
  $$
- Applying the Pythagorean theorem to right triangle $\triangle REQ$ ($QR^2 = RE^2 + EQ^2$, where $QR = AR$) gives:
  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  AR^2 &= 8.5^2 + (AR - BQ)^2 \\
       &= 8.5^2 + AR^2 + BQ^2 - 2 \cdot AR \cdot BQ \\
       &= 8.5^2 + AR^2 + (17x - 8.5^2) - 2 \cdot AR \cdot BQ
  \end{aligned}
  $$
  Canceling $AR^2$ and $8.5^2$ leaves $2 \cdot AR \cdot BQ = 17x$, or
  $$
  17x = 2\sqrt{L^2 - x^2}\sqrt{17x - 8.5^2}
  $$
Squaring both sides and solving for $L^2$ in terms of $x$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
17^2 x^2 &= 4(L^2 - x^2)\left(17x - \frac{17^2}{4}\right) \\
17^2 x^2 &= 68 L^2 x - 17^2 L^2 - 68x^3 + 17^2 x^2 \\
\implies L^2(68x - 17^2) &= 68x^3 \implies \boxed{L^2 = \frac{68x^3}{68x - 289} = \frac{4x^3}{4x - 17} = \frac{2x^3}{2x - 8.5}}
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus, the crease length as a function of $x$ is $L(x) = \sqrt{\dfrac{2x^3}{2x - 8.5}}$. Notice that minimizing $L(x)$ is equivalent to minimizing $L^2(x)$ (or setting the numerator of $\dfrac{dL}{dx}$ to $0$):
$$
(2x - 8.5)(6x^2) - (2x^3)(2) = 0 \implies 12x^3 - 51x^2 - 4x^3 = 0 \implies 8x^3 - 51x^2 = 0
$$
Since $x > \dfrac{8.5}{2} = \dfrac{17}{4}$, the unique critical point is $x = \dfrac{51}{8}\text{ in.}$. Substituting $x = \dfrac{51}{8}$ into our formula for $L$ gives the minimum crease length:
$$
L_{\min} = \sqrt{\frac{2\left(\dfrac{51}{8}\right)^3}{2\left(\dfrac{51}{8}\right) - \dfrac{17}{2}}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 51^3 / 8^3}{17 / 4}} = \sqrt{\frac{8}{17} \cdot \frac{51 \cdot 51^2}{8 \cdot 8^2}} = \boxed{\frac{51\sqrt{3}}{8}\text{ in.} \approx 11.04\text{ in.}}
$$
$\blacksquare$

# 3.7 Linearization and Differentials

If you zoom in closer and closer on any point $(a, f(a))$ of a smooth, differentiable curve, the curve flattens out and looks almost indistinguishable from its **tangent line**. This simple geometric observation is one of the most practical ideas in all of science and engineering: near a point $x = a$, we can replace a complicated nonlinear function $f(x)$ with a simple linear function $L(x)$—its tangent line!

## Linearization

Let us formalize the tangent-line approximation at $x = a$. Since the tangent line to $y = f(x)$ at $(a, f(a))$ has slope $f'(a)$, its point-slope equation is $y = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$.

> [!abstract] Definition: Linearization and Standard Linear Approximation
> If $f$ is differentiable at $x = a$, then the approximating linear function
> $$
> L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)
> $$
> is the **linearization** of $f$ at $a$. The approximation
> $$
> f(x) \approx L(x)
> $$
> of $f$ by $L$ is the **standard linear approximation** of $f$ at $a$. The point $x = a$ is called the **center** of the approximation.

Let us test how remarkably well the linearization approximates $\sqrt{1 + x}$ near $x = 0$.

> [!example] Example: Linearization of $y = \sqrt{1 + x}$ at $x = 0$
> Find the linearization of $f(x) = \sqrt{1 + x}$ at $x = 0$, and test its accuracy for $\sqrt{1.2}$, $\sqrt{1.05}$, and $\sqrt{1.005}$.

**Solution:** Evaluating $f(x) = \sqrt{1 + x}$ and its derivative $f'(x) = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{1 + x}}$ at the center $a = 0$ gives
$$
f(0) = 1, \qquad f'(0) = \left.\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1 + x}}\right|_{x=0} = \frac{1}{2}
$$
Therefore, the linearization of $\sqrt{1 + x}$ at $x = 0$ is
$$
L(x) = f(0) + f'(0)(x - 0) = 1 + \frac{x}{2}
$$
Let us compare the linear approximation $L(x) = 1 + \dfrac{x}{2}$ against the true square root as $x$ gets closer to $0$:

| Value | Linear Approximation $1 + \dfrac{x}{2}$ | Result | Accuracy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sqrt{1.2}$ ($x = 0.2$) | $1 + \dfrac{0.2}{2}$ | $1.10$ | Accurate to 2 decimal places ($\approx 1.0954$) |
| $\sqrt{1.05}$ ($x = 0.05$) | $1 + \dfrac{0.05}{2}$ | $1.025$ | Accurate to 3 decimal places ($\approx 1.02470$) |
| $\sqrt{1.005}$ ($x = 0.005$) | $1 + \dfrac{0.005}{2}$ | $1.00250$ | Accurate to 5 decimal places ($\approx 1.0024969$) |

Notice that each time we move $x$ ten times closer to the center $a = 0$, the approximation gains roughly **two extra decimal places of accuracy** (because the error shrinks quadratically like $x^2$)! $\blacksquare$

> [!note] Why Do We Use Linearization Today?
> With modern calculators and computers, we rarely need linearization just to compute a single numerical root like $\sqrt{1.05}$. The true power of linearization in physics and engineering is **symbolic**: it lets us replace a complicated nonlinear formula or differential equation (like $\sin\theta$ in a pendulum or $(1 - v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$ in relativity) with a tractable linear expression over a specified operating range and error tolerance!

### Common Linear Approximations Near $x = 0$

Four standard linearizations centered at $a = 0$ appear so frequently across mathematics, physics, and engineering that they are worth knowing by heart:
1. $\sin x \approx x \quad (x \approx 0)$
2. $\cos x \approx 1 \quad (x \approx 0)$
3. $\tan x \approx x \quad (x \approx 0)$
4. $(1 + x)^k \approx 1 + kx \quad (x \approx 0, \text{ for any real power } k)$

The binomial approximation $(1 + x)^k \approx 1 + kx$ for $x \approx 0$ is especially versatile (for instance, our earlier example $\sqrt{1 + x} = (1 + x)^{1/2} \approx 1 + \frac{1}{2}x$ is the special case $k = 1/2$).

## Differentials

Leibniz wrote the derivative as $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ because he thought of it as an actual quotient of an "infinitesimal change in $y$" divided by an "infinitesimal change in $x$." Using the tangent line, we can give $dx$ and $dy$ a completely rigorous modern definition as finite real variables!

> [!abstract] Definition: Differentials $dx$ and $dy$
> Let $y = f(x)$ be a differentiable function. The **differential $dx$** is an independent variable (capable of taking any real value). The **differential $dy$** is a dependent variable defined in terms of $x$ and $dx$ by
> $$
> dy = f'(x) \, dx
> $$

> [!note] Understanding Differentials Geometrically and Algebraically
> 1. **Geometric Meaning:** If you stand at $(x, f(x))$ and take a horizontal step of size $dx = \Delta x$, the true curve rises by $\Delta y = f(x + dx) - f(x)$, whereas the **tangent line** rises by $dy = f'(x) \, dx$. Thus, **$dy$ is the vertical rise (or fall) along the tangent line** corresponding to a horizontal change $dx$.
> 2. **Independent vs. Dependent:** While $dx$ is an independent variable (most often chosen equal to the input increment $\Delta x$), $dy$ is always a **dependent variable** that depends on two inputs: the base point $x$ (which determines the slope $f'(x)$) and the step size $dx$.
> 3. **Leibniz Quotient $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$:** Whenever $dx \neq 0$, we can literally divide both sides of $dy = f'(x) \, dx$ by $dx$ to obtain $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = f'(x)$. Thus, with differentials defined this way, $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ is genuinely the algebraic quotient of the two real numbers $dy$ and $dx$!
> 4. **Notation $df$:** We often write $df = f'(x) \, dx$ in place of $dy = f'(x) \, dx$ and call $df$ the **differential of $f$**.

Because $du = u'(x) \, dx$ and $dv = v'(x) \, dx$ are simply derivatives multiplied by $dx$, every differentiation rule has a clean, compact differential counterpart:
- **Sum Rule for Differentials:** $d(u + v) = du + dv$
  *Proof:* Letting $f(x) = u + v$, we have $f'(x) = u' + v'$. Multiplying by $dx$ gives
  $$
  d(u + v) = (u' + v') \, dx = u' \, dx + v' \, dx = du + dv \quad \blacksquare
  $$
- **Product Rule for Differentials:** $d(uv) = u \, dv + v \, du$
  *Proof:* Letting $f(x) = uv$, we have $f'(x) = uv' + vu'$. Multiplying by $dx$ gives
  $$
  d(uv) = (uv' + vu') \, dx = u(v' \, dx) + v(u' \, dx) = u \, dv + v \, du \quad \blacksquare
  $$
- **Quotient and Power Rules for Differentials:** By the exact same multiplication by $dx$, we obtain
  $$
  \boxed{d\left(\frac{u}{v}\right) = \frac{v \, du - u \, dv}{v^2}}, \qquad \boxed{d(u^n) = n u^{n-1} \, du}
  $$

## Estimating Change with Differentials

Suppose we know the value of a differentiable function $f(x)$ at a convenient point $x_0$, and we nudge the input slightly from $x_0$ to $x_0 + dx$. How much does the output $f$ change?

> [!theorem] Theorem: Differential Estimate of Change
> Let $f(x)$ be differentiable at $x = x_0$. When $x$ changes by a small amount $dx$ from $x_0$ to $x_0 + dx$, the true change $\Delta f = f(x_0 + dx) - f(x_0)$ is approximated by the differential
> $$
> \Delta f \approx df = f'(x_0) \, dx
> $$

**Proof:** For small $dx$, the function $f(x)$ near $x_0$ is closely approximated by its linearization $L(x) = f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x - x_0)$. Therefore, the true change $\Delta f$ is approximately equal to the change in the linearization $\Delta L$:
$$
\Delta f \approx \Delta L = L(x_0 + dx) - L(x_0) = \big[f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x_0 + dx - x_0)\big] - f(x_0) = f'(x_0) \, dx = df
$$
Thus, $\boxed{\Delta f \approx \Delta L = f'(x_0) \, dx = df}$, which also confirms our geometric interpretation of $df$ as the change along the tangent line at $x_0$. $\blacksquare$

### Absolute, Relative, and Percentage Change & Sensitivity

When engineers measure a physical quantity $f(x)$ near $x_0$, they often care not just about the raw error $\Delta f$, but about how large that error is *relative* to the quantity $f(x_0)$ itself. We summarize the true and differential-estimated versions of absolute, relative, and percentage change in the table below:

| Type of Change | True Value | Differential Estimate |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Absolute Change** | $\Delta f = f(x_0 + dx) - f(x_0)$ | $df = f'(x_0) \, dx$ |
| **Relative Change** | $\dfrac{\Delta f}{f(x_0)}$ | $\dfrac{df}{f(x_0)}$ |
| **Percentage Change** | $\dfrac{\Delta f}{f(x_0)} \times 100$ | $\dfrac{df}{f(x_0)} \times 100$ |

> [!note] Sensitivity to Change
> The differential formula $df = f'(x) \, dx$ measures the **sensitivity** of the output $f(x)$ to small changes in the input $x$. The larger the magnitude of the derivative $|f'(x)|$ at a point $x$, the more strongly a given input perturbation $dx$ is amplified in the output!

Let us work through three practical estimation and error-tolerance examples using differentials.

> [!example] Example: Estimating the Increase in a Circle's Area
> The radius $r$ of a circle increases from $r_0 = 10\text{ m}$ to $10.1\text{ m}$. Estimate the increase in the circle's area $A$ by calculating $dA$, and compare your estimate with the true change $\Delta A$.

**Solution:** Since the area of a circle is $A(r) = \pi r^2$, its derivative is $A'(r) = 2\pi r$. With $r_0 = 10\text{ m}$ and $dr = \Delta r = 0.1\text{ m}$, the differential estimate of the area increase is
$$
\Delta A \approx dA = A'(r_0) \, dr = 2\pi (10)(0.1) = 2\pi\text{ m}^2 \approx 6.283\text{ m}^2
$$
Let us compare this with the exact change $\Delta A$:
$$
\Delta A = A(10.1) - A(10) = \pi\big((10.1)^2 - 10^2\big) = \pi(102.01 - 100) = \underbrace{2\pi}_{dA} + \underbrace{0.01\pi}_{\text{error}}
$$
Notice that the approximation error $\Delta A - dA = 0.01\pi \approx 0.0314\text{ m}^2$ is tiny compared to $dA = 2\pi\text{ m}^2$! $\blacksquare$

> [!example] Example: Surface Area Uncertainty of the Earth
> Suppose the Earth were a perfect sphere and we measured its radius to be $r = 3959 \pm 0.1\text{ miles}$. What effect would the measurement tolerance of $\pm 0.1\text{ mi}$ have on our estimate of the Earth's surface area?

**Solution:** The surface area of a sphere of radius $r$ is $S(r) = 4\pi r^2$, so $S'(r) = 8\pi r$. Setting $r = 3959\text{ mi}$ and $dr = 0.1\text{ mi}$, the uncertainty in the calculated surface area is approximately
$$
\Delta S \approx dS = S'(r) \, dr = 8\pi (3959)(0.1) \approx 9950\text{ mi}^2
$$
To put that in perspective, a mere $0.1\text{-mile}$ ($\approx 528\text{-foot}$) uncertainty in measuring the Earth's radius creates an uncertainty of nearly $10,000\text{ square miles}$ in its surface area—roughly the entire land area of the state of Manipur or Meghalaya! $\blacksquare$

> [!example] Example: Controlling Measurement Error to Meet a $1\%$ Surface Area Tolerance
> About how accurately should we measure the radius $r$ of a sphere to calculate its surface area $S = 4\pi r^2$ within $1\%$ of its true value?

**Solution:** We want the absolute surface area error $|dS|$ to be at most $1\%$ of the true surface area $S$:
$$
dS \le \frac{1}{100}S = \frac{4\pi r^2}{100}
$$
Replacing $dS$ with the differential $S'(r) \, dr = 8\pi r \, dr$ gives
$$
8\pi r \, dr \le \frac{4\pi r^2}{100} \implies dr \le \frac{r}{200} = 0.005r
$$
In percentage terms,
$$
\frac{dr}{r} \times 100 \le 0.5\%
$$
Thus, to guarantee that the calculated surface area is within $1\%$ of its true value, we must measure the radius $r$ with a relative error of no more than **$0.5\%$**. $\blacksquare$

## The Error in the Linear Approximation ($\Delta y = f'(x_0)\Delta x + \varepsilon \Delta x$) and the Chain Rule Proof

How fast does the approximation error $\text{error} = \Delta f - df$ shrink as $\Delta x \to 0$? Not only does the error go to $0$, it goes to $0$ **faster than $\Delta x$ itself**! This characterization of differentiability gives us a completely bulletproof proof of the Chain Rule (avoiding the division-by-zero flaw that can happen when $\Delta u = 0$).

> [!theorem] Theorem: Increment Form of Differentiability (Approximation Error)
> If $f(x)$ is differentiable at $x = x_0$ and $x$ changes from $x_0$ to $x_0 + \Delta x$, then the true change $\Delta y = \Delta f = f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)$ can be written in the form
> $$
> \Delta y = f'(x_0) \cdot \Delta x + \varepsilon \cdot \Delta x
> $$
> where $\varepsilon \to 0$ as $\Delta x \to 0$.

**Proof:** For $\Delta x \neq 0$, consider the approximation error between the true change $\Delta f = f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)$ and the differential $df = f'(x_0) \, \Delta x$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{error} = \Delta f - df &= \big[f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)\big] - f'(x_0) \, \Delta x \\
  &= \underbrace{\left[\frac{f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)}{\Delta x} - f'(x_0)\right]}_{\varepsilon} \Delta x = \varepsilon \cdot \Delta x
\end{aligned}
$$
where we define $\varepsilon = \dfrac{f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)}{\Delta x} - f'(x_0)$ (and set $\varepsilon = 0$ at $\Delta x = 0$). Because $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$, as $\Delta x \to 0$ the difference quotient $\dfrac{f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)}{\Delta x}$ converges to $f'(x_0)$, which forces
$$
\lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \varepsilon = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \left(\frac{f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)}{\Delta x} - f'(x_0)\right) = f'(x_0) - f'(x_0) = 0
$$
Writing $\Delta f = df + \text{error}$ immediately gives
$$
\Delta f = f'(x_0) \cdot \Delta x + \varepsilon \cdot \Delta x \quad \text{with } \varepsilon \to 0 \text{ as } \Delta x \to 0
$$
Geometrically, as shown in the figure below, if $a$ is the angle of the tangent line ($\tan a = f'(x_0)$) and $b$ is the angle of the secant line ($\tan b = \dfrac{\Delta f}{\Delta x}$), then $\varepsilon = \tan b - \tan a = (\text{slope of secant} - \text{slope of tangent})$, so the vertical error is $\text{error} = \varepsilon \cdot \Delta x = (\tan b - \tan a) \cdot \Delta x$. $\blacksquare$

### Rigorous Proof of the Chain Rule

Now let us use this increment equation to give a completely rigorous proof of the **Chain Rule**!

> [!theorem] Theorem: The Chain Rule
> If $f(u)$ is a differentiable function of $u$ and $u = g(x)$ is a differentiable function of $x$, then the composite function $y = f(g(x))$ is a differentiable function of $x$. More precisely, if $g$ is differentiable at $x_0$ and $f$ is differentiable at $u_0 = g(x_0)$, then the composite $y = f(g(x))$ is differentiable at $x_0$ and
> $$
> \left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{x=x_0} = f'(g(x_0)) \cdot g'(x_0)
> $$

**Proof:** Let $x$ change from $x_0$ to $x_0 + \Delta x$, producing a corresponding change $\Delta u = g(x_0 + \Delta x) - g(x_0)$ in $u$ and a corresponding change $\Delta y = f(u_0 + \Delta u) - f(u_0)$ in $y$.

By applying the Increment Theorem to $f$ at $u_0$ and to $g$ at $x_0$, we can write both increments as
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta y &= f'(u_0)\Delta u + \varepsilon_1 \Delta u = \big(f'(u_0) + \varepsilon_1\big)\Delta u \\
\Delta u &= g'(x_0)\Delta x + \varepsilon_2 \Delta x = \big(g'(x_0) + \varepsilon_2\big)\Delta x
\end{aligned}
$$
where $\varepsilon_1 \to 0$ as $\Delta u \to 0$ (with $\varepsilon_1 = 0$ if $\Delta u = 0$) and $\varepsilon_2 \to 0$ as $\Delta x \to 0$. Notice that as $\Delta x \to 0$, continuity of $g$ at $x_0$ guarantees $\Delta u \to 0$, so both $\varepsilon_1 \to 0$ and $\varepsilon_2 \to 0$ as $\Delta x \to 0$.

Now substitute the expression for $\Delta u$ into the equation for $\Delta y$ (remembering that $u_0 = g(x_0)$):
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta y &= \big(f'(g(x_0)) + \varepsilon_1\big)\big(g'(x_0) + \varepsilon_2\big)\Delta x \\
  &= \big[f'(g(x_0)) \cdot g'(x_0) + \varepsilon_2 f'(g(x_0)) + \varepsilon_1 g'(x_0) + \varepsilon_1 \varepsilon_2\big]\Delta x
\end{aligned}
$$
Dividing both sides by $\Delta x \neq 0$ gives
$$
\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = f'(g(x_0)) \cdot g'(x_0) + \varepsilon_2 f'(g(x_0)) + \varepsilon_1 g'(x_0) + \varepsilon_1 \varepsilon_2
$$
Finally, taking the limit as $\Delta x \to 0$ (where $\varepsilon_1 \to 0$ and $\varepsilon_2 \to 0$), all three error terms vanish, leaving
$$
\left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{x=x_0} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = f'(g(x_0)) \cdot g'(x_0)
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Derivative vs. Differential of Composite Functions

There is an instructive subtle difference between the hypotheses needed for the derivative of $f(g(x))$ *with respect to $g(x)$* versus the differential $dy$ of $y = f(g(x))$:
1. **Derivative of $f(g(x))$ with respect to $g(x)$:** This is defined directly as the limit
   $$
   \frac{d\big(f(g(x))\big)}{d\big(g(x)\big)} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(g(x) + h) - f(g(x))}{h} = f'(g(x))
   $$
   For this limit to exist, we only need $g(x)$ to be defined and lie in the domain where $f$ is differentiable—$g(x)$ itself does not even need to be differentiable!
2. **Differential of $y = f(g(x))$:** The differential of $y$ is written as
   $$
   dy = f'(g(x)) \, dg = f'(g(x)) g'(x) \, dx
   $$
   Because $g$ is a dependent variable whose differential is defined by $dg = g'(x) \, dx$, writing the differential $dy$ of the composite function requires **$g(x)$ to be differentiable at $x$** in addition to $f$ being differentiable at $g(x)$.

### Physics Application: Einstein's Conversion of Mass to Energy ($E = \Delta m c^2$)

Let us see how the binomial linear approximation $(1 + x)^k \approx 1 + kx$ bridges Einstein's theory of special relativity and classical Newtonian mechanics!

In classical physics, Newton's second law states that force equals the time rate of change of momentum, and assuming mass $m$ is constant, this simplifies to $F = ma$:
$$
F = \frac{d}{dt}(mv) = m\frac{dv}{dt} = ma
$$
However, Albert Einstein discovered that the relativistic mass $m$ of a moving body is not constant—it increases with velocity $v$ according to the formula
$$
m = \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - \dfrac{v^2}{c^2}}} = m_0\left(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)^{-1/2}
$$
where $m_0$ is the **rest mass** of the body (at $v = 0$) and $c \approx 3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$ is the speed of light.

Why didn't anyone notice this mass change at everyday speeds? When $v$ is much smaller than the speed of light $c$, the ratio $x = -\dfrac{v^2}{c^2}$ is extremely close to $0$. Applying our standard linear approximation $(1 + x)^k \approx 1 + kx$ with $x = -\dfrac{v^2}{c^2}$ and $k = -\dfrac{1}{2}$ gives
$$
m \approx m_0\left(1 + \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(-\frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)\right) = m_0 + \frac{1}{2}m_0 v^2\left(\frac{1}{c^2}\right)
$$
Multiplying both sides by $c^2$ and rearranging yields
$$
(m - m_0)c^2 \approx \frac{1}{2}m_0 v^2 - 0 \implies \boxed{\Delta m \, c^2 \approx \Delta (\text{K.E.})}
$$
In other words, what classical physics calls **kinetic energy** ($\frac{1}{2}m_0 v^2$) is actually the energy equivalent $\Delta m \, c^2$ of the relativistic increase in mass! Because $c^2 \approx 9 \times 10^{16}\text{ m}^2/\text{s}^2$ is colossal, converting even a tiny amount of mass $\Delta m \approx 1\text{ gram}$ into energy releases the explosive energy of a $20\text{-kiloton}$ atomic bomb.

### Problem 61: Measurement Tolerance for the Volume of a Right Circular Cylinder

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> The height and radius of a right circular cylinder are equal ($r = h$), so the cylinder's volume is $V = \pi h^3$. The volume is to be calculated from a measurement of $h$ and must be calculated with an error of no more than $1\%$ of the true value. Find approximately the greatest error that can be tolerated in the measurement of $h$, expressed as a percentage of $h$.

**Solution:** Given $V = \pi h^3$, the differential of $V$ is $dV = 3\pi h^2 \, dh$. Dividing by $V = \pi h^3$ gives the relative change in volume in terms of the relative change in height:
$$
\frac{dV}{V} = \frac{3\pi h^2 \, dh}{\pi h^3} = 3\frac{dh}{h}
$$
We require the percentage error in volume to be at most $1\%$:
$$
\left|\frac{dV}{V}\right| \times 100 \le 1 \implies \left|3\frac{dh}{h}\right| \times 100 \le 1 \implies \left|\frac{dh}{h}\right| \times 100 \le \frac{1}{3}\% \approx 0.33\%
$$
Thus, $h$ must be measured with a percentage error of no more than **$\dfrac{1}{3}\% \approx 0.33\%$**. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 62: Reading Derivatives from Graphs by Local Straightness (Magnification)

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Explain how the property that differentiable curves flatten out when magnified can be used to estimate the derivative of a curve at a particular point directly from its graph.

**Solution:** Because a differentiable function $y = f(x)$ is locally well-approximated by its linearization $L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$ near $x = a$, zooming in repeatedly on the point $(a, f(a))$ makes the graph of $f(x)$ appear straighter and straighter until it is visually indistinguishable from its tangent line. Once the magnified curve looks like a straight line, we can read off the coordinates of any two points on the zoomed-in segment, compute their slope $\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$ to estimate $f'(a)$, and use the point-slope formula to write down the approximate equation of the tangent line! $\blacksquare$

### Problem 64: Why the Linearization Is the Best Linear Approximation

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Suppose that $y = f(x)$ is differentiable at $x = a$ and that $g(x) = m(x - a) + c$ is a general linear function in which $m$ and $c$ are constants. If the error $E(x) = f(x) - g(x)$ were small enough near $x = a$, we might think of using $g(x)$ as a linear approximation of $f$ instead of the linearization $L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$. Show that if we impose on $g$ the two natural accuracy conditions:
> 1. $E(a) = 0$ (the approximation error is zero right at the center $x = a$), and
> 2. $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} \frac{E(x)}{x - a} = 0$ (the error is negligible even when compared with the small displacement $x - a$),
> 
> then $g(x)$ is forced to be $L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$. Thus, the linearization $L(x)$ is the **only** linear approximation whose error is both zero at $x = a$ and negligible in comparison with $x - a$.

**Solution:** Let us apply the two conditions to $E(x) = f(x) - g(x) = f(x) - \big(m(x - a) + c\big)$ one at a time:
1. **Applying Condition 1 ($E(a) = 0$):** Evaluating $E(x)$ at $x = a$ gives
   $$
   E(a) = f(a) - m(a - a) - c = 0 \implies \boxed{c = f(a)}
   $$
2. **Applying Condition 2 ($\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} \frac{E(x)}{x - a} = 0$):** Substituting $c = f(a)$ into $E(x)$ and dividing by $x - a$ yields
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \lim_{x \to a} \frac{E(x)}{x - a} &= \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - \big(f(a) + m(x - a)\big)}{x - a} \\
     &= \lim_{x \to a} \left[\frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a} - \frac{m(x - a)}{x - a}\right] \\
     &= \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a} - m = f'(a) - m
   \end{aligned}
   $$
   Setting this limit equal to $0$ forces $f'(a) - m = 0 \implies \boxed{m = f'(a)}$.

Therefore, $g(x) = m(x - a) + c = f'(a)(x - a) + f(a) = L(x)$, proving that the tangent line $L(x)$ is the unique best linear approximation to $f(x)$ at $x = a$. $\blacksquare$

# 3.8 Newton's Method

We all know the quadratic formula for solving $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$, and during the 16th century Italian algebraists discovered analogous (though much messier) radical formulas for the roots of general cubic (degree 3) and quartic (degree 4) polynomials. For nearly three centuries, mathematicians assumed that someone would eventually find a radical formula for quintic (degree 5) and higher-degree polynomials as well.

Then, in the early 19th century, Niels Henrik Abel proved a stunning impossibility theorem: **no general formula using only arithmetic operations and radicals exists for the roots of polynomials of degree $5$ or higher**! Shortly afterward, Évariste Galois explained the deep structural reason why using the symmetries of roots—giving birth to modern **group theory**.

Because exact algebraic formulas do not exist for general polynomials of degree $\ge 5$ (nor for transcendental equations like $\cos x = x$), how do we find their roots in practice? We turn to **numerical methods**! One of the fastest and most celebrated root-finding algorithms in all of mathematics is **Newton's Method** (also called the **Newton–Raphson Method**), which applies **linearization iteratively** to zoom in on a root of $f(x) = 0$.

## The Newton–Raphson Iteration

How does Newton's Method work? Suppose we want to find a root $r$ where $f(r) = 0$, and we start with a reasonable initial guess $x_0$. If we replace the curve $y = f(x)$ with its tangent line at $(x_0, f(x_0))$, where does that tangent line cross the $x$-axis? Since the tangent line hugs the curve closely near $x_0$, its $x$-intercept $x_1$ will typically be much closer to the true root $r$ than $x_0$ was! We then repeat the exact same tangent-line step from $x_1$ to get $x_2$, from $x_2$ to get $x_3$, and so on.

> [!summary] Procedure: Newton's Method for Approximating Roots of $f(x) = 0$
> 1. **Guess a first approximation $x_0$** to a root of the equation $f(x) = 0$ (sketching a graph of $y = f(x)$ or checking where $f(x)$ changes sign helps choose a good starting point).
> 2. **Iterate using the tangent-intercept formula:** Use $x_n$ to compute the next approximation $x_{n+1}$ via the recurrence
>    $$
>    x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} \qquad \big(\text{provided } f'(x_n) \neq 0\big)
>    $$

**Derivation of Newton's Formula:** Let us derive the recurrence formula for $x_{n+1}$ from the geometry of the tangent line at $(x_n, f(x_n))$. The tangent line to $y = f(x)$ at $x = x_n$ has slope $f'(x_n)$, so its point-slope equation is
$$
y - f(x_n) = f'(x_n)(x - x_n)
$$
We define $x_{n+1}$ to be the point where this tangent line crosses the $x$-axis (i.e., where $y = 0$). Setting $x = x_{n+1}$ and $y = 0$ in the tangent equation gives
$$
0 - f(x_n) = f'(x_n)(x_{n+1} - x_n)
$$
Provided $f'(x_n) \neq 0$, we can divide by $f'(x_n)$ and solve for $x_{n+1}$:
$$
x_{n+1} - x_n = -\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} \implies \boxed{x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}}
$$
$\blacksquare$

Geometrically, as illustrated in the diagram below, each step slides up (or down) vertically from $x_n$ to the curve at $(x_n, f(x_n))$ and then rides the tangent line back to the $x$-axis at $x_{n+1}$, rapidly homing in on the root.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260924231244.webp)

> [!warning] Limitations and Pitfalls: When Can Newton's Method Fail?
> Although Newton's Method usually converges with breathtaking speed (often doubling the number of correct digits with every single step!) when started close to a simple root, several things can go wrong:
> 1. **Zero Derivative ($f'(x_n) = 0$):** If our iterate $x_n$ lands on a point where the tangent line is horizontal ($f'(x_n) = 0$), the tangent line never crosses the $x$-axis and we cannot divide by $f'(x_n)$. In that case, the algorithm stops and we must restart with a different initial guess $x_0$.
> 2. **Multiple Roots ($f(r) = 0$ and $f'(r) = 0$ Coincide):** If the root $r$ itself is also a root of $f'(x) = 0$ (meaning the curve is tangent to the $x$-axis at $r$), dividing by $f'(x_n) \approx 0$ near the root causes numerical instability or much slower convergence. (We can check for this beforehand by finding the roots of $f'(x) = 0$ and testing whether any of them satisfy $f(x) = 0$.)
> 3. **Infinite Oscillation (2-Cycles):** Sometimes the sequence of iterates $(x_n)$ fails to converge and instead bounces back and forth between two values forever! For example, consider the odd-like root function centered at $x = r$:
>    $$
>    f(x) = \begin{cases}
>    -\sqrt{r - x}, & x < r \\
>    \sqrt{x - r}, & x \ge r
>    \end{cases}
>    $$
>    If you start Newton's Method at $x_0 = r - h$ (a distance $h > 0$ to the left of the root $r$), the tangent line shoots across the root to $x_1 = r + h$ (the exact same distance to the right of $r$), whose tangent line shoots right back to $x_2 = r - h = x_0$, trapping the iterates in the perpetual loop $x_0, x_1, x_0, x_1, \dots$ forever!
> 4. **Further Reading:** See pages 281–283 of Thomas' *Calculus* (9th ed.) for additional visual examples of convergence basins and chaos in Newton's Method.

## Problem 7: Global Minimum Criterion from One-Sided Sign of $f'$

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Suppose that $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and that $c$ is an interior point of the interval. Show that if $f'(x) \le 0$ on $[a, c)$ and $f'(x) \ge 0$ on $(c, b]$, then $f(x)$ is never less than $f(c)$ on $[a, b]$; that is,
> $$
> f(x) \ge f(c) \quad \forall x \in [a, b]
> $$

**Solution:** We prove the inequality on the left sub-interval $[a, c)$ and the right sub-interval $(c, b]$ separately using the Mean Value Theorem (at $x = c$ itself, $f(c) \ge f(c)$ holds trivially):
1. **For any $x \in [a, c)$:** Because $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $[a, c)$, the restriction of $f$ to the closed interval $[x, c]$ is continuous on $[x, c]$ and differentiable on $(x, c)$. By the Mean Value Theorem, there exists some $\alpha \in (x, c) \subset [a, c)$ such that
   $$
   \frac{f(c) - f(x)}{c - x} = f'(\alpha)
   $$
   Since $c - x > 0$ and $f'(\alpha) \le 0$ on $[a, c)$, we have $f(c) - f(x) \le 0 \implies f(x) \ge f(c)$ for all $x \in [a, c)$.
2. **For any $x \in (c, b]$:** Similarly, applying the Mean Value Theorem to $f$ on the closed interval $[c, x]$ gives some $\beta \in (c, x) \subset (c, b]$ such that
   $$
   \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} = f'(\beta)
   $$
   Since $x - c > 0$ and $f'(\beta) \ge 0$ on $(c, b]$, we have $f(x) - f(c) \ge 0 \implies f(x) \ge f(c)$ for all $x \in (c, b]$.

Combining both halves with $x = c$ shows that $f(x) \ge f(c)$ for every $x \in [a, b]$. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 8: Bounding Function Differences via a Bounded Derivative

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> **(a)** Show that
> $$
> -\frac{1}{2} \le \frac{x}{1 + x^2} \le \frac{1}{2}
> $$
> for all real $x$.
> 
> **(b)** Suppose that $f$ is a function whose derivative is $f'(x) = \dfrac{x}{1 + x^2}$. Use the result in **(a)** to show that
> $$
> |f(b) - f(a)| \le \frac{1}{2}|b - a|
> $$
> for any real numbers $a$ and $b$.

**Solution:**
**(a)** Let $g(x) = \dfrac{x}{1 + x^2}$, which is continuous and differentiable for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$. Differentiating via the Quotient Rule gives
$$
g'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{x}{1 + x^2}\right) = \frac{(1 + x^2)(1) - x(2x)}{(1 + x^2)^2} = \frac{1 - x^2}{(1 + x^2)^2}
$$
Setting $g'(x) = 0$ gives the critical points $x = -1$ and $x = 1$. Since $g(x) \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$, $g(-1) = -\dfrac{1}{2}$ is the absolute minimum and $g(1) = \dfrac{1}{2}$ is the absolute maximum of $g$ on $\mathbb{R}$. Thus,
$$
-\frac{1}{2} \le \frac{x}{1 + x^2} \le \frac{1}{2} \quad \forall x \in \mathbb{R}
$$
**(b)** Since $f'(x) = \dfrac{x}{1 + x^2}$ exists for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$, $f$ is differentiable (and hence continuous) on the closed interval between any two real numbers $a$ and $b$ (if $a = b$, the inequality $0 \le 0$ holds trivially). By the Mean Value Theorem, there exists a point $c$ between $a$ and $b$ such that
$$
\frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a} = f'(c) = \frac{c}{1 + c^2}
$$
Taking absolute values and applying the bound $|f'(c)| = \left|\dfrac{c}{1 + c^2}\right| \le \dfrac{1}{2}$ from part **(a)** yields
$$
\left|\frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}\right| \le \frac{1}{2} \implies \boxed{|f(b) - f(a)| \le \frac{1}{2}|b - a|}
$$
$\blacksquare$

## Problem 10: Local Maxima and Inflection Points of a Product $h = fg$

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Let $h = fg$ be the product of two differentiable functions of $x$.
> **(a)** If $f$ and $g$ are positive, with local maxima at $x = a$, and if $f'$ and $g'$ change sign at $a$, does $h$ have a local maximum at $x = a$?
> **(b)** If the graphs of $f$ and $g$ have inflection points at $x = a$, does the graph of $h$ necessarily have an inflection point at $x = a$?

**Solution:**
**(a)** **Yes**, $h = fg$ must have a local maximum at $x = a$. By the Product Rule,
$$
h'(x) = f(x)g'(x) + g(x)f'(x)
$$
Because $f$ and $g$ have local maxima at $x = a$ and their derivatives change sign at $a$, we know that $f'(a) = g'(a) = 0$, that $f' > 0$ and $g' > 0$ immediately to the left of $a$ ($x < a$), and that $f' < 0$ and $g' < 0$ immediately to the right of $a$ ($x > a$). Since $f(x) > 0$ and $g(x) > 0$ are strictly positive:
- For $x < a$ near $a$: $h' = fg' + gf' > 0$ (sum of two positive products).
- At $x = a$: $h'(a) = f(a)(0) + g(a)(0) = 0$.
- For $x > a$ near $a$: $h' = fg' + gf' < 0$ (sum of two negative products).

Since $h'$ changes sign from positive to negative at $x = a$, the First Derivative Test guarantees that $h$ has a **local maximum at $x = a$**.

**(b)** **No**, $h = fg$ does **not** necessarily have a point of inflection at $x = a$. For a concrete counterexample, take $f(x) = \sin x$ and $g(x) = \sin 2x$ at $a = 0$. Both $f(x) = \sin x$ and $g(x) = \sin 2x$ have a point of inflection at $x = 0$ (since $f''(x) = -\sin x$ and $g''(x) = -4\sin 2x$ both change sign at $x = 0$). However, differentiating their product $h = fg$ twice via the Product Rule gives
$$
h'' = fg'' + 2f'g' + gf''
$$
Evaluating $h''(0)$ at $x = 0$, where $f(0) = g(0) = 0$ and $f''(0) = g''(0) = 0$, we obtain
$$
h''(0) = 0 + 2f'(0)g'(0) + 0 = 2(\cos 0)(2\cos 0) = 4 > 0
$$
Because $h''$ is continuous and $h''(0) = 4 > 0$, $h''(x)$ remains strictly positive throughout an open interval around $x = 0$ (it does not change sign!), so $h = \sin x \sin 2x$ is concave up on both sides of $0$ and has **no point of inflection at $x = 0$**. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 13: Maximizing the Perimeter of a Triangle Inscribed in a Semicircle

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Points $A$ and $B$ lie at the ends of a diameter of a circle of radius $r$, and point $C$ lies on the circumference. Is it true that the perimeter of triangle $ABC$ is largest when the triangle is isosceles?

![](Pasted%20image%2020260924233045.webp)

**Solution:** **Yes**, it is true! Because $AB = 2r$ is a diameter of the circle, Thales' theorem guarantees that $\angle ACB = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ is a right angle. Let $\angle CAB = \theta \in \left(0, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)$. Then the two legs of the right triangle $\triangle ABC$ have lengths
$$
AC = 2r\cos\theta, \qquad BC = 2r\sin\theta
$$
Therefore, the perimeter $P(\theta)$ of $\triangle ABC$ is
$$
P(\theta) = AB + AC + BC = 2r(1 + \cos\theta + \sin\theta), \quad \theta \in \left(0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)
$$
Differentiating $P(\theta)$ with respect to $\theta$ and setting $\dfrac{dP}{d\theta} = 0$ gives
$$
\frac{dP}{d\theta} = 2r(-\sin\theta + \cos\theta) = 0 \implies \cos\theta = \sin\theta \implies \theta = \frac{\pi}{4}
$$
Since $P''(\pi/4) = 2r(-\cos(\pi/4) - \sin(\pi/4)) = -2\sqrt{2}r < 0$, the perimeter is maximized when $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$, which makes $AC = BC = \sqrt{2}r$ and therefore makes $\triangle ABC$ **isosceles**. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 14: The Corridor Ladder Problem (Two Methods)

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> What is the approximate length (in feet) of the longest ladder you can carry horizontally around the corner of the corridor shown here (where one hallway is $8\text{ ft}$ wide and the perpendicular hallway is $6\text{ ft}$ wide)? Round your answer down to the nearest foot.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260924233545.webp)

**Solution:** We present two complete methods for solving this classic optimization problem: the standard trigonometric method (minimizing the corner-touching line length) and an algebraic coordinate-geometry method.

### Method 1: Minimizing the Corner-Touching Secant Segment

Why does finding the *longest* ladder that can get around the corner require finding a *minimum* length? Imagine holding a telescoping rod touching both outer walls at a fixed angle $\theta \in (0, \pi/2)$ with the $x$-axis, and extending it until it hits the inner corner at $(8, 6)$. That corner-touching length $L(\theta)$ is the maximum rod length that can fit at angle $\theta$. Because the ladder must pass through *every* angle $\theta$ as it turns the corner, the longest rigid ladder that can clear the turn without getting stuck is equal to the **minimum value of $L(\theta)$** over all angles $\theta \in (0, \pi/2)$!

![](Pasted%20image%2020260925110820.webp)

From the two right triangles meeting at the inner corner $(8, 6)$, the segments of the line have lengths $s_1 = \dfrac{8}{\cos\theta} = 8\sec\theta$ and $s_2 = \dfrac{6}{\sin\theta} = 6\csc\theta$, giving a total length of
$$
L(\theta) = s_1 + s_2 = \frac{8}{\cos\theta} + \frac{6}{\sin\theta} = 8\sec\theta + 6\csc\theta, \quad \theta \in \left(0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)
$$
Differentiating $L(\theta)$ with respect to $\theta$ and setting $\dfrac{dL}{d\theta} = 0$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dL}{d\theta} &= 8\sec\theta\tan\theta - 6\csc\theta\cot\theta = 0 \\
\implies \frac{8\sin\theta}{\cos^2\theta} &= \frac{6\cos\theta}{\sin^2\theta} \implies \tan^3\theta = \frac{6}{8} = \frac{3}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus, at the critical angle, $\tan\theta = \left(\dfrac{3}{4}\right)^{1/3}$ and $\cot\theta = \left(\dfrac{4}{3}\right)^{1/3}$. Substituting these into $\sec\theta = \sqrt{1 + \tan^2\theta}$ and $\csc\theta = \sqrt{1 + \cot^2\theta}$ gives the minimum clearing length:
$$
\begin{aligned}
L_{\min} &= 8\sqrt{1 + \tan^2\theta} + 6\sqrt{1 + \cot^2\theta} \\
         &= 8\left(1 + \left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{2/3}\right)^{1/2} + 6\left(1 + \left(\frac{4}{3}\right)^{2/3}\right)^{1/2} \approx 19.73\text{ ft}
\end{aligned}
$$
Rounding down to the nearest foot, the longest ladder that can make the turn is **$19\text{ ft}$**.

### Method 2: Algebraic Envelope via Intercept Equation

Alternatively, suppose a ladder of fixed length $l$ touches the outer walls ($x$- and $y$-axes) at $(a, 0)$ and $(0, b)$, where $a^2 + b^2 = l^2 \implies b = \sqrt{l^2 - a^2}$. The equation of the line segment representing the ladder is
$$
\frac{x}{a} + \frac{y}{b} = 1 \iff bx + ay - ab = 0
$$
At the inner corner's $x$-coordinate $x = 8$ (for $a > 8$), the height $y$ of the ladder above the horizontal outer wall is
$$
y(a) = \frac{ab - 8b}{a} = \frac{(a - 8)\sqrt{l^2 - a^2}}{a} \qquad \text{(i)}
$$
For the ladder to clear the inner corner at $(8, 6)$ at its tightest position, even the *maximum* height $y(a)$ that the ladder can reach at $x = 8$ must not exceed $6$ (wait—as the ladder slides with its ends on the positive axes, $(8, 6)$ lies in the region $y \ge y(a)$, so to avoid hitting $(8, 6)$ from the inside of the turn, the peak height $y_{\max}$ of the curve of positions at $x = 8$ must satisfy $y_{\max} \le 6$). Differentiating $y(a)$ with respect to $a$ and setting $y'(a) = 0$:
$$
y'(a) = \frac{-a^3 + 8l^2}{a^2\sqrt{l^2 - a^2}} = 0 \implies a^3 = 8l^2 \implies a = 2l^{2/3}
$$
Substituting $a = 2l^{2/3}$ back into equation **(i)** and requiring $y \le 6$:
$$
y_{\max} = \frac{\big(2l^{2/3} - 8\big)\sqrt{l^2 - 4l^{4/3}}}{2l^{2/3}} \le 6
$$
Letting $k = l^{2/3}$ (so $l^2 = k^3$ and $l^{4/3} = k^2$), this simplifies cleanly to
$$
\frac{2(k - 4)\sqrt{k^3 - 4k^2}}{2k} = (k - 4)\sqrt{k - 4} = (k - 4)^{3/2} \le 6
$$
Solving for $k$ and then $l = k^{3/2}$ yields
$$
k - 4 \le 6^{2/3} \implies k \le 4 + 6^{2/3} = 2^{2/3} + 3^{2/3}\cdot 2^{2/3} \implies l \le \left(4 + 6^{2/3}\right)^{3/2} = \left(8^{2/3} + 6^{2/3}\right)^{3/2} \approx 19.73\text{ ft}
$$
Once again, rounding down to the nearest foot gives **$19\text{ ft}$**! $\blacksquare$

## Problem 16: Optimal Distance for Kicking a Ball Through Goalposts (Regiomontanus's Problem)

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> From the figure below, find the distance $h$ down the field along a line perpendicular to the goal line that gives the player the maximum kicking angle $\beta$ between the goalposts (located at distances $a$ and $a + b$ from the player's line of motion).

![](Pasted%20image%2020260925111425.webp)

**Solution:** From the two right triangles in the diagram, we have
$$
\tan(\theta + \beta) = \frac{a + b}{h}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{a}{h}
$$
Expanding $\tan(\theta + \beta)$ using the tangent addition formula and substituting $\tan\theta = \dfrac{a}{h}$ gives
$$
\frac{\tan\theta + \tan\beta}{1 - \tan\theta\tan\beta} = \frac{a + b}{h} \implies \frac{\dfrac{a}{h} + \tan\beta}{1 - \dfrac{a}{h}\tan\beta} = \frac{a + b}{h}
$$
Multiplying numerator and denominator of the left-hand side by $h$ and cross-multiplying:
$$
\begin{aligned}
a + h\tan\beta &= (a + b)\left(1 - \frac{a}{h}\tan\beta\right) = a + b - \frac{a(a + b)}{h}\tan\beta \\
\implies \left(h + \frac{a(a + b)}{h}\right)\tan\beta &= b \implies \tan\beta = \frac{bh}{h^2 + a(a + b)}
\end{aligned}
$$
This function is defined for all $h \in [0, \infty)$. Because the angle $\beta$ lies in the acute interval $\left(0, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)$ where $\tan\beta$ is strictly increasing, maximizing $\beta$ is equivalent to maximizing $\tan\beta$! Differentiating $\tan\beta$ with respect to $h$ via the Quotient Rule and setting the derivative equal to $0$:
$$
\frac{d(\tan\beta)}{dh} = \frac{\big(h^2 + a^2 + ab\big)b - (bh)(2h)}{(h^2 + a^2 + ab)^2} = \frac{b\big(a^2 + ab - h^2\big)}{(h^2 + a^2 + ab)^2} = 0
$$
Solving $a^2 + ab - h^2 = 0$ for $h > 0$ yields the unique critical point
$$
\boxed{h = \sqrt{a(a + b)}}
$$
Notice that $\dfrac{d(\tan\beta)}{dh} > 0$ for $0 < h < \sqrt{a(a + b)}$ and $\dfrac{d(\tan\beta)}{dh} < 0$ for $h > \sqrt{a(a + b)}$ (and at the endpoint $h = 0$ as well as $h \to \infty$, $\tan\beta = 0$). Therefore, $h = \sqrt{a(a + b)}$ (the geometric mean of the distances $a$ and $a + b$ to the two goalposts!) gives the **absolute maximum kicking angle $\beta$**. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 17: A Max-Min Problem with a Variable Answer (Cylinder Inscribed in a Cone)

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Sometimes the solution of a max-min problem depends on the proportions of the shapes involved. Suppose that a right circular cylinder of radius $r$ and height $h$ is inscribed in a right circular cone of radius $R$ and height $H$, as shown here. Find the value of $r$ (in terms of $R$ and $H$) that maximizes the **total surface area** of the cylinder (including top and bottom).

![](Pasted%20image%2020260925113106.webp)

**Solution:** To maximize the surface area of the cylinder for any given radius $r \in [0, R]$, the top rim of the cylinder must touch the slanted wall of the cone. From the similar right triangles in the cross-sectional diagram above, we have
$$
\tan\theta = \frac{H}{R} = \frac{h}{R - r} = \frac{H - h}{r} \implies h = \frac{H}{R}(R - r)
$$
Substituting $h = \dfrac{H}{R}(R - r)$ into the total surface area of the cylinder (top and bottom circular disks $2\pi r^2$ plus lateral side wall $2\pi rh$) expresses $A$ as a function of $r$ on the closed domain $[0, R]$:
$$
A(r) = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi rh = 2\pi r^2 + \frac{2\pi H}{R}r(R - r) = 2\pi\left(1 - \frac{H}{R}\right)r^2 + 2\pi Hr, \quad r \in [0, R]
$$
First, notice the special degenerate linear case **$H = R$**: if $H = R$, the $r^2$ term vanishes completely and $A(r) = (2\pi R)r$, which is a strictly increasing linear function on $[0, R]$ whose maximum occurs at the right endpoint $r = R$ (where $h = 0$ and $A_{\max} = 2\pi R^2$).

Now suppose **$H \neq R$**, so $A(r)$ is a quadratic function (parabola) in $r$. Differentiating $A(r)$ twice with respect to $r$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dA}{dr} &= 4\pi r + \frac{2\pi H}{R}(R - 2r) = 0 \implies 2Rr + HR - 2Hr = 0 \implies r_{\text{vertex}} = \frac{HR}{2(H - R)} \\
\frac{d^2A}{dr^2} &= 4\pi - \frac{4\pi H}{R} = \frac{4\pi(R - H)}{R}
\end{aligned}
$$
Because $r$ is restricted to the physical domain $[0, R]$, the location of the absolute maximum of $A(r)$ on $[0, R]$ depends on the concavity of the parabola and whether the vertex $r_{\text{vertex}} = \dfrac{HR}{2(H - R)}$ falls inside $[0, R]$! This splits our analysis into three geometric cases:

1. **Case I ($H < R$):** Here $\dfrac{d^2A}{dr^2} = \dfrac{4\pi(R - H)}{R} > 0$, so the parabola $A(r)$ is **concave up**, and its vertex $r_{\text{vertex}} = \dfrac{HR}{2(H - R)} < 0$ lies to the left of the domain $[0, R]$. Throughout $[0, R]$, $A(r)$ is strictly increasing, so it attains its maximum at the right endpoint **$r = R$**, with $A_{\max} = A(R) = 2\pi R^2$.
2. **Case II ($H > R$, when is the vertex inside $[0, R]$?):** When $H > R$, we have $\dfrac{d^2A}{dr^2} < 0$, so the parabola $A(r)$ is **concave down** and its vertex $r_{\text{vertex}} > 0$ is the global maximum of the unrestricted parabola. When does $r_{\text{vertex}}$ lie inside the domain $[0, R]$?
   $$
   \frac{HR}{2(H - R)} \le R \iff H \le 2(H - R) = 2H - 2R \iff \boxed{H \ge 2R}
   $$
   Thus, whenever **$H \ge 2R$**, the vertex lies inside $[0, R]$ and gives the maximum surface area at **$r = \dfrac{HR}{2(H - R)}$**, with maximum value:
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   A_{\max} &= 2\pi\left(1 - \frac{H}{R}\right)\frac{H^2 R^2}{4(H - R)^2} + 2\pi H\left(\frac{HR}{2(H - R)}\right) \\
            &= 2\pi\left[\frac{H^2 R^2 - H^3 R + 2H^3 R - 2H^2 R^2}{4(H - R)^2}\right] \\
            &= \frac{\pi}{2}\frac{H^2 R(H - R)}{(H - R)^2} = \boxed{\frac{\pi H^2 R}{2(H - R)}}
   \end{aligned}
   $$
3. **Case III ($R < H < 2R$):** When $R < H < 2R$, the parabola is concave down, but the vertex $r_{\text{vertex}} = \dfrac{HR}{2(H - R)} > R$ lies strictly to the *right* of the domain $[0, R]$. Because $[0, R]$ lies entirely to the left of the concave-down vertex, $A(r)$ is strictly increasing on $[0, R]$ and again attains its maximum at the right endpoint **$r = R$**, with $A_{\max} = 2\pi R^2$.

**Final Verdict Summary:** Combining all cases, the radius $r$ that maximizes the total surface area of the inscribed cylinder is:
- **If $H < 2R$:** $A_{\max} = 2\pi R^2$, achieved at the degenerate flat disk **$r = R$** ($h = 0$).
- **If $H \ge 2R$:** $A_{\max} = \dfrac{\pi H^2 R}{2(H - R)}$, achieved at **$r = \dfrac{HR}{2(H - R)}$**. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 17b (Section 3.2): Apparent Contradiction to Corollary 2 of the MVT

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Show that
> $$
> \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{x}{x + 1}\right) = \frac{d}{dx}\left(-\frac{1}{x + 1}\right)
> $$
> even though $\dfrac{x}{x + 1} \neq -\dfrac{1}{x + 1}$. Doesn't this contradict Corollary 2 of the Mean Value Theorem?

**Solution:** First, let us verify that the two derivatives are indeed identical for all $x \neq -1$:
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{x}{x + 1}\right) = \frac{(x + 1)(1) - x(1)}{(x + 1)^2} = \frac{1}{(x + 1)^2}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\left(-\frac{1}{x + 1}\right) = \frac{1}{(x + 1)^2}
$$
Why doesn't this contradict Corollary 2? Because Corollary 2 does *not* say that two functions with the same derivative must be equal—it says that they must **differ by a constant**! Indeed, if we rewrite $-\dfrac{1}{x + 1}$ by adding and subtracting $x$ in the numerator, we see that
$$
-\frac{1}{x + 1} = \frac{-1 - x + x}{x + 1} = -\frac{x + 1}{x + 1} + \frac{x}{x + 1} = -1 + \frac{x}{x + 1}
$$
Thus, $\dfrac{x}{x + 1}$ and $-\dfrac{1}{x + 1}$ differ by the constant $C = 1$ on each interval of their domain, in complete agreement with Corollary 2! $\blacksquare$

# Appendix: Supplementary Real Analysis Theorems

In Section 3.1, our rigorous proof of the Extreme Value Theorem (Theorem 1) relied on the **Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem**, which in turn rests on the **Nested Interval Theorem**. Here we provide the complete proofs of these foundational real-analysis theorems, along with Lebesgue's theorem on the differentiability of monotonic functions.

## Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem Proof

> [!theorem] Theorem: The Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem
> Every bounded infinite sequence of real numbers has a convergent subsequence.

**Proof (Method of Bisection):** Let $(x_n)$ be a bounded sequence of real numbers, so that all of its terms $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n, \dots$ lie inside some closed, bounded interval $[a, b]$. We construct a convergent subsequence by repeatedly bisecting $[a, b]$:
1. Divide $[a, b]$ into two equal closed halves, $\left[a, \dfrac{a + b}{2}\right]$ and $\left[\dfrac{a + b}{2}, b\right]$. Because the sequence has infinitely many indices $n \in \mathbb{N}$, at least one of these two half-intervals must contain $x_n$ for infinitely many indices $n$. Call that half-interval $I_1$.
2. Next, bisect $I_1$ into two equal closed halves. Again, at least one half must contain $x_n$ for infinitely many indices $n$; call that half-interval $I_2$.
3. Continuing this bisection process inductively produces a nested sequence of closed intervals
   $$
   I_1 \supseteq I_2 \supseteq I_3 \supseteq I_4 \supseteq \cdots
   $$
   where each $I_k$ contains $x_n$ for infinitely many indices $n$, and the length of $I_k$ shrinks exponentially to $0$:
   $$
   \text{length}(I_k) = \frac{b - a}{2^k} \to 0 \quad \text{as } k \to \infty
   $$
4. By the **Nested Interval Theorem** (proved below), there is a unique real number $L \in [a, b]$ that belongs to every single interval $I_k$ ($L \in I_k$ for all $k \in \mathbb{N}$).
5. Now we select our subsequence $(x_{n_k})$ inductively:
   - Pick any term $x_{n_1} \in I_1$.
   - Since $I_2$ contains terms $x_n$ for infinitely many indices $n$, we can choose an index $n_2 > n_1$ such that $x_{n_2} \in I_2$.
   - In general, having chosen $x_{n_{k-1}} \in I_{k-1}$, we choose an index $n_k > n_{k-1}$ such that $x_{n_k} \in I_k$.
6. Because both $x_{n_k}$ and $L$ lie inside the interval $I_k$ of length $\dfrac{b - a}{2^k}$, the distance between them satisfies
   $$
   0 \le |x_{n_k} - L| \le \frac{b - a}{2^k}
   $$
   Taking the limit as $k \to \infty$, since $\dfrac{b - a}{2^k} \to 0$, the Sandwich Theorem forces $x_{n_k} \to L$. Thus, $(x_{n_k})$ is a convergent subsequence of $(x_n)$. $\blacksquare$

## The Nested Interval Theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem: The Nested Interval Theorem
> Let $I_n = [a_n, b_n]$ be a sequence of closed, bounded intervals that are nested:
> $$
> I_1 \supseteq I_2 \supseteq I_3 \supseteq \cdots
> $$
> Then there is at least one real number $x$ common to all of the intervals ($x \in I_n$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$). Furthermore, if the lengths of the intervals $b_n - a_n$ approach $0$ as $n \to \infty$, then this common point $x$ is **unique**.

**Proof:**
1. **Existence of a Common Point:** Because $I_1 \supseteq I_2 \supseteq I_3 \supseteq \cdots$, the left endpoints form a non-decreasing sequence and the right endpoints form a non-increasing sequence:
   $$
   a_1 \le a_2 \le a_3 \le \cdots \le a_n \le b_n \le \cdots \le b_3 \le b_2 \le b_1
   $$
   Consider the set of all left endpoints $S = \{a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n, \dots\}$ and the set of all right endpoints $T = \{b_1, b_2, \dots, b_n, \dots\}$. Every element of $S$ is less than or equal to every element of $T$ ($a_i \le b_j$ for all $i, j \in \mathbb{N}$).

   Thus, $S$ is a non-empty set of real numbers that is bounded from above (by every $b_j \in T$). By the **Completeness Axiom** of the real numbers, $S$ has a least upper bound (supremum); let $x = \sup S$.
   - Since $x$ is an upper bound for $S$, we have $a_n \le x$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.
   - Since each $b_n \in T$ is an upper bound for $S$, and $x = \sup S$ is the *least* upper bound of $S$, we have $x \le b_n$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

   Combining these two inequalities gives $a_n \le x \le b_n$, meaning $x \in [a_n, b_n] = I_n$ for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$!

2. **Uniqueness When $b_n - a_n \to 0$:** Suppose $\lim_{n \to \infty} (b_n - a_n) = 0$, and suppose both $x$ and $y$ belong to $I_n = [a_n, b_n]$ for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$. Then for every $n$, the distance between $x$ and $y$ cannot exceed the length of the interval $I_n$:
   $$
   0 \le |x - y| \le b_n - a_n \quad \forall n \in \mathbb{N}
   $$
   Taking the limit as $n \to \infty$, since $b_n - a_n \to 0$, we obtain $|x - y| = 0 \implies x = y$. Therefore, the common point $x$ is unique. $\blacksquare$

## Lebesgue's Monotonicity Theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem: Lebesgue's Monotonicity Theorem
> Any monotonic (increasing or decreasing) function on an interval $[a, b]$ is **differentiable almost everywhere** on $[a, b]$ (that is, the set of points in $[a, b]$ where the function fails to be differentiable has Lebesgue measure zero).
