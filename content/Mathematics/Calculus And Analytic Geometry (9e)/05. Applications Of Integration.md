# 5.1 Areas Between Curves

## Approximating and Defining Area Between Curves

Suppose we want to find the area of a plane region that is bounded above by the curve $y = f(x)$, below by the curve $y = g(x)$, and on the left and right by the vertical lines $x = a$ and $x = b$. How can we turn this geometric question into a calculus problem? Just as we did when finding the area under a single curve, we can start by slicing the region into thin vertical strips and approximating each strip with a rectangle.

Suppose $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ are both continuous functions on the closed interval $[a, b]$ with $f(x) \geq g(x)$ throughout $[a, b]$, so the graph of $f$ always lies on or above the graph of $g$. Let $P$ be a partition of $[a, b]$ into $n$ subintervals. Within the $k$-th subinterval $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$, we pick any sample point $c_k$. The vertical rectangle over this subinterval has a height equal to the vertical distance between the curves, $f(c_k) - g(c_k)$, and a width of $\Delta x_k$.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926124818.webp)

Summing the areas of all these individual rectangles gives us a Riemann sum that approximates the total area $A$ of the region:
$$
A \approx \sum_{k=1}^n [f(c_k) - g(c_k)] \, \Delta x_k
$$
Because both $f$ and $g$ are continuous on $[a, b]$, their difference $f(x) - g(x)$ is also continuous on $[a, b]$. Consequently, as we refine the partition so that the norm $\lvert P \rvert \to 0$, the Riemann sum converges to a single definite integral:
$$
A = \lim_{\lvert P \rvert \to 0} \sum_{k=1}^n [f(c_k) - g(c_k)] \, \Delta x_k = \int_a^b [f(x) - g(x)] \, dx
$$
This leads directly to our formal definition for the area between two curves.

> [!abstract] Definition: Area Between Curves
> If $f$ and $g$ are continuous functions with $f(x) \geq g(x)$ throughout the interval $[a, b]$, then the area $A$ of the region bounded above by $y = f(x)$, below by $y = g(x)$, and on the sides by the vertical lines $x = a$ and $x = b$ is the integral of $(f - g)$ from $a$ to $b$:
> $$
> A = \int_a^b [f(x) - g(x)] \, dx
> $$

In plain terms, the integrand $f(x) - g(x)$ represents the height of a typical vertical cross-sectional strip at position $x$—often remembered simply as $\text{upper curve} - \text{lower curve}$—and the integral accumulates these infinitely thin strips from $x = a$ to $x = b$.

> [!example] Example: Area Between Explicit Vertical Bounds
> Find the area of the region bounded between the curves $y = \sec^2 x$ and $y = \sin x$ from $x = 0$ to $x = \frac{\pi}{4}$.

**Solution:**
On the interval $\left[0, \frac{\pi}{4}\right]$, the curve $f(x) = \sec^2 x$ lies above $g(x) = \sin x$. Applying the area formula directly, we integrate the difference between the upper and lower functions:
$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \int_0^{\pi/4} [\sec^2 x - \sin x] \, dx \\
  &= [\tan x + \cos x]_0^{\pi/4} \\
  &= \left[1 + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right] - [0 + 1] \\
  &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

## Curves That Intersect

What happens if the vertical boundary lines $x = a$ and $x = b$ are not explicitly given? Frequently, a region is completely enclosed by two curves that cross one another. In such cases, the $x$-coordinates of the points of intersection form the natural left and right limits of integration. To find them, we set the two bounding equations equal to each other and solve for $x$.

> [!example] Example: Area Enclosed by Intersecting Curves
> Find the area of the region enclosed by the parabola $y = 2 - x^2$ and the line $y = -x$.

**Solution:**
First, we need to determine where the parabola $y = 2 - x^2$ and the line $y = -x$ intersect so we know our limits of integration.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926125020.webp)

Equating the two expressions for $y$, we solve for $x$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
2 - x^2 &= -x \\
x^2 - x - 2 &= 0 \\
(x - 2)(x + 1) &= 0 \implies x = -1, \, 2
\end{aligned}
$$
Over the interval $[-1, 2]$, the parabola $f(x) = 2 - x^2$ forms the upper boundary and the line $g(x) = -x$ forms the lower boundary. Integrating their difference from $x = -1$ to $x = 2$ yields:
$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \int_{-1}^2 [(2 - x^2) - (-x)] \, dx \\
  &= \left[2x - \frac{x^3}{3} + \frac{x^2}{2}\right]_{-1}^2 \\
  &= \left[4 - \frac{8}{3} + 2\right] - \left[-2 + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{2}\right] \\
  &= \frac{4}{3} + 2 + 2 - \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{2} \\
  &= 5 - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{9}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

## Boundaries with Changing Formulas

Sometimes the upper or lower boundary of a region is not described by a single formula across the entire interval, but instead switches from one curve to another at a corner point. When the formula for a bounding curve changes at one or more points, we cannot use a single integral with a single integrand. Instead, we partition the region into vertical subregions that correspond to each formula change, apply the area integral to each subregion separately, and add the resulting areas together.

> [!example] Example: Region with a Split Lower Boundary
> Find the area of the region in the first quadrant that is bounded above by $y = \sqrt{x}$ and below by the $x$-axis and the line $y = x - 2$.

**Solution:**
Let's examine the boundaries of this region as $x$ moves from left to right.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926125332.webp)

The upper boundary is always $y = \sqrt{x}$. However, the lower boundary is the $x$-axis ($y = 0$) from $x = 0$ to $x = 2$, and then switches to the line $y = x - 2$ from $x = 2$ to the intersection point at $x = 4$ (where $\sqrt{4} = 4 - 2 = 2$). We therefore split the region at $x = 2$ into two subregions with areas $A_1$ and $A_2$.

For the left subregion on $[0, 2]$:
$$
A_1 = \int_0^2 \sqrt{x} \, dx = \left[\frac{x^{3/2}}{3/2}\right]_0^2 = \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3}
$$
For the right subregion on $[2, 4]$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
A_2 &= \int_2^4 [\sqrt{x} - (x - 2)] \, dx \\
    &= \left[\frac{x^{3/2}}{3/2} - \frac{x^2}{2} + 2x\right]_2^4 \\
    &= \left(\frac{16}{3} - \frac{16}{2} + 8\right) - \left(\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3} - 2 + 4\right) \\
    &= \frac{16}{3} - \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3} - 2 \\
    &= \frac{10}{3} - \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$
Adding the two sub-areas together gives the total area of the region:
$$
A = A_1 + A_2 = \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3} + \frac{10}{3} - \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3} = \frac{10}{3}
$$
$\blacksquare$

## Integration with Respect to $y$

Notice how the previous example required two separate integrals because the bottom boundary changed formula at $x = 2$. Could we avoid splitting the region altogether? Yes—by changing our perspective and slicing the region horizontally instead of vertically!

When a region's bounding curves are more naturally described as functions of $y$ (say, $x = f(y)$ on the right and $x = g(y)$ on the left), we use horizontal approximating rectangles of thickness $\Delta y$. In this setup, the basic area formula simply replaces $x$ with $y$, integrating from the bottom bound $y = c$ to the top bound $y = d$:
$$
A = \int_c^d [f(y) - g(y)] \, dy
$$

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926125510.webp)

Here, $f(y)$ is the right-hand boundary and $g(y)$ is the left-hand boundary, so the integrand $f(y) - g(y)$ represents the horizontal length ($\text{right curve} - \text{left curve}$) of a typical horizontal strip at height $y$.

> [!example] Example: Finding Area by Integrating with Respect to $y$
> Find the area of the region in the first quadrant bounded above by $y = \sqrt{x}$ and below by the $x$-axis and the line $y = x - 2$ by integrating with respect to $y$.

**Solution:**
Let's rewrite the bounding curves as functions of $y$. The upper-left curve $y = \sqrt{x}$ becomes $x = y^2$ (for $y \geq 0$), and the right-hand line $y = x - 2$ becomes $x = y + 2$.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926125629.webp)

Notice that every horizontal strip from the bottom of the region at $y = 0$ to the top intersection point at $y = 2$ stretches consistently from the parabola $g(y) = y^2$ on the left to the line $f(y) = y + 2$ on the right. We can therefore find the area with a single integral:
$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \int_0^2 [(y + 2) - y^2] \, dy \\
  &= \left[\frac{y^2}{2} + 2y - \frac{y^3}{3}\right]_0^2 \\
  &= 2 + 4 - \frac{8}{3} \\
  &= \frac{18 - 8}{3} = \frac{10}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$
We obtain the exact same result of $\frac{10}{3}$, with noticeably less algebraic effort than splitting the integral along the $x$-axis. $\blacksquare$

## Combining Integrals with Formulas from Geometry

Sometimes the fastest way to find the area of a region is to combine calculus with elementary geometry rather than relying purely on integration. For instance, reconsider the region from the previous two examples.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926125938.webp)

If we take the entire area under the curve $y = \sqrt{x}$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 4$, we only need to subtract the area of the unshaded right triangle below the line $y = x - 2$ on the interval $[2, 4]$. That triangle has base $4 - 2 = 2$ and height $2$, so its area is $\frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 2$. Subtracting this triangle from the single integral gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \int_0^4 \sqrt{x} \, dx - \frac{1}{2} \cdot (4 - 2) \cdot 2 \\
  &= \left[\frac{x^{3/2}}{3/2}\right]_0^4 - \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \\
  &= \frac{2}{3} \cdot 8 - 2 \\
  &= \frac{16 - 6}{3} = \frac{10}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Choosing the Fastest Method
> The most efficient way to calculate the area of a region depends entirely on the geometry of its boundaries. Any of the three approaches—vertical slicing ($dx$), horizontal slicing ($dy$), or combining integrals with geometric formulas—will yield the same area, so it pays to sketch the region first and inspect which setup avoids splitting the integral.

## 5.1 Exercises

### Problem 43: Area of a Propeller-Shaped Region

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Find the area of the propeller-shaped region enclosed by the curve $x - y^3 = 0$ and the line $y - x = 0$.

**Solution:**
The bounding curves are $x = y^3$ and $x = y$, which intersect when $y^3 = y$, or $y(y^2 - 1) = 0$, giving $y = -1, 0, 1$.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926130123.webp)

Because the curves are already simple functions of $y$ and the propeller shape is symmetric about the origin, the area $A_1$ of the upper blade in the first quadrant ($0 \leq y \leq 1$, where $y \geq y^3$) equals the area $A_2$ of the lower blade in the third quadrant. Computing $A_1$ by integrating with respect to $y$:
$$
A_1 = \int_0^1 [y - y^3] \, dy = \left[\frac{y^2}{2} - \frac{y^4}{4}\right]_0^1 = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{4}
$$
By symmetry, $A_1 = A_2$, so the total enclosed area is:
$$
A = 2A_1 = 2 \left(\frac{1}{4}\right) = \frac{1}{2}
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 46: Region Bounded by Sine and Cosine

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Find the area of the region in the first quadrant bounded on the left by the $y$-axis and on the right by the curves $y = \sin x$ and $y = \cos x$.

**Solution:**
In the first quadrant, the curves $y = \sin x$ and $y = \cos x$ first intersect at $x = \frac{\pi}{4}$. From $x = 0$ (the $y$-axis) to $x = \frac{\pi}{4}$, the curve $y = \cos x$ lies above $y = \sin x$.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926130301.webp)

Integrating the vertical distance $\cos x - \sin x$ from $0$ to $\frac{\pi}{4}$ gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \int_0^{\pi/4} [\cos x - \sin x] \, dx \\
  &= [\sin x + \cos x]_0^{\pi/4} \\
  &= \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) - (0 + 1) \\
  &= \sqrt{2} - 1 \approx 0.414
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 47: Bisecting a Parabolic Region with a Horizontal Line

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> The region bounded below by the parabola $y = x^2$ and above by the line $y = 4$ is to be partitioned into two subsections of equal area by cutting across it with a horizontal line $y = c$.
> 1. Sketch the region and draw a line $y = c$ across it that looks about right. In terms of $c$, what are the coordinates of the points where the line and the parabola intersect?
> 2. Find $c$ by integrating with respect to $y$.
> 3. Find $c$ by integrating with respect to $x$.

**Solution:**
**1.** The parabola $y = x^2$ and the horizontal line $y = c$ (where $0 < c < 4$) intersect when $x^2 = c$, which means $x = \pm\sqrt{c}$.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926130702.webp)

Thus, the two points of intersection are:
$$
A = (\sqrt{c}, c) \quad \text{and} \quad B = (-\sqrt{c}, c)
$$

**2.** Since the entire region is symmetric about the $y$-axis, the horizontal line $y = c$ bisects the total area if and only if it bisects the right half of the region in the first quadrant (bounded by $x = 0$ and $x = \sqrt{y}$). Integrating with respect to $y$, the area $A_1$ of the lower portion ($0 \leq y \leq c$) in the first quadrant is:
$$
A_1 = \int_0^c \sqrt{y} \, dy = \left[\frac{y^{3/2}}{3/2}\right]_0^c = \frac{2}{3} c^{3/2}
$$
The area $A_2$ of the upper portion ($c \leq y \leq 4$) in the first quadrant is:
$$
A_2 = \int_c^4 \sqrt{y} \, dy = \left[\frac{2}{3} y^{3/2}\right]_c^4 = \frac{2}{3} [8 - c^{3/2}]
$$
Setting $A_1 = A_2$ to enforce equal areas:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{2}{3} c^{3/2} &= \frac{2}{3} [8 - c^{3/2}] \\
c^{3/2} &= 4 \\
c &= 4^{2/3} = 2\sqrt[3]{2} \approx 2.52
\end{aligned}
$$

**3.** To find $c$ by integrating with respect to $x$, we again work in the first quadrant. The lower subsection ($0 \leq x \leq \sqrt{c}$) is bounded above by $y = c$ and below by $y = x^2$:
$$
A_1 = \int_0^{\sqrt{c}} (c - x^2) \, dx = \left[cx - \frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^{\sqrt{c}} = c^{3/2} - \frac{c^{3/2}}{3} = \frac{2}{3} c^{3/2}
$$
The upper subsection in the first quadrant consists of a rectangular part over $[0, \sqrt{c}]$ of height $4 - c$, plus the region over $[\sqrt{c}, 2]$ bounded above by $y = 4$ and below by $y = x^2$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
A_2 &= \sqrt{c} \cdot (4 - c) + \int_{\sqrt{c}}^2 (4 - x^2) \, dx \\
    &= 4\sqrt{c} - c^{3/2} + \left[4x - \frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{\sqrt{c}}^2 \\
    &= 4\sqrt{c} - c^{3/2} + \left[\left(8 - \frac{8}{3}\right) - \left(4\sqrt{c} - \frac{c^{3/2}}{3}\right)\right] \\
    &= \frac{16}{3} - \frac{2}{3} c^{3/2}
\end{aligned}
$$
Equating $A_1 = A_2$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{2}{3} c^{3/2} &= \frac{16}{3} - \frac{2}{3} c^{3/2} \\
\frac{4}{3} c^{3/2} &= \frac{16}{3} \\
c^{3/2} &= 4 \implies c = 2\sqrt[3]{2} \approx 2.52
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

# 5.2 Finding Volumes by Slicing

## Cross-Sectional Slices and the Volume Integral

Now that we can find the exact area of a flat region with curved boundaries, we can take a natural step into three dimensions. If we have a cylindrical solid with a flat base of area $A$ and height $h$, its volume is simply $\text{area} \times \text{height} = A \cdot h$. How can we use this elementary idea to find the volume of an arbitrarily shaped three-dimensional solid whose cross-sections vary from point to point?

Imagine slicing the solid like a loaf of bread with planes perpendicular to the $x$-axis over the interval $[a, b]$. At any position $x \in [a, b]$, let $A(x)$ denote the area of the planar cross-section formed by intersecting the solid with the plane perpendicular to the $x$-axis at $x$. To approximate the total volume, we partition $[a, b]$ into $n$ subintervals of width $\Delta x_k$. Each slice between $x_{k-1}$ and $x_k$ can be approximated by a cylindrical slab of base area $A(x_k)$ and thickness $\Delta x_k$, as illustrated below.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926131114.webp)

Summing the volumes of these $n$ cylindrical slabs gives our Riemann sum approximation for the total volume:
$$
V \approx \sum_{k=1}^{n} A(x_k) \, \Delta x_k
$$
If the cross-sectional area function $A(x)$ is continuous (or more generally, integrable) on $[a, b]$, then as we make the slices thinner and thinner by taking the limit as the partition norm $\lvert P \rvert \to 0$, the approximation becomes exact and converges to the definite integral of $A(x)$.

> [!abstract] Definition: Volume by Cross-Sectional Slicing
> The volume $V$ of a solid of known integrable cross-sectional area $A(x)$ from $x = a$ to $x = b$ is the integral of $A$ from $a$ to $b$:
> $$
> V = \int_a^b A(x) \, dx
> $$

To apply the method of slicing systematically to any solid, we follow four straightforward steps:
1. **Sketch** the solid and a typical cross-section.
2. **Find a formula** for the cross-sectional area $A(x)$.
3. **Find the limits** of integration $a$ and $b$.
4. **Integrate** $A(x)$ from $a$ to $b$ to find the volume.

> [!example] Example: Volume of a Cylindrical Wedge
> A curved wedge is cut from a cylinder of radius $3$ by two planes. One plane is perpendicular to the axis of the cylinder. The second plane crosses the first plane at a $45^{\circ}$ angle at the center of the cylinder. Find the volume of the wedge.

**Solution:**
We follow the four-step slicing procedure:

**Step 1 (Sketching):** We set up our coordinate axes in the base plane (the plane perpendicular to the cylinder's axis) so that the line of intersection of the two planes lies along the $y$-axis, and the $x$-axis runs perpendicular to it from $x = 0$ to $x = 3$.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926131320.webp)

**Step 2 (Cross-sectional area):** Every slice perpendicular to the $x$-axis at position $x$ is a rectangle. Because the upper plane rises at a $45^{\circ}$ angle from the $y$-axis, the height of the rectangle at distance $x$ is $a = x \tan 45^{\circ} = x$. Because the base is a circle of radius $3$ ($x^2 + y^2 = 9$), the width of the rectangle extends from $y = -\sqrt{9 - x^2}$ to $y = \sqrt{9 - x^2}$, giving $b = 2\sqrt{9 - x^2}$. Thus, the area of the rectangular cross-section is:
$$
A(x) = a \cdot b = x \cdot 2\sqrt{9 - x^2} = 2x\sqrt{9 - x^2}
$$

**Step 3 (Limits of integration):** The slices run from the center line at $x = 0$ to the outer edge of the cylinder at $x = 3$.

**Step 4 (Integration):** We integrate $A(x)$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 3$:
$$
V = \int_0^3 2x\sqrt{9 - x^2} \, dx
$$
Using the substitution $u = 9 - x^2$, we have $-du = 2x \, dx$. When $x = 0$, $u = 9$; when $x = 3$, $u = 0$. Evaluating the transformed integral gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= \int_9^0 \sqrt{u} \, (-du) \\
  &= -\left[ \frac{2}{3} u^{3/2} \right]_9^0 \\
  &= -\frac{2}{3} \left[ 0 - 9^{3/2} \right] \\
  &= -\frac{2}{3} \cdot (-27) = 18
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

## Cavalieri's Theorem

Bonaventura Cavalieri, a student of Galileo, discovered a remarkable geometric principle: if two plane regions can be arranged to lie over the same interval of the $x$-axis in such a way that they have identical vertical cross-sectional lengths at every point, then the two regions must have the exact same area.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926152655.webp)

This principle extends naturally to three-dimensional solids (and bears Cavalieri's name in three dimensions as well, even though the 3D extension came later).

> [!theorem] Theorem: Cavalieri's Principle for Solids
> If two solids lie between parallel planes and every plane parallel to these bounding planes intersects both solids in cross-sections of equal area, then the two solids have the same volume.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926153248.webp)

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Why Cavalieri's Theorem Works
> If we look at our slicing integral $V = \int_a^b A(x) \, dx$, the volume of a solid depends *only* on the cross-sectional area function $A(x)$ and the interval $[a, b]$ over which the solid lies—not on how those cross-sections are shifted or twisted horizontally in space. Therefore, any two solids sharing the same cross-sectional area $A(x)$ at every $x \in [a, b]$ automatically have the exact same volume integral.

## 5.2 Exercises

### Problem 12: Comparing a Sliced Solid with a Right Circular Cone

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> A solid lies between planes perpendicular to the $x$-axis at $x = 0$ and $x = 12$. The cross-sections by planes perpendicular to the $x$-axis are circular disks whose diameters run from the line $y = x$ to the line $y = \frac{x}{2}$. Explain why the solid has the same volume as a right circular cone with base radius $3$ and height $12$.

**Solution:**
At any point $x \in [0, 12]$, the circular cross-section has a diameter spanning vertically from $y = \frac{x}{2}$ to $y = x$:
$$
d(x) = x - \frac{x}{2} = \frac{x}{2} \implies r(x) = \frac{x}{4}
$$
Therefore, the cross-sectional area of the solid at position $x$ is:
$$
A(x) = \pi [r(x)]^2 = \pi \frac{x^2}{16}
$$

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926153857.webp)

Integrating $A(x)$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 12$ gives the volume:
$$
V = \int_0^{12} \frac{\pi}{16} x^2 \, dx = \frac{\pi}{16} \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_0^{12} = \frac{\pi}{16} \cdot \frac{12^3}{3} = 36\pi
$$
Now compare this with a right circular cone of altitude $12$ and base radius $3$ (note that at $x = 12$, our solid also has diameter $d = 6$, or radius $3$). By similar triangles, the radius $r$ of the cone at distance $x$ from its vertex satisfies:
$$
\frac{r}{x} = \frac{3}{12} \implies r = \frac{x}{4}
$$
Thus, the cross-sectional area of the right circular cone at position $x$ is also $\pi r^2 = \pi \frac{x^2}{16}$.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926153949.webp)

Because both solids have the exact same cross-sectional area at every point $x \in [0, 12]$ and the same altitude, Cavalieri's Theorem guarantees that they have the same volume. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 13: Proving Cavalieri's Original Two-Dimensional Theorem

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Prove Cavalieri's original theorem for plane regions, assuming that each region is bounded above and below by the graphs of continuous functions.

**Solution:**
Suppose Region 1 is bounded above and below over $[a, b]$ by the continuous functions $f(x)$ and $g(x)$, and Region 2 is bounded above and below over $[a, b]$ by the continuous functions $f_2(x)$ and $g_2(x)$ (denoted $f'(x)$ and $g'(x)$ in the diagram below).

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926154321.webp)

At any point $x \in [a, b]$, the vertical cross-sectional length of Region 1 is $h(x) = f(x) - g(x)$, and the vertical cross-sectional length of Region 2 is $h_2(x) = f_2(x) - g_2(x)$. We are given that the vertical cross-sections have equal length at every point $x$:
$$
h(x) = f(x) - g(x) = f_2(x) - g_2(x) = h_2(x) \quad \forall x \in [a, b]
$$
Integrating both sides over $[a, b]$ and applying the definition of area between curves from Section 5.1, we obtain:
$$
\text{Area}(\text{Region 1}) = \int_a^b h(x) \, dx = \int_a^b h_2(x) \, dx = \text{Area}(\text{Region 2})
$$
Hence, the two plane regions have identical areas. $\blacksquare$

# 5.3 Volumes of Solids of Revolution

## The Disk Method

One of the most common applications of the slicing method occurs when a plane region is revolved about a line in the plane—such as the $x$-axis or $y$-axis—to sweep out a three-dimensional solid called a **solid of revolution**. When the region borders directly on the axis of revolution with no gap in between, slicing the solid perpendicular to the axis of revolution produces circular disks.

> [!abstract] Definition: Volume by Disks for Rotation About the $x$-Axis
> The volume $V$ of the solid generated by revolving about the $x$-axis the region between the $x$-axis and the graph of a continuous function $y = R(x)$, $a \leq x \leq b$, is:
> $$
> V = \int_a^b \pi [R(x)]^2 \, dx
> $$

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926154608.webp)

Why does the integrand take this form? Because at any point $x \in [a, b]$, a slice perpendicular to the $x$-axis is a circular disk of radius $R(x)$, so its cross-sectional area is simply the area of a circle, $A(x) = \pi [R(x)]^2$. Substituting $A(x)$ into our general slicing formula $V = \int_a^b A(x) \, dx$ immediately gives the disk formula.

> [!example] Example: Revolving a Square-Root Curve About the $x$-Axis
> The region between the curve $y = \sqrt{x}$, $0 \leq x \leq 4$, and the $x$-axis is revolved about the $x$-axis to generate a solid. Find its volume.

**Solution:**
Here, the radius of a circular cross-section at position $x$ is $R(x) = \sqrt{x}$.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926155531.webp)

Applying the disk method from $x = 0$ to $x = 4$, we obtain:
$$
V = \int_0^4 \pi (\sqrt{x})^2 \, dx = \int_0^4 \pi x \, dx = \frac{\pi}{2} [x^2]_0^4 = 8\pi
$$
$\blacksquare$

What if the axis of revolution is a horizontal or vertical line other than one of the coordinate axes? The underlying geometry does not change at all—we still slice perpendicular to the axis of revolution and measure the radius $R$ from the axis of revolution to the outer boundary curve.

> [!example] Example: Revolving About a Horizontal Line $y = 1$
> Find the volume of the solid generated by revolving the region bounded by $y = \sqrt{x}$ and the lines $y = 1$ and $x = 4$ about the horizontal line $y = 1$.

**Solution:**
At any point $x \in [1, 4]$, the vertical distance from the axis of revolution $y = 1$ to the curve $y = \sqrt{x}$ gives the disk radius $R(x) = \sqrt{x} - 1$.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926155638.webp)

Thus, the cross-sectional area at position $x$ is:
$$
A(x) = \pi (\sqrt{x} - 1)^2 = \pi (x + 1 - 2\sqrt{x})
$$
Integrating $A(x)$ from $x = 1$ to $x = 4$ yields the volume:
$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= \int_1^4 \pi (x + 1 - 2\sqrt{x}) \, dx \\
  &= \pi \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} + x - \frac{4}{3} x^{3/2} \right]_1^4 \\
  &= \pi \left( \left[ 8 + 4 - \frac{32}{3} \right] - \left[ \frac{1}{2} + 1 - \frac{4}{3} \right] \right) \\
  &= \pi \left( \frac{4}{3} - \frac{3}{2} + \frac{4}{3} \right) \\
  &= \frac{7\pi}{6}
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Revolving About Any Axis
> As the previous example illustrates, we can calculate the volume of a solid of revolution about *any* horizontal or vertical axis as long as we stick to the method of slicing and measure the radius $R$ perpendicular to the axis of rotation.

> [!example] Example: Revolving About a Vertical Line $x = 3$
> Find the volume of the solid generated by revolving the region between the parabola $x = y^2 + 1$ and the line $x = 3$ about the line $x = 3$.

**Solution:**
Because the axis of revolution $x = 3$ is vertical, we slice the solid horizontally—perpendicular to $x = 3$—and integrate with respect to $y$. At any height $y$, the horizontal distance from the axis $x = 3$ to the parabola $x = y^2 + 1$ is $R(y) = 3 - (y^2 + 1) = 2 - y^2$, giving a circular cross-section of area:
$$
A(y) = \pi (2 - y^2)^2
$$

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926161210.webp)

To find the $y$-limits of integration, we intersect the parabola with the line $x = 3$:
$$
y^2 + 1 = 3 \implies y^2 = 2 \implies y = \pm\sqrt{2}
$$
Integrating $A(y)$ from $y = -\sqrt{2}$ to $y = \sqrt{2}$ gives the volume:
$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= \int_{-\sqrt{2}}^{\sqrt{2}} \pi (4 + y^4 - 4y^2) \, dy \\
  &= \pi \left[ 4y + \frac{y^5}{5} - \frac{4}{3} y^3 \right]_{-\sqrt{2}}^{\sqrt{2}} \\
  &= \pi \left[ \left( 4\sqrt{2} + \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{5} - \frac{8\sqrt{2}}{3} \right) - \left( -4\sqrt{2} - \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{5} + \frac{8\sqrt{2}}{3} \right) \right] \\
  &= 2\pi \left( 4\sqrt{2} + \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{5} - \frac{8\sqrt{2}}{3} \right) \\
  &= \frac{2\sqrt{2}\pi}{15} (60 + 12 - 40) \\
  &= \frac{2\sqrt{2}\pi}{15} \cdot 32 = \frac{64\sqrt{2}\pi}{15}
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

## The Washer Method

What happens if the region we revolve does *not* border continuously on the axis of revolution? Revolving such a region leaves a hollow cavity or hole running through the interior of the solid. When we slice perpendicular to the axis of revolution, each cross-section is no longer a solid disk, but a flat ring—an annular **washer**—with an outer radius $R(x)$ and an inner hole radius $r(x)$.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926180057.webp)

Subtracting the area of the inner hole $\pi [r(x)]^2$ from the area of the outer circle $\pi [R(x)]^2$ gives the cross-sectional area of the washer at position $x$:
$$
A(x) = \pi \left( [R(x)]^2 - [r(x)]^2 \right)
$$
Assuming $R(x)$ and $r(x)$ are continuous functions on $[a, b]$ with $0 \leq r(x) \leq R(x)$, slicing the solid yields the washer volume formula.

> [!abstract] Definition: Volume by Washers
> The volume $V$ of the solid generated by revolving a region with outer radius $R(x)$ and inner radius $r(x)$ about the $x$-axis over $[a, b]$ is:
> $$
> V = \int_a^b \pi \left( [R(x)]^2 - [r(x)]^2 \right) \, dx
> $$

> [!example] Example: Volume by Washers About the $y$-Axis
> The region bounded by the parabola $y = x^2$ and the line $y = 2x$ in the first quadrant is revolved about the $y$-axis to generate a solid. Find its volume.

**Solution:**
Because the region is revolved about the vertical $y$-axis, we slice horizontally and integrate with respect to $y$. First, note that $y = x^2$ and $y = 2x$ intersect when $x^2 = 2x$, which gives $x = 0$ ($y = 0$) and $x = 2$ ($y = 4$).

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926180248.webp)

At any height $y \in [0, 4]$, the inner radius from the $y$-axis to the line $x = \frac{y}{2}$ is $r(y) = \frac{y}{2}$, and the outer radius from the $y$-axis to the parabola $x = \sqrt{y}$ is $R(y) = \sqrt{y}$. The cross-sectional washer area is therefore:
$$
\begin{aligned}
A(y) &= \pi \left( (\sqrt{y})^2 - \left(\frac{y}{2}\right)^2 \right) \\
     &= \pi \left( y - \frac{y^2}{4} \right) \\
     &= \frac{\pi}{4} (4y - y^2)
\end{aligned}
$$
Integrating $A(y)$ from $y = 0$ to $y = 4$ gives the volume of the solid:
$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= \frac{\pi}{4} \int_0^4 (4y - y^2) \, dy \\
  &= \frac{\pi}{4} \left[ 2y^2 - \frac{y^3}{3} \right]_0^4 \\
  &= \frac{\pi}{4} \left( 32 - \frac{64}{3} \right) \\
  &= \frac{8\pi}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 43: Minimizing and Maximizing Volume of a Revolved Arch

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> The arch $y = \sin x$, $0 \leq x \leq \pi$, is revolved about the horizontal line $y = c$, $0 \leq c \leq 1$, to generate the solid shown in the figure below.
> 1. Find the value of $c$ that minimizes the volume of the solid. What is the minimum value?
> 2. What value of $c$ in $[0, 1]$ maximizes the volume of the solid?

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926180651.webp)

**Solution:**
**1.** Let $a \in \left[0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$ be the point where $\sin a = c$. On the outer intervals $[0, a]$ and $[\pi - a, \pi]$, the line $y = c$ lies above $y = \sin x$, so the cross-sectional disk has radius $c - \sin x$ and area $A_1(x) = \pi (c - \sin x)^2$. On the middle interval $[a, \pi - a]$, the curve $y = \sin x$ lies above $y = c$, so the cross-sectional disk has radius $\sin x - c$ and area $A_2(x) = \pi (\sin x - c)^2$. Because $(c - \sin x)^2 = (\sin x - c)^2$ everywhere, all three integrals combine into a single integral over $[0, \pi]$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
V(c) &= \int_0^a \pi (c - \sin x)^2 \, dx + \int_a^{\pi-a} \pi (\sin x - c)^2 \, dx + \int_{\pi-a}^\pi \pi (c - \sin x)^2 \, dx \\
     &= \int_0^\pi \pi (\sin x - c)^2 \, dx \\
     &= \pi \int_0^\pi (\sin^2 x + c^2 - 2c\sin x) \, dx \\
     &= \pi \left[ \frac{x}{2} - \frac{\sin 2x}{4} + c^2 x + 2c \cos x \right]_0^\pi \\
     &= \pi \left[ \left(\frac{\pi}{2} - 0 + c^2\pi - 2c\right) - (0 - 0 + 0 + 2c) \right] \\
     &= \pi \left[ \frac{\pi}{2} + c^2\pi - 4c \right]
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $V(c)$ is a continuous, differentiable function of $c$ on the closed interval $[0, 1]$, we find its critical points by setting $\frac{dV}{dc} = 0$:
$$
\frac{dV}{dc} = \pi [0 + 2\pi c - 4] = 0 \implies c = \frac{4}{2\pi} = \frac{2}{\pi}
$$
We now evaluate $V(c)$ at the critical point $c = \frac{2}{\pi}$ and at the endpoints $c = 0$ and $c = 1$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
V(0) &= \frac{\pi^2}{2} \approx 4.93 \\
V(1) &= \pi \left[ \frac{\pi}{2} + \pi - 4 \right] = \frac{\pi}{2}(3\pi - 8) \approx 2.24 \\
V\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right) &= \pi \left[ \frac{\pi}{2} + \frac{4}{\pi} - \frac{8}{\pi} \right] = \pi \left[ \frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{4}{\pi} \right] = \frac{\pi^2 - 8}{2} \approx 0.93
\end{aligned}
$$
Comparing these values, the minimum volume occurs at $c = \frac{2}{\pi}$, and the minimum volume is $V_{\min} = \frac{\pi^2 - 8}{2}$.

**2.** From our endpoint comparison on $[0, 1]$, the maximum volume occurs at $c = 0$ (where $V_{\max} = \frac{\pi^2}{2}$). $\blacksquare$

# 5.4 Volumes by Cylindrical Shells

## The Shell Method and Its Derivation

In Section 5.3, we found volumes of solids of revolution by slicing *perpendicular* to the axis of revolution, producing disks or washers. Sometimes, however, slicing perpendicular to the axis of rotation is awkward—for example, when the inner and outer radii are determined by the same curve, or when solving for $x$ in terms of $y$ is difficult. What if, instead, we slice the region *parallel* to the axis of revolution?

When a thin vertical strip of width $\Delta x$ is revolved around the vertical $y$-axis, it sweeps out a thin-walled hollow cylinder—a **cylindrical shell**—rather than a disk or washer. By nesting these concentric cylindrical shells together like Russian nesting dolls, we get a powerful alternative formula for volume.

> [!theorem] Theorem: Shell Formula for Revolution About the $y$-Axis
> The volume $V$ of the solid generated by revolving the region between the $x$-axis and the graph of a continuous function $y = f(x) \geq 0$, $0 \leq a \leq x \leq b$, about the $y$-axis is:
> $$
> V = \int_a^b 2\pi x f(x) \, dx
> $$

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926181721.webp)

**Proof:**
Let $P$ be a partition of the interval $[a, b]$ into $n$ subintervals. When the $k$-th sub-region over $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$ is rotated about the $y$-axis, we can approximate the volume $V_k$ of the resulting solid piece using a cylindrical shell of height $f(x_k)$, thickness $\Delta x_k$, outer radius $R = x_k$, and inner radius $r = x_k - \Delta x_k$.

The volume of a hollow cylinder is the area of its annular base times its height:
$$
\begin{aligned}
V_k &\approx \pi \times \text{height} \times (R^2 - r^2) \\
    &= \pi f(x_k) \left( x_k^2 - (x_k - \Delta x_k)^2 \right) \\
    &= \pi f(x_k) \left( x_k^2 - x_k^2 - \Delta x_k^2 + 2x_k \Delta x_k \right) \\
    &= 2\pi x_k f(x_k) \, \Delta x_k - \pi f(x_k) \, \Delta x_k^2
\end{aligned}
$$
Summing over all $n$ shells and taking the limit as the partition norm $\lvert P \rvert \to 0$, we obtain:
$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= \lim_{\lvert P \rvert \to 0} \sum_{k=1}^n V_k \\
  &= \lim_{\lvert P \rvert \to 0} \left( \sum_{k=1}^n 2\pi x_k f(x_k) \, \Delta x_k - \sum_{k=1}^n \pi f(x_k) \, \Delta x_k^2 \right) \\
  &= \int_{a}^{b} 2\pi x f(x) \, dx - 0 = \int_a^b 2\pi x f(x) \, dx
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Why the Second Sum Vanishes in the Limit
> Why does the second sum $\sum_{k=1}^n \pi f(x_k) \, \Delta x_k^2$ go to $0$ as $\lvert P \rvert \to 0$? Because $f(x) \geq 0$ and every subinterval width satisfies $\Delta x_k \leq \lvert P \rvert$, we can bound the sum from above and below:
> $$
> 0 \leq \sum_{k=1}^{n} \pi f(x_{k}) \, \Delta x_{k}^{2} \leq \left( \sum_{k=1}^{n} \pi f(x_{k}) \, \Delta x_{k} \right) \lvert P \rvert
> $$
> Taking the limit as $\lvert P \rvert \to 0$ across this inequality gives:
> $$
> 0 \leq \lim_{\lvert P \rvert \to 0} \sum_{k=1}^{n} \pi f(x_{k}) \, \Delta x_{k}^{2} \leq \left( \int_{a}^{b} \pi f(x) \, dx \right) \lim_{\lvert P \rvert \to 0} \lvert P \rvert = 0
> $$
> Since both ends of the inequality are $0$, the Sandwich Theorem guarantees that the middle limit is identically $0$.

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Comparing Slicing vs. Shells
> Notice an important algebraic advantage of the shell method: the disk and washer formulas from Section 5.3 come from cross-sectional areas $\pi R^2$, so they always involve *squared* function terms $[R(x)]^2$. By contrast, the shell integrand $2\pi (\text{shell radius})(\text{shell height}) = 2\pi x f(x)$ does not square $f(x)$, which often makes the resulting integral much easier to evaluate.

> [!example] Example: Comparing the Shell Method and the Washer Method
> Find the volume of the solid generated by revolving the region bounded by the $x$-axis, the curve $y = \sqrt{x}$, and the line $x = 4$ about the $y$-axis. Compute the volume using both the shell method and the washer method to verify that they give the same result.

**Solution:**
**Method 1 (Shell Method):** Integrating with respect to $x$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 4$, a vertical strip at position $x$ has shell radius $x$ and shell height $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= \int_0^4 2\pi x \sqrt{x} \, dx \\
  &= 2\pi \int_0^4 x^{3/2} \, dx \\
  &= 2\pi \left[ \frac{x^{5/2}}{5/2} \right]_0^4 \\
  &= 2\pi \cdot \frac{2}{5} \cdot 32 = \frac{128\pi}{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Method 2 (Washer Method):** Rewriting $y = \sqrt{x}$ as $x = y^2$ for $y \in [0, 2]$, a horizontal washer at height $y$ has outer radius $R(y) = 4$ and inner radius $r(y) = y^2$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= \int_0^2 \pi \left( [R(y)]^2 - [r(y)]^2 \right) \, dy \\
  &= \int_0^2 \pi (16 - y^4) \, dy \\
  &= \pi \left[ 16y - \frac{y^5}{5} \right]_0^2 \\
  &= \pi \left( 32 - \frac{32}{5} \right) \\
  &= 32\pi \cdot \frac{4}{5} = \frac{128\pi}{5}
\end{aligned}
$$
Both methods produce the exact same volume of $\frac{128\pi}{5}$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 37: Comparing Disk, Washer, and Shell Methods on a Compound Region

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> The region shown below is to be revolved about the $x$-axis to generate a solid. Which of the methods (disk, washer, shell) could you use to find the volume of the solid? How many integrals would be required in each case? Compute the volume using both slicing (disk/washer) and shells.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926191757.webp)

**Solution:**
To revolve the region about the $x$-axis, we can use either the slicing method (which combines a disk integral on $[-2, 0]$ with a washer integral on $[0, 1]$, requiring **two** integrals) or the cylindrical shell method (integrating with respect to $y$ on $[0, 1]$, requiring only **one** integral).

**Disk and Washer Method (Two Integrals):**
We split the $x$-interval into $[-2, 0]$ and $[0, 1]$ because the lower boundary changes at $x = 0$:
- On $[-2, 0]$, the region touches the $x$-axis, so cross-sections are solid disks of radius $R(x) = \sqrt{\frac{x+2}{3}}$ and area $A_1(x) = \pi \left( \sqrt{\frac{x+2}{3}} \right)^2 = \frac{\pi}{3} (x + 2)$.
- On $[0, 1]$, there is a cavity bounded by $y = \sqrt{x}$, so cross-sections are washers with outer radius $R(x) = \sqrt{\frac{x+2}{3}}$ and inner radius $r(x) = \sqrt{x}$, giving area:
  $$
  A_2(x) = \pi \left( \left(\sqrt{\frac{x+2}{3}}\right)^2 - (\sqrt{x})^2 \right) = \pi \left( \frac{x+2}{3} - x \right) = \frac{\pi}{3} (2 - 2x)
  $$
Adding the two integrals gives the total volume:
$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= \int_{-2}^0 \frac{\pi}{3} (x + 2) \, dx + \int_0^1 \frac{2\pi}{3} (1 - x) \, dx \\
  &= \frac{\pi}{3} \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} + 2x \right]_{-2}^0 + \frac{2\pi}{3} \left[ x - \frac{x^2}{2} \right]_0^1 \\
  &= \frac{\pi}{3} [0 - (2 - 4)] + \frac{2\pi}{3} \left( \frac{1}{2} - 0 \right) \\
  &= \frac{2\pi}{3} + \frac{\pi}{3} = \pi
\end{aligned}
$$

**Shell Method (One Integral):**
Slicing horizontally parallel to the $x$-axis over $y \in [0, 1]$, each horizontal strip at height $y$ has shell radius $y$ and horizontal length (shell height) $f(y) = y^2 - (3y^2 - 2) = 2 - 2y^2$. Integrating with respect to $y$ gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= \int_0^1 2\pi y (2 - 2y^2) \, dy \\
  &= 4\pi \int_0^1 (y - y^3) \, dy \\
  &= 4\pi \left[ \frac{y^2}{2} - \frac{y^4}{4} \right]_0^1 \\
  &= 4\pi \left( \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4} - 0 \right) = 4\pi \cdot \frac{1}{4} = \pi
\end{aligned}
$$
Both methods confirm that the volume is $\pi$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 39: Determining an Unknown Function from Its Revolved Volume

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Suppose that the function $f(x)$ is non-negative and continuous for $x \geq 0$. Suppose also that, for every positive number $b$, revolving the region enclosed by the graph of $f$, the coordinate axes, and the line $x = b$ about the $y$-axis generates a solid of volume $2\pi b^3$. Find $f(x)$.

**Solution:**
By the cylindrical shell method, revolving the region under $y = f(x)$ from $x = 0$ to $x = b$ about the $y$-axis generates a solid whose volume is:

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926191928.webp)

$$
V(b) = \int_0^b 2\pi x f(x) \, dx = 2\pi b^3
$$
Because $f$ is continuous, we can differentiate both sides with respect to $b$ using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dV}{db} = 2\pi b f(b) &= 6\pi b^2 \\
b f(b) &= 3b^2 \implies f(b) = 3b
\end{aligned}
$$
Since this holds for all $b > 0$ and $f$ is continuous at $0$, the function is $f(x) = 3x$. $\blacksquare$

# 5.5 Lengths of Plane Curves

## Smooth Functions and Arc Length

How do we measure the exact length of a curved path in the plane—the distance you would travel if you walked along the graph of $y = f(x)$ from $x = a$ to $x = b$? Before setting up an integral, we need a regularity condition on $f(x)$ that prevents the curve from having sharp corners, cusps, or wild infinitary wiggles.

> [!abstract] Definition: Smooth Function and Smooth Curve
> A function with a continuous first derivative is said to be **smooth**, and its graph is called a **smooth curve**.

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Differentiability vs. Smoothness
> Note that merely knowing a function is differentiable on $(a, b)$ does *not* automatically guarantee that it is smooth (which requires $f'(x)$ to be *continuous*). Interestingly, even if a derivative $f'(x)$ is discontinuous at a point, its discontinuity can never be a simple jump or removable discontinuity (Type I); by **Darboux's Theorem** (the Intermediate Value Theorem for derivatives), any discontinuity in $f'(x)$ must be oscillatory! Requiring $f'(x)$ to be continuous rules out such pathological oscillations and ensures the arc length integral exists.

## Length of a Smooth Curve

> [!abstract] Definition: Arc Length Formula ($y$ as a Function of $x$)
> If $f$ is smooth on $[a, b]$, the length $L$ of the curve $y = f(x)$ from $x = a$ to $x = b$ is:
> $$
> L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, dx
> $$

**Proof:**
To approximate the length of the curve $y = f(x)$ over $[a, b]$, let $P$ be a partition of $[a, b]$ into $n$ subintervals.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926192203.webp)

On the $k$-th subinterval $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$, we approximate the curved arc by the straight line segment connecting the endpoints $(x_{k-1}, f(x_{k-1}))$ and $(x_k, f(x_k))$. By the Pythagorean theorem, the length $\Delta s_k$ of this $k$-th chord is:
$$
\Delta s_k = \sqrt{\Delta x_k^2 + \Delta y_k^2} = \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\Delta y_k}{\Delta x_k}\right)^2} \, \Delta x_k
$$
Because $f$ is smooth, it is continuous on $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$ and differentiable on $(x_{k-1}, x_k)$. By the Mean Value Theorem, there exists a point $c_k \in (x_{k-1}, x_k)$ such that $\frac{\Delta y_k}{\Delta x_k} = f'(c_k)$. Substituting this into $\Delta s_k$ gives:
$$
\Delta s_k = \sqrt{1 + [f'(c_k)]^2} \, \Delta x_k
$$
Summing the chord lengths over all $n$ subintervals yields an approximation for the total curve length $L$:
$$
L \approx \sum_{k=1}^n \sqrt{1 + [f'(c_k)]^2} \, \Delta x_k
$$
As the partition norm $\lvert P \rvert \to 0$, this Riemann sum converges to a definite integral provided the integrand is integrable. Because $f$ is smooth, $f'(x)$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, which implies $\sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2}$ is also continuous and therefore integrable on $[a, b]$. Thus:
$$
L = \lim_{\lvert P \rvert \to 0} \sum_{k=1}^n \sqrt{1 + [f'(c_k)]^2} \, \Delta x_k = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, dx
$$
$\blacksquare$

> [!example] Example: Finding the Arc Length of a Smooth Curve
> Find the length of the curve $y = \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3} x^{3/2} - 1$ for $0 \leq x \leq 1$.

**Solution:**
First, we compute the derivative $\frac{dy}{dx}$:
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3} \cdot \frac{3}{2} x^{1/2} - 0 = 2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{x}
$$
Substituting $\frac{dy}{dx}$ into the arc length formula gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= \int_0^1 \sqrt{1 + (2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{x})^2} \, dx \\
  &= \int_0^1 \sqrt{1 + 8x} \, dx
\end{aligned}
$$
Using the substitution $u = 1 + 8x$, we have $du = 8 \, dx$, with $u = 1$ at $x = 0$ and $u = 9$ at $x = 1$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= \int_1^9 \sqrt{u} \, \frac{du}{8} \\
  &= \frac{1}{8} \left[ \frac{2}{3} u^{3/2} \right]_1^9 \\
  &= \frac{1}{12} [27 - 1] = \frac{13}{6}
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

## Dealing with Discontinuities in $\frac{dy}{dx}$

At times, a perfectly well-behaved curve has a vertical tangent or cusp where $\frac{dy}{dx}$ blows up and is undefined at one or more points in $[a, b]$, preventing us from integrating with respect to $x$. When that happens, we can often express the curve as $x = g(y)$ instead! If $\frac{dx}{dy}$ is continuous on the corresponding $y$-interval $[c, d]$, we simply integrate with respect to $y$:
$$
L = \int_c^d \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dx}{dy}\right)^2} \, dy
$$

> [!example] Example: Arc Length of a Curve with a Vertical Tangent
> Find the length of the curve $y = \left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2/3}$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 2$.

**Solution:**
If we differentiate $y$ with respect to $x$, we find:
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{-1/3} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{2}{x}\right)^{1/3}
$$

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926192413.webp)

Notice that $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is undefined at $x = 0$ because the curve has a vertical tangent at the origin. To bypass this singularity, we solve for $x$ in terms of $y$:
$$
y = \left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2/3} \implies x = 2y^{3/2} \implies \frac{dx}{dy} = 3y^{1/2}
$$
Now $\frac{dx}{dy} = 3\sqrt{y}$ is completely continuous on the corresponding interval $y \in [0, 1]$. Integrating with respect to $y$ yields:
$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= \int_0^1 \sqrt{1 + (3\sqrt{y})^2} \, dy \\
  &= \int_0^1 \sqrt{1 + 9y} \, dy \\
  &= \frac{1}{9} \left[ \frac{2}{3} (1 + 9y)^{3/2} \right]_0^1 \\
  &= \frac{2}{27} (10\sqrt{10} - 1) \approx 2.27
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

## The Arc Length Differential

We can unify both the $dx$ and $dy$ arc length formulas into a single, symmetric shorthand by introducing the **arc length differential** $ds$:
$$
ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2}
$$

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926192736.webp)

Geometrically, you can think of $ds$ as the hypotenuse of an infinitesimal right triangle with horizontal leg $dx$ and vertical leg $dy$. In terms of $ds$, the arc length of a curve between two points is written compactly as:
$$
L = \int_a^b ds
$$
When $dx \neq 0$ and $\frac{dy}{dx} = f'(x)$, factoring out $dx$ recovers the $x$-integral, and factoring out $dy$ recovers the $y$-integral:
$$
L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, dx = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2} \, dx = \int_a^b \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2} = \int_a^b ds
$$

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Utility of the Differential Form
> Writing $L = \int ds$ is a convenient shorthand that carries the exact same meaning as our original formulas while leaving us free to factor out either $dx$ or $dy$ depending on which parameterization is smoother.

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Arc Length Integrals and Numerical Integration
> The arc length formula $L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, dx$ is notorious for producing integrands whose elementary antiderivatives cannot be found in closed form, largely because of the square root. Whenever we encounter such an integral in practice, we turn to numerical integration methods (such as the Trapezoidal Rule or Simpson's Rule) to approximate the length.

## 5.5 Exercises

### Problem 19: Reconstructing Curves from an Arc Length Integral

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> 1. Find a curve through the point $(1, 1)$ whose length integral is:
>    $$
>    L = \int_1^4 \sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{4x}} \, dx
>    $$
> 2. How many such curves are there? Give reasons for your answer.

**Solution:**
**1.** Comparing the given integral with the standard arc length formula $L = \int_1^4 \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, dx$, we equate the squared derivative terms:
$$
\begin{aligned}
[f'(x)]^2 &= \frac{1}{4x} \implies f'(x) = \pm \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} \\
f(x) &= \int \pm \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} \, dx = \pm \frac{1}{2} \left[ \frac{x^{1/2}}{1/2} \right] + c = \pm \sqrt{x} + c
\end{aligned}
$$
Imposing the condition that the curve passes through $(1, 1)$, so $f(1) = 1$:
$$
\pm \sqrt{1} + c = 1 \implies c = 1 \mp 1 \implies c = 0 \text{ or } c = 2
$$
Hence, the possible curves are $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ (with $c = 0$) or $f(x) = -\sqrt{x} + 2$ (with $c = 2$).

**2.** There are **two** such curves because $[f'(x)]^2 = \frac{1}{4x}$ yields two distinct continuous choices for the sign of the derivative $f'(x)$ on $[1, 4]$, each uniquely determined once anchored at $(1, 1)$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 21: Arc Length of a Function Defined by an Integral

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Find the length of the curve:
> $$
> y = \int_0^x \sqrt{\cos 2t} \, dt, \quad 0 \leq x \leq \frac{\pi}{4}
> $$

**Solution:**
By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, differentiating $y$ with respect to $x$ gives $\frac{dy}{dx} = \sqrt{\cos 2x}$. Substituting this into the arc length formula and using the double-angle identity $1 + \cos 2x = 2\cos^2 x$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= \int_0^{\pi/4} \sqrt{1 + \cos 2x} \, dx \\
  &= \int_0^{\pi/4} \sqrt{2} \cos x \, dx \\
  &= \sqrt{2} [\sin x]_0^{\pi/4} = 1
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 22: Total Perimeter of an Astroid

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> The graph of the equation $x^{2/3} + y^{2/3} = 1$ is one of a family of curves called **astroids** (not "asteroids") because of their star-like appearance. Find the total length of this curve.

**Solution:**
Because the astroid is symmetric across both coordinate axes, we can find the length of its arc in the first quadrant and multiply by $4$.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926193159.webp)

Solving for $y$ in the first quadrant ($x > 0, y \geq 0$):
$$
x^{2/3} + y^{2/3} = 1 \implies y^{2/3} = 1 - x^{2/3} \implies y = (1 - x^{2/3})^{3/2}
$$
Differentiating with respect to $x$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dy}{dx} &= \frac{3}{2} (1 - x^{2/3})^{1/2} \cdot \left(-\frac{2}{3} x^{-1/3}\right) \\
             &= -\frac{\sqrt{1 - x^{2/3}}}{x^{1/3}}
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore, the first-quadrant arc length $L_1$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 1$ is:
$$
\begin{aligned}
L_1 &= \int_0^1 \sqrt{1 + \frac{1 - x^{2/3}}{x^{2/3}}} \, dx \\
    &= \int_0^1 \sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{x^{2/3}} - 1} \, dx \\
    &= \int_0^1 x^{-1/3} \, dx \\
    &= \left[ \frac{x^{2/3}}{2/3} \right]_0^1 = \frac{3}{2} [1 - 0] = \frac{3}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$
Multiplying by $4$ gives the total length of the astroid: $L = 4 \times \frac{3}{2} = 6$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 25: Curves Whose Arc Length on $[0, a]$ Is Always $\sqrt{2}a$

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Is there a smooth curve $y = f(x)$ whose length over the interval $0 \leq x \leq a$ is always $\sqrt{2}a$? Give reasons for your answer.

**Solution:**
Suppose the arc length from $0$ to $a$ satisfies:
$$
L(a) = \int_0^a \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, dx = \sqrt{2}a \quad \forall a \geq 0
$$
Differentiating both sides with respect to $a$ by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{1 + [f'(a)]^2} &= \sqrt{2} \\
1 + [f'(a)]^2 &= 2 \implies f'(a) = \pm 1 \\
f(x) &= \int (\pm 1) \, dx = \pm x + c
\end{aligned}
$$

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926193258.webp)

Yes! Geometrically, any straight line of slope $+1$ or $-1$ ($y = \pm x + c$) makes a $45^{\circ}$ angle with the $x$-axis, so the segment over $[0, a]$ is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $a$ and $a$, which always has length $\sqrt{a^2 + a^2} = \sqrt{2}a$ regardless of the vertical intercept $c$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 26: Approximating Arc Length with Tangent Fins

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Assume $f$ is smooth on $[a, b]$ and partition the interval $[a, b]$ in the usual way. In each subinterval $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$, construct the **tangent fin** at the point $(x_{k-1}, f(x_{k-1}))$, as shown in the figure below.
> 1. Show that the length of the $k$-th tangent fin over the interval $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$ equals $\sqrt{\Delta x_k^2 + (f'(x_{k-1}) \Delta x_k)^2}$.
> 2. Show that:
>    $$
>    \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^n (\text{length of } k\text{-th tangent fin}) = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, dx
>    $$
>    which is the length $L$ of the curve $y = f(x)$ from $a$ to $b$.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926193334.webp)

**Solution:**
**1.** The tangent line at $(x_{k-1}, f(x_{k-1}))$ has slope $f'(x_{k-1}) = \tan \theta = \frac{\Delta y_k}{\Delta x_k}$, where $\Delta y_k$ is the vertical rise of the tangent fin over the horizontal run $\Delta x_k$:
$$
\Delta y_k = f'(x_{k-1}) \Delta x_k
$$
By the Pythagorean theorem, the length of the $k$-th tangent fin is:
$$
\text{Length of } k\text{-th tangent fin} = \sqrt{\Delta x_k^2 + \Delta y_k^2} = \sqrt{\Delta x_k^2 + (f'(x_{k-1}) \Delta x_k)^2}
$$

**2.** Factoring $\Delta x_k$ out of the square root and summing over all $n$ subintervals gives:
$$
\sum_{k=1}^n (\text{length of } k\text{-th tangent fin}) = \sum_{k=1}^n \sqrt{1 + [f'(x_{k-1})]^2} \, \Delta x_k
$$
Because $f$ is smooth on $[a, b]$, $f'(x)$ is continuous, so $\sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2}$ is continuous and integrable on $[a, b]$. Taking the limit as $n \to \infty$ (with $\lvert P \rvert \to 0$), this Riemann sum converges to the arc length integral:
$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^n \sqrt{1 + [f'(x_{k-1})]^2} \, \Delta x_k = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, dx = L
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 28: Width of Flat Sheet for Corrugated Iron Roofing

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Your metal fabrication company is bidding for a contract to make sheets of corrugated iron roofing like the one shown below. The cross-sections of the corrugated sheets are to conform to the curve:
> $$
> y = \sin\left(\frac{3\pi}{20} x\right), \quad 0 \leq x \leq 20 \text{ in.}
> $$
> If the roofing is to be stamped from a flat sheet by a process that does not stretch the material, how wide should the original flat material be? Give your answer correct to two decimal places.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926193508.webp)

**Solution:**
Because the stamping process does not stretch the metal, the required width of the original flat sheet equals the arc length $L$ of the corrugated cross-section over $0 \leq x \leq 20$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= \sin\left(\frac{3\pi}{20} x\right) \implies f'(x) = \frac{3\pi}{20} \cos\left(\frac{3\pi}{20} x\right) \\
L &= \int_0^{20} \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{3\pi}{20} \cos\left(\frac{3\pi}{20} x\right)\right)^2} \, dx
\end{aligned}
$$
This elliptic integral has no elementary antiderivative, so we must approximate it numerically to within an error tolerance of $0.01$. Let $h(x) = \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{3\pi}{20} \cos\left(\frac{3\pi}{20} x\right)\right)^2}$. Differentiating $h(x)$ twice to bound the Trapezoidal Rule error:
$$
\begin{aligned}
h'(x) &= \frac{1}{2h(x)} \cdot 2\left(\frac{3\pi}{20} \cos\left(\frac{3\pi}{20} x\right)\right)\left(-\frac{3\pi}{20} \sin\left(\frac{3\pi}{20} x\right)\right) \cdot \frac{3\pi}{20} \\
      &= -\left(\frac{3\pi}{20}\right)^3 \frac{\sin\left(\frac{3\pi}{10} x\right)}{2h(x)} \\
h''(x) &= -\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{3\pi}{20}\right)^3 \left[ \frac{h(x) \left(\frac{3\pi}{10} \cos\left(\frac{3\pi}{10} x\right)\right) - h'(x) \sin\left(\frac{3\pi}{10} x\right)}{[h(x)]^2} \right]
\end{aligned}
$$
Using a computer algebra system (WolframAlpha), the maximum magnitude of the second derivative on $[0, 20]$ is $\lvert h''(x) \rvert_{\max} \approx 0.0493$. For the Trapezoidal Rule error $\lvert E_T \rvert$ to be at most $\frac{1}{100}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lvert E_T \rvert \leq \frac{(b - a)^3}{12n^2} M \leq \frac{1}{100} &\implies \frac{20^3}{12n^2} (0.0493) \leq \frac{1}{100} \\
&\implies n^2 \geq \frac{8000}{12} \cdot 4.93 \approx 3287 \implies n \geq 58
\end{aligned}
$$
Evaluating $58$ subintervals by hand is impractical, so we turn to **Simpson's Rule**. Using WolframAlpha to bound the fourth derivative gives $\lvert h^{(4)}(x) \rvert_{\max} \approx 0.034224$. Requiring the Simpson's Rule error $\lvert E_S \rvert \leq \frac{1}{100}$ gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lvert E_S \rvert \leq \frac{(b - a)^5}{180n^4} M \leq \frac{1}{100} &\implies \frac{20^5}{180n^4} (0.034224) \leq \frac{1}{100} \\
&\implies n^4 \geq \frac{3{,}200{,}000}{180} \cdot 3.4224 \implies n \geq 16
\end{aligned}
$$
Applying Simpson's Rule with $n = 16$ subintervals yields $L \approx 21.07\text{ in.}$ ($21.067\text{ in.}$). $\blacksquare$

# 5.6 Areas of Surfaces of Revolution

## Why Cylinder Approximations Fail for Surface Area

When we revolve a smooth curve $y = f(x) \geq 0$ about the $x$-axis, it sweeps out a thin two-dimensional shell in space called a **surface of revolution**. How should we compute its surface area? Before jumping to the correct formula, it is deeply instructive to examine why the most tempting, naive approach—approximating the surface with flat-rimmed cylinders—completely fails!

### The Problem with Cylinder Approximations

In the disk method for volume (Section 5.3), we approximated each slice of thickness $\Delta x_k$ with a right circular cylinder of radius $f(x_{k-1})$ and width $\Delta x_k$. It is natural to wonder: can we use the lateral surface area of that same cylinder, $2\pi f(c_k) \Delta x_k$, to approximate the surface area of the $k$-th slice?

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926195854.webp)

If this cylinder approximation converged to the true surface area, then taking the limit as $\lvert P \rvert \to 0$ would give:
$$
S_{\text{naive}} = \lim_{\lvert P \rvert \to 0} \sum_{k=1}^n 2\pi f(c_k) \, \Delta x_k = \int_a^b 2\pi f(x) \, dx
$$

Let's test this naive formula on a concrete example where we already know the exact geometric answer: a right circular cone.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926195914.webp)

Consider revolving the straight line segment $f(x) = -\frac{r}{h}(x - h)$ over $0 \leq x \leq h$ about the $x$-axis, which generates a cone of base radius $r$, height $h$, and slant height $L = \sqrt{r^2 + h^2}$. Applying the naive cylinder integral gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
S_{\text{naive}} &= \int_0^h 2\pi \left(-\frac{r}{h}(x - h)\right) dx \\
                 &= -\frac{2\pi r}{h} \left[\frac{x^2}{2} - hx\right]_0^h \\
                 &= -\frac{2\pi r}{h} \left(\frac{h^2}{2} - h^2\right) = \pi r h
\end{aligned}
$$
But from elementary geometry, the true lateral surface area of a cone is $\pi r L = \pi r \sqrt{r^2 + h^2}$, *not* $\pi r h$! The reason the cylinder approximation fails is simple: a cylinder uses purely horizontal line segments of length $\Delta x_k$ along its rim, and horizontal steps can never converge to the tilted arc length $\Delta s_k = \sqrt{\Delta x_k^2 + \Delta y_k^2}$ of a sloped curve, no matter how fine we make the partition.

### Why Did Cylinders and Rectangles Work for Area and Volume?

This raises a natural question: if horizontal steps fail to capture the tilted boundary, why did rectangles and cylinders work so well when we calculated plane areas and solid volumes?

The key difference is that plane areas and solid volumes measure the *interior space* enclosed by the boundary. For a thin strip or disk, the vast bulk of the interior space is captured accurately by the rectangle or cylinder, and the missing triangular wedge near the tilted boundary is only a tiny edge correction that shrinks dramatically as the partition gets finer. For surface area, however, we are not measuring the space inside—we are measuring the *boundary itself*!

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926200028.webp)

### First-Order vs. Second-Order Error Analysis

We can make this intuition rigorous by comparing the order of the approximation error in each case. When we zoom in closely on a smooth curve over a tiny subinterval $\Delta x$, the curve looks locally like a straight line with rise $\Delta y \approx f'(x_k) \Delta x$.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926200256.webp)

For **plane area**, the error between the true region and the flat-topped rectangle in each subinterval is approximately the area of the small right triangle of base $\Delta x$ and height $\Delta y$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
E_A &\approx \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{2} \Delta x \cdot \Delta y \\
    &\approx \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{2} f'(x_k) \, \Delta x^2
\end{aligned}
$$
Similarly, for **volume of revolution**, the extra ring swept out by that small triangle has volume roughly equal to the triangle's area $\frac{1}{2} \Delta x \Delta y$ times the circumference $2\pi f(x_k)$:
$$
E_V \approx \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{2} \Delta x \cdot \Delta y \cdot 2\pi f(x_k) = \sum_{k=1}^n \pi f(x_k) f'(x_k) \, \Delta x^2
$$
In both area and volume, the error in each slice is **second-order**—that is, proportional to $\Delta x^2$—so when we sum over $n \sim \frac{1}{\Delta x}$ slices, the total error is still proportional to $\Delta x$ and vanishes completely as $\Delta x \to 0$.

Now look at what happens with **surface area**. Over the $k$-th subinterval, the true locally linear piece of the revolved surface is the frustum of a cone with radii $r_1 = f(x_{k-1})$ and $r_2 = f(x_k)$ and slant height $\Delta s_k = \sqrt{\Delta x_k^2 + \Delta y_k^2}$, whose lateral surface area is $\pi (r_1 + r_2) \Delta s_k = \pi (f(x_{k-1}) + f(x_k)) \sqrt{\Delta x_k^2 + \Delta y_k^2}$. Meanwhile, the flat cylinder gives $2\pi f(x_k) \Delta x_k$. Their difference is:
$$
\begin{aligned}
E_S &= \sum_{k=1}^n \pi \left[ (f(x_k) + f(x_{k-1})) \sqrt{\Delta x_k^2 + \Delta y_k^2} - 2 f(x_k) \, \Delta x_k \right] \\
    &= \sum_{k=1}^n \pi \left[ (r_1 + r_2)\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\Delta y_k}{\Delta x_k}\right)^2} - 2 f(x_k) \right] \Delta x_k
\end{aligned}
$$
Here, the error in each slice is **first-order**—proportional to $\Delta x_k$ rather than $\Delta x_k^2$! Because the bracketed factor does not vanish whenever the slope $\frac{\Delta y_k}{\Delta x_k} \neq 0$, summing $n$ first-order errors accumulates into a macroscopic discrepancy that never goes to zero as $\Delta x_k \to 0$.

## Approximating with Frustums: The Surface Area Formula

Our error analysis points directly to the right geometry: instead of approximating each slice with a cylinder, we must approximate it with the **frustum of a cone** formed by revolving the chord connecting $(x_{k-1}, f(x_{k-1}))$ and $(x_k, f(x_k))$.

![](Pasted%20image%2020260926200738.webp)

For the $k$-th subinterval, the approximating frustum has radii $r_1 = f(x_{k-1})$ and $r_2 = f(x_k)$ and slant height $\sqrt{\Delta x_k^2 + \Delta y_k^2}$, so its lateral surface area $S_k$ is:
$$
\begin{aligned}
S_k &= \pi (r_1 + r_2) \sqrt{\Delta x_k^2 + \Delta y_k^2} \\
    &= \pi (f(x_{k-1}) + f(x_k)) \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\Delta y_k}{\Delta x_k}\right)^2} \, \Delta x_k
\end{aligned}
$$
Summing over all $n$ subintervals gives the total frustum approximation:
$$
S \approx \sum_{k=1}^n \pi (f(x_{k-1}) + f(x_k)) \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\Delta y_k}{\Delta x_k}\right)^2} \, \Delta x_k
$$
When $f$ is smooth and $f(x) \geq 0$ on $[a, b]$, **Bliss's Theorem** from advanced calculus guarantees that as the partition norm $\lvert P \rvert \to 0$, both $f(x_{k-1})$ and $f(x_k)$ merge into $f(x)$ (giving $2\pi f(x)$) and the sum converges to a single definite integral.

> [!abstract] Definition: Surface Area for Revolution About the $x$-Axis
> If the function $f(x) \geq 0$ is smooth on $[a, b]$, the area $S$ of the surface generated by revolving the curve $y = f(x)$ about the $x$-axis from $x = a$ to $x = b$ is:
> $$
> S = \int_a^b 2\pi y \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2} \, dx = \int_a^b 2\pi f(x) \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, dx
> $$

> [!example] Example: Surface Area of a Parabolic Cap About the $x$-Axis
> Find the area of the surface generated by revolving the curve $y = 2\sqrt{x}$, $1 \leq x \leq 2$, about the $x$-axis.

**Solution:**
First, we compute the derivative $y' = \frac{d}{dx}(2\sqrt{x}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$. Substituting $y = 2\sqrt{x}$ and $y' = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$ into the surface area formula gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
S &= \int_1^2 2\pi (2\sqrt{x}) \sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{x}} \, dx \\
  &= 4\pi \int_1^2 \sqrt{x + 1} \, dx \\
  &= 4\pi \left[ \frac{(1 + x)^{3/2}}{3/2} \right]_1^2 \\
  &= \frac{8\pi}{3} (3\sqrt{3} - 2\sqrt{2})
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

## Rotation About the $y$-Axis and the Short Differential Form

If a smooth curve $x = g(y) \geq 0$ on $c \leq y \leq d$ is revolved about the **$y$-axis**, the roles of $x$ and $y$ simply interchange: the radius from the $y$-axis to the curve is $x$, and the arc length element is $\sqrt{1 + (x')^2} \, dy$:
$$
S = \int_c^d 2\pi x \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dx}{dy}\right)^2} \, dy
$$
Even better, we can unite all surface area formulas into a single, intuitive **short differential form** using the arc length differential $ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2}$:
$$
S = \int 2\pi \rho \, ds
$$
where $\rho$ is the perpendicular distance (radius) from the axis of revolution to the point on the curve, and $ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2}$ is the infinitesimal arc length along the curve. Written this way, you never have to memorize separate formulas—just identify the radius $\rho$ from the axis of rotation and expand $ds$ in terms of whichever variable ($dx$ or $dy$) is more convenient!

> [!example] Example: Using the Differential Form for Surface Area
> Find the area of the surface of revolution generated by revolving the curve $y = x^3$, $0 \leq x \leq \frac{1}{2}$, about the $x$-axis.

**Solution:**
Using $S = \int 2\pi \rho \, ds$ for rotation about the $x$-axis, the radius from the $x$-axis to the curve is $\rho = y = x^3$, and the arc length differential is:
$$
ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2} = \sqrt{dx^2 + (3x^2 \, dx)^2} = \sqrt{1 + 9x^4} \, dx
$$
Integrating from $x = 0$ to $x = \frac{1}{2}$, we use the substitution $u = 9x^4 + 1$, so $du = 36x^3 \, dx$, with $u = 1$ at $x = 0$ and $u = \frac{25}{16}$ at $x = \frac{1}{2}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
S &= \int_0^{1/2} 2\pi x^3 \sqrt{9x^4 + 1} \, dx \\
  &= \int_1^{25/16} 2\pi \sqrt{u} \, \frac{du}{36} \\
  &= \frac{\pi}{18} \int_1^{25/16} u^{1/2} \, du \\
  &= \frac{\pi}{18} \cdot \frac{2}{3} \left[ u^{3/2} \right]_1^{25/16} \\
  &= \frac{\pi}{27} \left[ \frac{125}{64} - 1 \right] \\
  &= \frac{61\pi}{27 \cdot 64} = \frac{61\pi}{1728}
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

## Surfaces That Cross the Axis of Revolution

What if the curve we revolve crosses the axis of revolution so that $f(x)$ or $f(y)$ becomes negative on part of the interval? Because the geometric radius $\rho$ in $S = \int 2\pi \rho \, ds$ represents a physical distance from the axis of revolution, it can never be negative. Thus, when a curve takes negative values, we simply use its absolute value $\rho = \lvert f(x) \rvert$ (or $\lvert f(y) \rvert$):
$$
S = \int 2\pi \lvert f(x) \rvert \, ds \quad \text{or} \quad S = \int 2\pi \lvert f(y) \rvert \, ds
$$

## 5.6 Exercises

### Problem 21: Surface Area of a Curve $x = g(y)$ Revolved About the $x$-Axis

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Find the area of the surface generated by revolving the curve $x = \frac{y^4}{4} + \frac{1}{8y^2}$, $1 \leq y \leq 2$, about the $x$-axis.

**Solution:**
Even though the curve is revolved about the $x$-axis (so the radius from the axis of revolution is $\rho = y$), $x$ is given as a function of $y$, so it is much easier to express $ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2}$ in terms of $dy$ and integrate from $y = 1$ to $y = 2$ (where $x$ goes from $x(1) = \frac{3}{8}$ to $x(2) = \frac{129}{32}$). Differentiating $x$ with respect to $y$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
dx &= \left(y^3 - \frac{1}{4y^3}\right) dy = \frac{4y^6 - 1}{4y^3} \, dy \\
dx^2 &= \frac{16y^{12} - 8y^6 + 1}{16y^6} \, dy^2
\end{aligned}
$$
Substituting $dx^2$ into $ds = \sqrt{dy^2 + dx^2}$ forms a perfect square under the radical:
$$
\begin{aligned}
ds &= \sqrt{1 + \frac{16y^{12} - 8y^6 + 1}{16y^6}} \, dy \\
   &= \frac{\sqrt{16y^{12} + 8y^6 + 1}}{4y^3} \, dy \\
   &= \frac{4y^6 + 1}{4y^3} \, dy
\end{aligned}
$$
Now we integrate $2\pi \rho \, ds = 2\pi y \, ds$ over the interval $1 \leq y \leq 2$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
S &= \int_1^2 2\pi y \left( \frac{4y^6 + 1}{4y^3} \right) dy \\
  &= \frac{\pi}{2} \int_1^2 \left( 4y^4 + \frac{1}{y^2} \right) dy \\
  &= \frac{\pi}{2} \left[ \frac{4y^5}{5} - \frac{1}{y} \right]_1^2 \\
  &= \frac{\pi}{2} \left[ \left(\frac{128}{5} - \frac{1}{2}\right) - \left(\frac{4}{5} - 1\right) \right] \\
  &= \frac{253\pi}{20}
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 26: Surface Area of a Revolved Astroid

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Find the area of the surface generated by revolving about the $x$-axis the upper portion of the astroid $x^{2/3} + y^{2/3} = 1$ (for $-1 \leq x \leq 1, \, y \geq 0$).

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: Astroid curve $x^{2/3} + y^{2/3} = 1$ in the upper half-plane extending from $x = -1$ to $x = 1$

**Solution:**
By symmetry across the $y$-axis, we can compute the surface area generated by revolving the first-quadrant portion ($0 \leq x \leq 1$) about the $x$-axis and multiply the result by $2$. In the first quadrant:
$$
x^{2/3} + y^{2/3} = 1 \implies y = (1 - x^{2/3})^{3/2}
$$
Differentiating $y$ with respect to $x$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
y' &= \frac{3}{2}(1 - x^{2/3})^{1/2} \cdot \left(-\frac{2}{3} x^{-1/3}\right) = -\frac{(1 - x^{2/3})^{1/2}}{x^{1/3}} \\
(y')^2 &= \frac{1 - x^{2/3}}{x^{2/3}} = \frac{1}{x^{2/3}} - 1 \implies \sqrt{1 + (y')^2} = \frac{1}{x^{1/3}}
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus, the surface area $S_1$ generated by the first-quadrant arc is:
$$
\begin{aligned}
S_1 &= \int_{0}^{1} 2\pi y \sqrt{1 + (y')^2} \, dx \\
    &= \int_{0}^{1} 2\pi (1 - x^{2/3})^{3/2} \frac{1}{x^{1/3}} \, dx
\end{aligned}
$$
Let $u = 1 - x^{2/3}$, so $du = -\frac{2}{3x^{1/3}} \, dx \implies \frac{dx}{x^{1/3}} = -\frac{3}{2} \, du$, with $u = 1$ at $x = 0$ and $u = 0$ at $x = 1$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
S_1 &= \pi \int_{1}^{0} u^{3/2} (-3 \, du) \\
    &= -3\pi \left[ \frac{u^{5/2}}{5/2} \right]_1^0 \\
    &= -\frac{6\pi}{5} [0 - 1] = \frac{6\pi}{5}
\end{aligned}
$$
Doubling $S_1$ to include the second-quadrant half gives the total surface area:
$$
S = 2 S_1 = \frac{12\pi}{5}
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 27: Enamel Coating Required for Spherical-Cap Woks

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Estimate the amount of enamel required to apply a $0.5\text{ mm}$ thick coating of enamel on both the inside and outside of a wok shaped like a spherical cap (generated by revolving the circular arc $x^2 + y^2 = 16^2\text{ cm}^2$ for $-16 \leq y \leq -7$, $-3\sqrt{23} \leq x \leq 3\sqrt{23}$ about the $y$-axis). Give the total volume of enamel required for $5000$ woks in liters.

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: Cross-section of a spherical-cap wok with radius $16\text{ cm}$ spanning $y \in [-16, -7]$ and $x \in [-3\sqrt{23}, 3\sqrt{23}]$

**Solution:**
> [!warning] Caution: Choosing the Correct Axis of Revolution
> A common mistake is to revolve the profile curve about the horizontal chord $y = -7$ (or $y = 7$), which generates a spindle-like shape rather than a bowl! Because a wok is radially symmetric around its vertical central axis, we must revolve the circular arc $x = f(y) = \sqrt{16^2 - y^2}$ for $-16 \leq y \leq -7$ about the **$y$-axis**.

Revolving $x = f(y) = \sqrt{16^2 - y^2}$ ($-16 \leq y \leq -7$) about the $y$-axis, we have $f'(y) = \frac{-y}{\sqrt{16^2 - y^2}}$, so the surface area of a single wok is:
$$
\begin{aligned}
S &= \int_{-16}^{-7} 2\pi f(y) \sqrt{1 + [f'(y)]^2} \, dy \\
  &= \int_{-16}^{-7} 2\pi \sqrt{16^2 - y^2} \cdot \sqrt{1 + \frac{y^2}{16^2 - y^2}} \, dy \\
  &= \int_{-16}^{-7} 2\pi \cdot 16 \, dy \\
  &= 32\pi [-7 - (-16)] = 288\pi\text{ cm}^2
\end{aligned}
$$
Because the $0.5\text{ mm} = \frac{1}{20}\text{ cm}$ enamel coating is applied to *both* the inside and outside surfaces of each wok, the volume of enamel needed per wok is:
$$
V_{\text{wok}} \approx 2 \times \text{Area} \times \text{thickness} = 2 \times 288\pi \times \frac{1}{20} = 28.8\pi\text{ cm}^3
$$
For $5000$ woks (using $1\text{ L} = 1000\text{ cm}^3$), the total volume of enamel required is:
$$
V_{\text{total}} = \frac{5000 \times 28.8\pi}{1000} = 144\pi\text{ L} \approx 452.39\text{ L}
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Problem 28: Surface Area of a Spherical Zone of Width $h$

> [!abstract] Problem Statement
> Prove that the surface area of a slice (zone) of width $h$ cut out from a sphere of radius $r$ by two parallel planes is always $2\pi r h$, regardless of where the slice is cut from the sphere.

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: A spherical zone of thickness $h$ over $[a, a+h]$ cut from a sphere of radius $r$

**Solution:**
Generate the sphere of radius $r$ by revolving the upper semicircle $f(x) = \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}$ about the $x$-axis, and consider a slice of width $h$ between $x = a$ and $x = a + h$ (where $-r \leq a < a + h \leq r$). First, notice how the arc length factor simplifies:
$$
[f'(x)]^2 + 1 = \frac{x^2}{r^2 - x^2} + 1 = \frac{r^2}{r^2 - x^2}
$$
Computing the surface area of the slice over $[a, a + h]$ gives:
$$
\begin{aligned}
S &= \int_{a}^{a+h} 2\pi f(x)\sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, dx \\
  &= \int_{a}^{a+h} 2\pi \sqrt{r^2 - x^2} \cdot \frac{r}{\sqrt{r^2 - x^2}} \, dx \\
  &= 2\pi r [x]_a^{a+h} = 2\pi r h
\end{aligned}
$$
Notice that the $\sqrt{r^2 - x^2}$ factors cancel completely! Because the resulting surface area $S = 2\pi r h$ depends only on the sphere's radius $r$ and the slice width $h$—and is completely independent of the starting position $a$—every slice of width $h$ on the sphere has the exact same surface area. $\blacksquare$

# 5.7 Moments and Center of Mass

## Moment of Mass and Center of Mass in One Dimension

Why does a seesaw balance when the fulcrum is placed at just the right spot, and how can we find that balance point for a continuous rod, flat plate, or curved wire? In physics, the tendency of a mass to produce a rotation about a pivot point under gravity is measured by its **torque** (or **moment**).

Consider a system of discrete point masses $m_1, m_2, \dots, m_n$ located at signed positions $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ along the $x$-axis.

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: A horizontal rod along the $x$-axis balanced on a fulcrum, with point masses $m_1, m_2, \dots, m_5$ at signed distances from the origin

If gravity acts downward with acceleration $g$, the $k$-th mass exerts a downward force (weight) $m_k g$ at signed lever arm $x_k$, producing a torque of $m_k x_k g$ about the origin. Summing over all masses gives the total **system torque** about the origin:
$$
\text{System Torque} = \sum_{k=1}^n m_k x_k g = \left(\sum_{k=1}^n m_k x_k\right) g = M_0 g
$$
Notice that the gravitational constant $g$ factors out completely! The purely geometric and mass-dependent factor $M_0$ is called the **moment of the system about the origin** (also known as the **first moment** or **static moment**).

> [!abstract] Definition: Moment About the Origin and Center of Mass (1D)
> For a system of point masses $m_k$ at signed positions $x_k$ along the $x$-axis:
> 1. The **moment about the origin** is:
>    $$
>    M_0 = \sum_{k=1}^n m_k x_k
>    $$
> 2. About any arbitrary pivot point $x$ on the $x$-axis, the moment is $M_x = \sum_{k=1}^n m_k (x_k - x)$, with system torque $M_x g$.
> 3. The **center of mass** ($\text{COM}$), denoted $\bar{x}$, is the unique balance point where placing a fulcrum results in zero net torque ($M_{\bar{x}} = 0$):
>    $$
>    \sum_{k=1}^n m_k (x_k - \bar{x}) = 0 \implies \bar{x} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^n m_k x_k}{\sum_{k=1}^n m_k} = \frac{\text{Moment about origin}}{\text{Total mass}} = \frac{M_0}{M}
>    $$

## Center of Mass of a Thin Rod

How do we pass from discrete point masses to a continuous **thin rod** lying along the $x$-axis over $[a, b]$? Calling the rod "thin" simply means we can model it as a one-dimensional object whose density $\delta(x)$ (mass per unit length) varies only with $x$.

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: A 1D thin rod along the $x$-axis from $x = a$ to $x = b$, showing a typical segment of width $\Delta x_k$ at distance $x_k$ from the origin

To find its center of mass, we partition $[a, b]$ into $n$ small segments of length $\Delta x_k$. If the linear density $\delta(x)$ is continuous, the mass of the $k$-th segment is approximately $\Delta m_k \approx \delta(x_k) \Delta x_k$, and its moment about the origin is approximately $x_k \Delta m_k \approx x_k \delta(x_k) \Delta x_k$. Thus:
$$
\bar{x} \approx \frac{\sum_{k=1}^n x_k \, \Delta m_k}{\sum_{k=1}^n \Delta m_k} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^n x_k \delta(x_k) \, \Delta x_k}{\sum_{k=1}^n \delta(x_k) \, \Delta x_k}
$$
As we refine the partition ($\lvert P \rvert \to 0$), both Riemann sums converge to definite integrals:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Moment about origin: } M_0 &= \int_a^b x \delta(x) \, dx \\
\text{Total Mass: } M &= \int_a^b \delta(x) \, dx \\
\text{Center of Mass: } \bar{x} &= \frac{M_0}{M} = \frac{\int_a^b x \delta(x) \, dx}{\int_a^b \delta(x) \, dx}
\end{aligned}
$$

> [!example] Example: Center of Mass of a Rod of Constant Density
> Show that the center of mass of a straight, thin rod or strip of length $L$ and constant density $\delta$ lies halfway between its two ends.

**Solution:**
Place the rod along the $x$-axis over the interval $[0, L]$, with constant density $\delta(x) = \delta$ at every point. Computing the moment about the origin $M_0$ and the total mass $M$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
M_0 &= \int_{0}^{L} x \delta \, dx = \delta \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} \right]_0^L = \frac{\delta L^2}{2} \\
M &= \int_{0}^{L} \delta \, dx = \delta L \\
\bar{x} &= \frac{M_0}{M} = \frac{\delta L^2 / 2}{\delta L} = \frac{L}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus, the center of mass lies at $\bar{x} = \frac{L}{2}$, right at the midpoint of the rod. $\blacksquare$

## Masses Distributed Over a Plane Region

When masses are spread across a two-dimensional plane rather than a single line, we measure their tendency to rotate about the **coordinate axes** rather than a single point (imagining gravity acting perpendicular to the plane, along the $z$-axis).

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: Point masses scattered in the 2D Cartesian plane, with a typical mass $m_k$ at $(x_k, y_k)$

For a collection of point masses $m_k$ located at coordinates $(x_k, y_k)$ in the $xy$-plane:
- The **moment about the $y$-axis**, $M_y$, uses the signed horizontal distance $x_k$ from the $y$-axis:
  $$
  M_y = \sum_{k=1}^n m_k x_k
  $$
- The **moment about the $x$-axis**, $M_x$, uses the signed vertical distance $y_k$ from the $x$-axis:
  $$
  M_x = \sum_{k=1}^n m_k y_k
  $$

Consequently, the coordinates $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$ of the two-dimensional **center of mass** are:
$$
\bar{x} = \frac{M_y}{M} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^n m_k x_k}{\sum_{k=1}^n m_k}, \quad \bar{y} = \frac{M_x}{M} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^n m_k y_k}{\sum_{k=1}^n m_k}
$$
Physically, $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$ is the single point where you could balance the entire planar system on the tip of a pin without it toppling over.

## Center of Mass of a Thin Flat Plate

Now consider a continuous **thin flat plate** (lamina) occupying a region in the $xy$-plane. Just as "thin" made a rod one-dimensional, calling a plate "thin" means we can treat it as a two-dimensional surface with areal density $\delta(x, y)$ (mass per unit area).

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: A 2D thin plate region in the first quadrant partitioned into vertical strips of width $\Delta x_k$

To find the center of mass $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$ of the plate, we slice the region into thin strips—either vertical strips of width $dx$ or horizontal strips of height $dy$. Consider the $k$-th strip of mass $\Delta m_k$, and let $(\tilde{x}_k, \tilde{y}_k)$ denote the center of mass of that individual strip alone. (For instance, if the plate has constant density $\delta$, the center of mass $(\tilde{x}_k, \tilde{y}_k)$ of a thin rectangular strip is simply its geometric midpoint!)

By replacing each strip with an equivalent point mass $\Delta m_k$ concentrated at its strip center $(\tilde{x}_k, \tilde{y}_k)$, we approximate the plate's overall center of mass by:
$$
\bar{x} \approx \frac{\sum_{k=1}^n \tilde{x}_k \, \Delta m_k}{\sum_{k=1}^n \Delta m_k}, \quad \bar{y} \approx \frac{\sum_{k=1}^n \tilde{y}_k \, \Delta m_k}{\sum_{k=1}^n \Delta m_k}
$$
Taking the limit as the strip widths approach zero turns these sums into integrals over the differential mass element $dm$:

> [!abstract] Definition: Moments and Center of Mass of a Thin Flat Plate
> If a thin flat plate is sliced into infinitesimal strips of mass $dm$ whose individual strip centers of mass are located at $(\tilde{x}, \tilde{y})$, then:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \text{Moment about } y\text{-axis: } M_y &= \int \tilde{x} \, dm \\
> \text{Moment about } x\text{-axis: } M_x &= \int \tilde{y} \, dm \\
> \text{Total Mass: } M &= \int dm \\
> \text{Center of Mass: } (\bar{x}, \bar{y}) &= \left(\frac{M_y}{M}, \, \frac{M_x}{M}\right)
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> When the density $\delta$ is constant throughout the plate, it cancels out of the numerator and denominator, and $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$ is called the **centroid** of the plane region.

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Vertical vs. Horizontal Strips
> Both vertical strips ($dx$) and horizontal strips ($dy$) always lead to the exact same center of mass $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$, so you can choose whichever strip orientation matches the region's boundaries more cleanly.

> [!example] Example: Center of Mass of a Triangular Plate via Vertical and Horizontal Strips
> Find the center of mass of the right-angled triangular plate with vertices at $(0, 0)$, $(1, 0)$, and $(1, 2)$ if it has constant density $\delta = 3\text{ g/cm}^2$. Compute the answer using both vertical strips and horizontal strips to verify that they agree.

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: Right triangle with vertices $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, and $(1,2)$ bounded above by the hypotenuse $y = 2x$

**Solution:**
The hypotenuse connecting $(0, 0)$ to $(1, 2)$ is the line $y = 2x$.

**Method 1 (Vertical Strips):**
Slice the triangle into vertical strips of width $dx$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 1$. At position $x$, the strip has height $y = 2x$, differential mass $dm = \delta y \, dx = 2\delta x \, dx$, and strip midpoint $(\tilde{x}, \tilde{y}) = \left(x, \frac{y}{2}\right) = (x, x)$.

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: Triangle showing a vertical strip of width $dx$ and height $y = 2x$ at position $x$, with $dm = \delta y \, dx$

Integrating from $x = 0$ to $x = 1$ (with $\delta = 3$):
$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \int_0^1 dm = \int_0^1 \delta y \, dx = 2\delta \int_0^1 x \, dx = \delta [x^2]_0^1 = \delta = 3 \\
M_x &= \int_0^1 \tilde{y} \, dm = \int_0^1 \frac{y}{2} \cdot \delta y \, dx = 2\delta \int_0^1 x^2 \, dx = 2\delta \cdot \frac{1}{3} = 2 \\
M_y &= \int_0^1 \tilde{x} \, dm = \int_0^1 x \cdot \delta y \, dx = 2\delta \int_0^1 x^2 \, dx = 2\delta \cdot \frac{1}{3} = 2 \\
(\bar{x}, \bar{y}) &= \left(\frac{M_y}{M}, \frac{M_x}{M}\right) = \left(\frac{2}{3}, \frac{2}{3}\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

**Method 2 (Horizontal Strips):**
Slice the triangle into horizontal strips of thickness $dy$ from $y = 0$ to $y = 2$. At height $y$, the strip stretches from $x = \frac{y}{2}$ on the left to $x = 1$ on the right, so its length is $1 - x = 1 - \frac{y}{2}$ and its differential mass is $dm = \delta \left(1 - \frac{y}{2}\right) dy$.

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: Triangle showing a horizontal strip of thickness $dy$ and length $1 - x$ at height $y$, with $dm = \delta(1 - x) \, dy$

The strip's center of mass has vertical coordinate $\tilde{y} = y$ and horizontal midpoint $\tilde{x}$:
$$
\tilde{x} = x + \frac{1 - x}{2} = \frac{x + 1}{2} = \frac{\frac{y}{2} + 1}{2} = \frac{y + 2}{4}
$$
Integrating from $y = 0$ to $y = 2$ (with $\delta = 3$):
$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \int_0^2 dm = \int_0^2 \delta \left(1 - \frac{y}{2}\right) dy = \frac{\delta}{2} \left[ 2y - \frac{y^2}{2} \right]_0^2 = \delta = 3 \\
M_x &= \int_0^2 \tilde{y} \, dm = \int_0^2 y \cdot \delta \left(1 - \frac{y}{2}\right) dy = \frac{\delta}{2} \left[ y^2 - \frac{y^3}{3} \right]_0^2 = \frac{\delta}{2} \left(4 - \frac{8}{3}\right) = \frac{2\delta}{3} = 2 \\
M_y &= \int_0^2 \tilde{x} \, dm = \int_0^2 \left( \frac{2 + y}{4} \right) \delta \left( \frac{2 - y}{2} \right) dy = \frac{\delta}{8} \left[ 4y - \frac{y^3}{3} \right]_0^2 = \frac{\delta}{8} \left(8 - \frac{8}{3}\right) = \frac{2\delta}{3} = 2 \\
(\bar{x}, \bar{y}) &= \left(\frac{M_y}{M}, \frac{M_x}{M}\right) = \left(\frac{2}{3}, \frac{2}{3}\right)
\end{aligned}
$$
Both methods yield the exact same center of mass $\left(\frac{2}{3}, \frac{2}{3}\right)$, which matches the geometric centroid of the triangle (the average of its three vertices). $\blacksquare$

> [!example] Example: Center of Mass of a Parabolic Plate with Constant Density
> Find the center of mass of a thin plate of constant density $\delta$ covering the region bounded above by the parabola $y = 4 - x^2$ and below by the $x$-axis.

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: Downward-opening parabola $y = 4 - x^2$ intersecting the $x$-axis at $-2$ and $2$, with a vertical strip of width $dx$ and height $y$ at position $x$

**Solution:**
Using vertical strips of width $dx$ over $-2 \leq x \leq 2$, each strip has height $y = 4 - x^2$, midpoint $(\tilde{x}, \tilde{y}) = \left(x, \frac{y}{2}\right) = \left(x, \frac{4 - x^2}{2}\right)$, and mass $dm = \delta y \, dx = \delta (4 - x^2) \, dx$. First, we compute the total mass $M$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \int_{-2}^2 dm = \delta \int_{-2}^2 (4 - x^2) \, dx \\
  &= \delta \left[ 4x - \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_{-2}^2 = \frac{32\delta}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$
Next, we compute the moment about the $x$-axis, $M_x$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
M_x &= \int_{-2}^2 \tilde{y} \, dm = \int_{-2}^2 \frac{y}{2} \, dm \\
    &= \int_{-2}^2 \frac{4 - x^2}{2} \cdot \delta (4 - x^2) \, dx \\
    &= \frac{\delta}{2} \int_{-2}^2 (16 - 8x^2 + x^4) \, dx \\
    &= \frac{\delta}{2} \left[ 16x - \frac{8x^3}{3} + \frac{x^5}{5} \right]_{-2}^2 \\
    &= 32\delta \left[ 1 - \frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{5} \right] = 32\delta \cdot \frac{15 - 10 + 3}{15} = \frac{256\delta}{15}
\end{aligned}
$$
For the moment about the $y$-axis, notice that the integrand is an odd function on $[-2, 2]$ (reflecting symmetry about the $y$-axis):
$$
M_y = \int_{-2}^2 \tilde{x} \, dm = \int_{-2}^2 x \cdot \delta (4 - x^2) \, dx = \delta \left[ \frac{4x^2}{2} - \frac{x^4}{4} \right]_{-2}^2 = 0
$$
Dividing the moments by $M$ gives the center of mass:
$$
\bar{x} = \frac{M_y}{M} = 0, \quad \bar{y} = \frac{M_x}{M} = \frac{256\delta / 15}{32\delta / 3} = \frac{8}{5} \implies (\bar{x}, \bar{y}) = \left(0, \frac{8}{5}\right)
$$
$\blacksquare$

> [!example] Example: Center of Mass of a Parabolic Plate with Variable Density
> Find the center of mass of the parabolic plate from the previous example ($y = 4 - x^2$, $y \geq 0$) if the density at any point $(x, y)$ is $\delta(x) = 2x^2$.

**Solution:**
Because both the region and the density function $\delta(x) = 2x^2$ are symmetric about the $y$-axis, the horizontal coordinate of the center of mass is still $\bar{x} = 0$. Furthermore, along any vertical strip at position $x$, the density $\delta(x) = 2x^2$ does not depend on $y$, so the strip's vertical center of mass is still at its midpoint $\tilde{y} = \frac{y}{2} = \frac{4 - x^2}{2}$.

Computing the differential mass $dm$ and total mass $M$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
dm &= 2x^2 \cdot y \, dx = 2x^2 (4 - x^2) \, dx = 2(4x^2 - x^4) \, dx \\
M &= \int_{-2}^2 2(4x^2 - x^4) \, dx \\
  &= 2 \left[ \frac{4x^3}{3} - \frac{x^5}{5} \right]_{-2}^2 \\
  &= 4 \cdot 32 \left[ \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{5} \right] = \frac{256}{15}
\end{aligned}
$$
Computing the moment about the $x$-axis, $M_x$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
M_x &= \int_{-2}^2 \tilde{y} \, dm = \int_{-2}^2 \frac{1}{2}(4 - x^2) \cdot 2(4x^2 - x^4) \, dx \\
    &= \int_{-2}^2 (16x^2 - 8x^4 + x^6) \, dx \\
    &= \left[ \frac{16x^3}{3} - \frac{8}{5} x^5 + \frac{x^7}{7} \right]_{-2}^2 \\
    &= 2 \left[ \frac{128}{3} - \frac{256}{5} + \frac{128}{7} \right] \\
    &= 256 \left( \frac{1}{3} - \frac{2}{5} + \frac{1}{7} \right) \\
    &= 256 \left( \frac{35 - 42 + 15}{105} \right) = 256 \cdot \frac{8}{105} = \frac{2048}{105}
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore, the vertical coordinate of the center of mass is:
$$
\bar{y} = \frac{M_x}{M} = \frac{2048}{105} \cdot \frac{15}{256} = \frac{8}{7} \implies (\bar{x}, \bar{y}) = \left(0, \frac{8}{7}\right)
$$
Notice that because the plate is heavier near its outer edges ($x = \pm 2$) where the plate is shorter, its center of mass $\left(0, \frac{8}{7}\right)$ sits lower than the constant-density centroid $\left(0, \frac{8}{5}\right)$. $\blacksquare$

## Center of Mass of a Wire or Curved Arc

For a thin wire bent along a smooth plane curve with linear density $\delta$ (mass per unit arc length), an infinitesimal element of the wire at $(x, y)$ has arc length $ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2}$ and differential mass $dm = \delta \, ds$. The moments and center of mass are computed using the exact same integral formulas $\bar{x} = \frac{M_y}{M}$ and $\bar{y} = \frac{M_x}{M}$ with $\tilde{x} = x$, $\tilde{y} = y$, and $dm = \delta \, ds$.

> [!example] Example: Center of Mass of a Semicircular Wire
> Find the center of mass of a wire with constant density $\delta$ shaped like the upper semicircle of radius $a$ ($y = \sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$, $-a \leq x \leq a$).

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: A semicircular wire of radius $a$ in the upper half-plane, showing an arc element $ds = a \, d\theta$ at angle $\theta$ and position $(x, y)$

**Solution:**
By symmetry about the $y$-axis, $\bar{x} = 0$. Along the semicircle $y = \sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$, an infinitesimal piece of wire has arc length $ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2}$ and mass $dm = \delta \, ds = \delta \sqrt{1 + (y')^2} \, dx$. Since:
$$
y' = \frac{-x}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} \implies 1 + (y')^2 = \frac{a^2}{a^2 - x^2} \implies ds = \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} \, dx
$$
we can evaluate the integrals either by parameterizing the circle with the polar angle $\theta$ or by integrating directly with respect to $x$.

**Method 1 (Integrating in terms of $\theta$):**
In polar coordinates, $x = a\cos\theta$ and $y = a\sin\theta$ for $0 \leq \theta \leq \pi$. A small angle $d\theta$ subtends an arc of length $ds = a \, d\theta$, so $dm = \delta a \, d\theta$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \int_0^\pi dm = \int_0^\pi \delta a \, d\theta = \delta \pi a \\
M_x &= \int_0^\pi \tilde{y} \, dm = \int_0^\pi (a\sin\theta) \cdot \delta a \, d\theta = \delta a^2 [-\cos\theta]_0^\pi = 2\delta a^2 \\
\bar{y} &= \frac{M_x}{M} = \frac{2\delta a^2}{\delta \pi a} = \frac{2a}{\pi}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Method 2 (Integrating in terms of $x$):**
Since $x = a\cos\theta$, differentiating gives $dx = -a\sin\theta \, d\theta = -y \, d\theta$, so As $x$ increases from $-a$ to $a$, the positive arc length differential is $ds = \frac{a \, dx}{y} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} \, dx$. Integrating from $x = -a$ to $x = a$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \int_{-a}^a \delta \, ds = \delta a \int_{-a}^a \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} \, dx \\
  &= \delta a \left[ \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) \right]_{-a}^a = \delta a \left[ \frac{\pi}{2} - \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right) \right] = \delta \pi a \\
M_x &= \int_{-a}^a y \delta \, ds = \int_{-a}^a y \cdot \delta \frac{a \, dx}{y} = \delta a [x]_{-a}^a = 2\delta a^2 \\
\bar{y} &= \frac{M_x}{M} = \frac{2\delta a^2}{\delta \pi a} = \frac{2a}{\pi}
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus, the center of mass of the semicircular wire is $\left(0, \frac{2a}{\pi}\right)$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note] Theoretical Remark: Evaluating $\int \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} \, dx$
> In Method 2 above, we used the standard antiderivative $\int \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} \, dx = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) + C$. This follows immediately from the trigonometric substitution $x = a\sin\alpha$ (so $\alpha = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right)$ and $dx = a\cos\alpha \, d\alpha$):
> $$
> \int \frac{a\cos\alpha \, d\alpha}{\sqrt{a^2 - a^2\sin^2\alpha}} = \int \frac{a\cos\alpha \, d\alpha}{a\cos\alpha} = \alpha + C = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) + C
> $$

# 5.10 Delesse's Rule and the Theorems of Pappus

## Delesse's Rule

Suppose a geologists' rock sample or a biological tissue block is modeled as a solid cube of side length $L$ composed of different materials, and we want to determine what fraction of the cube's total volume is occupied by a specific constituent material $A$. How can we estimate a 3D volume proportion merely by looking at 2D cross-sectional slices under a microscope?

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: A cube of side length $L$ aligned with the coordinate axes, showing a thin cross-sectional slice of thickness $\Delta x_k$ at position $x_k$

Align the cube along the $x$-axis over $[0, L]$ and partition $[0, L]$ into thin slices of thickness $\Delta x_k$, each having cross-sectional face area $L^2$ and slice volume $L^2 \Delta x_k$. If we examine the face of the $k$-th slice at $x_k$ under a microscope, let $u(x_k)$ denote the **areal proportion** of material $A$ visible on that 2D surface (i.e., the area of $A$ on the slice divided by $L^2$). Because the slice is very thin, the volume of material $A$ inside the $k$-th slice is approximately:
$$
\text{Volume of } A \text{ in } k\text{-th slice} \approx u(x_k) \cdot L^2 \, \Delta x_k
$$
Summing over all slices gives the approximate total volume of material $A$ in the cube:
$$
V_A \approx \sum_{k=1}^n u(x_k) L^2 \, \Delta x_k
$$
Taking the limit as the partition norm approaches zero converts the sum into an integral:
$$
V_A = \int_0^L u(x) L^2 \, dx
$$
Dividing $V_A$ by the total volume $L^3$ of the cube gives the volumetric proportion of material $A$:
$$
\text{Volumetric Proportion of } A = \frac{V_A}{L^3} = \frac{L^2 \int_0^L u(x) \, dx}{L^3} = \frac{1}{L} \int_0^L u(x) \, dx
$$
Notice that $\frac{1}{L} \int_0^L u(x) \, dx$ is simply the **average value** of the 2D areal proportion $u(x)$ over $[0, L]$! This equivalence between average 2D cross-sectional area fraction and 3D volume fraction is known as **Delesse's Rule**.

## The Theorems of Pappus

In the fourth century A.D., Pappus of Alexandria discovered two elegant geometric theorems that connect centroids directly to the volumes and surface areas of solids of revolution. When you know any two of the three quantities (centroid, area/arc length, and volume/surface area), Pappus's theorems give you the third almost instantaneously!

### Theorem of Pappus for Volumes

> [!theorem] Theorem: Pappus's Theorem for Volumes
> If a plane region is revolved once about a line in the plane that does not cut through the region's interior, then the volume $V$ of the solid it generates is equal to the region's area $A$ times the distance $2\pi \rho$ traveled by the region's centroid during the revolution:
> $$
> V = 2\pi \rho A
> $$
> where $\rho$ is the perpendicular distance from the axis of revolution to the centroid of the region.

**Proof:**
Recall that the centroid is the center of mass of a flat region assuming constant density $\delta$. Choose coordinate axes so that the axis of revolution is the $x$-axis and the plane region lies in the upper half-plane ($y \geq 0$) between $y = c$ and $y = d$.

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: A plane region in the upper half-plane with a horizontal strip of length $L(y)$ and thickness $dy$ at height $y$

Slicing the region into horizontal strips of length $L(y)$ and thickness $dy$, the differential area of a strip at height $y$ is $dA = L(y) \, dy$, and the vertical coordinate $\bar{y}$ of the region's centroid (which equals its distance $\rho$ from the $x$-axis) is:
$$
\bar{y} = \frac{M_x}{M} = \frac{\int \tilde{y} \, dm}{\int dm} = \frac{\int y \delta \, dA}{\int \delta \, dA} = \frac{\int_c^d y L(y) \, dy}{A} \implies \int_c^d y L(y) \, dy = \bar{y} A
$$
Now compute the volume $V$ generated by revolving this region about the $x$-axis using the cylindrical shell method:
$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= \int_c^d 2\pi (\text{shell radius})(\text{shell height}) \, dy \\
  &= 2\pi \int_c^d y L(y) \, dy \\
  &= 2\pi \bar{y} A = 2\pi \rho A
\end{aligned}
$$
$\blacksquare$

> [!example] Example: Volume of a Torus via Pappus's Theorem
> Find the volume of the torus (donut) generated by revolving a circular disk of radius $a$ about the $y$-axis, given that the center of the circle lies at distance $b \geq a$ from the $y$-axis.

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: A circle of radius $a$ centered at distance $b \geq a$ from the $y$-axis, revolved about the $y$-axis to form a torus

**Solution:**
By symmetry, the centroid of the circular disk is right at its center, so its distance from the axis of revolution ($y$-axis) is $\rho = b$. The area of the disk is $A = \pi a^2$. By Pappus's Theorem for Volumes:
$$
V = 2\pi \rho A = (2\pi b)(\pi a^2) = 2\pi^2 a^2 b
$$
$\blacksquare$

> [!example] Example: Finding the Centroid of a Semicircular Region Using Pappus's Theorem
> Locate the centroid of a semicircular plane region of radius $a$.

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: A semicircular region of radius $a$ in the upper half-plane resting on the $x$-axis, centered at the origin

**Solution:**
Place the semicircle of radius $a$ in the upper half-plane with its flat diameter along the $x$-axis and centered at the origin. By symmetry, the centroid lies on the $y$-axis ($\bar{x} = 0$).

To find $\bar{y}$ without computing any new integrals, imagine revolving the semicircular region of area $A = \frac{1}{2}\pi a^2$ about the $x$-axis. The solid generated is a solid sphere of radius $a$, whose volume we know is $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi a^3$. Applying Pappus's Theorem for Volumes ($V = 2\pi \bar{y} A$):
$$
\begin{aligned}
2\pi \bar{y} \left(\frac{1}{2}\pi a^2\right) &= \frac{4}{3}\pi a^3 \\
\pi^2 a^2 \bar{y} &= \frac{4}{3}\pi a^3 \implies \bar{y} = \frac{4a}{3\pi}
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus, the centroid of the semicircular region is at $\left(0, \frac{4a}{3\pi}\right)$ (notice how this compares with $\left(0, \frac{2a}{\pi}\right)$ for a hollow semicircular wire!). $\blacksquare$

### Theorem of Pappus for Surface Areas

> [!theorem] Theorem: Pappus's Theorem for Surface Areas
> If an arc of a smooth plane curve is revolved once about a line in the plane that does not intersect the arc's interior, then the area $S$ of the surface of revolution is equal to the arc length $L$ times the distance $2\pi \rho$ traveled by the centroid of the arc during the revolution:
> $$
> S = 2\pi \rho L
> $$
> where $\rho$ is the perpendicular distance from the axis of revolution to the centroid of the curve, and $L$ is the length of the arc.

**Proof:**
Choose coordinate axes so that the axis of revolution is the $x$-axis and the smooth arc lies in the upper half-plane ($y \geq 0$) from $x = a$ to $x = b$.

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: A smooth arc in the first quadrant from $x = a$ to $x = b$, showing an arc element $ds$ at height $y$

For a curve of constant linear density $\delta$, the vertical coordinate $\bar{y}$ of the arc's centroid (which equals its distance $\rho$ from the $x$-axis) is:
$$
\bar{y} = \frac{\int_a^b \tilde{y} \, dm}{\int_a^b dm} = \frac{\int_a^b y \delta \, ds}{\int_a^b \delta \, ds} = \frac{\int_a^b y \, ds}{L} \implies \int_a^b y \, ds = \bar{y} L
$$
Meanwhile, the surface area $S$ generated by revolving the arc about the $x$-axis is:
$$
S = \int_a^b 2\pi y \sqrt{1 + (y')^2} \, dx = 2\pi \int_a^b y \, ds = 2\pi \bar{y} L = 2\pi \rho L
$$
$\blacksquare$

> [!example] Example: Surface Area of a Torus via Pappus's Theorem
> Find the surface area of the torus generated by revolving a circle of radius $a$ about the $y$-axis, where the center of the circle is at distance $b \geq a$ from the $y$-axis.

> [!figure] Figure Placeholder: A circle of radius $a$ centered at distance $b$ from the $y$-axis revolved to form a torus

**Solution:**
The centroid of the circular circumference of length $L = 2\pi a$ lies at its center, at distance $\rho = b$ from the $y$-axis. By Pappus's Theorem for Surface Areas:
$$
S = 2\pi \rho L = (2\pi b)(2\pi a) = 4\pi^2 a b
$$
$\blacksquare$

- Define the system torque, the moment about the origin $M_0$, the moment about an arbitrary point $x$, and the center of mass $\bar{x}$ for a 1D system of discrete point masses.

### Center of Mass of a Thin Rod
- Derive the integral formulas for the moment about the origin $M_0$, total mass $M$, and center of mass $\bar{x}$ of a thin rod with continuous density $\delta(x)$ over $[a, b]$.
- **Example: Center of Mass of a Rod of Constant Density**
  - Show that the center of mass of a straight, thin rod or strip of length $L$ and constant density $\delta$ lies halfway between its two ends.

### Masses Distributed Over a Plane Region
- Define the moments $M_y$ and $M_x$ about the coordinate axes and the center of mass $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$ for a 2D system of discrete point masses in a plane.

### Center of Mass of a Thin Flat Plate
- Derive the integral formulas for $M_y$, $M_x$, $M$, and $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$ of a thin flat plate using thin strips of mass $dm$ and strip centers $(\tilde{x}, \tilde{y})$, and define the centroid.
- **Example: Center of Mass of a Triangular Plate via Vertical and Horizontal Strips**
  - Find the center of mass of the right-angled triangular plate with vertices at $(0, 0)$, $(1, 0)$, and $(1, 2)$ if it has constant density $\delta = 3\text{ g/cm}^2$, using both vertical strips and horizontal strips.
- **Example: Center of Mass of a Parabolic Plate with Constant Density**
  - Find the center of mass of a thin plate of constant density $\delta$ covering the region bounded above by the parabola $y = 4 - x^2$ and below by the $x$-axis.
- **Example: Center of Mass of a Parabolic Plate with Variable Density**
  - Find the center of mass of the plate bounded above by $y = 4 - x^2$ and below by the $x$-axis if the density at any point $(x, y)$ is $\delta(x) = 2x^2$.

### Center of Mass of a Wire or Curved Arc
- Explain how to set up the differential mass element $dm$ and moment integrals for a thin wire bent along a smooth curve.
- **Example: Center of Mass of a Semicircular Wire**
  - Find the center of mass of a wire with constant density $\delta$ shaped like the upper semicircle of radius $a$ ($y = \sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$, $-a \leq x \leq a$) by integrating with respect to $\theta$ and by integrating with respect to $x$.
- Derive the antiderivative $\int \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} \, dx$ using trigonometric substitution.

## 5.10 Delesse's Rule and the Theorems of Pappus

### Delesse's Rule
- State and derive Delesse's Rule relating the 3D volumetric proportion of a constituent material in a cube of side length $L$ to the 2D cross-sectional areal proportion $u(x)$.

### The Theorems of Pappus
- **Theorem of Pappus for Volumes**
  - State and prove Pappus's Theorem for the volume of a solid of revolution in terms of the region's area and the distance traveled by its centroid.
- **Example: Volume of a Torus via Pappus's Theorem**
  - Find the volume of the torus generated by revolving a circular disk of radius $a$ about the $y$-axis, given that the center of the circle lies at distance $b \geq a$ from the $y$-axis.
- **Example: Finding the Centroid of a Semicircular Region Using Pappus's Theorem**
  - Locate the centroid of a semicircular plane region of radius $a$ using Pappus's Theorem for Volumes.
- **Theorem of Pappus for Surface Areas**
  - State and prove Pappus's Theorem for the surface area of a surface of revolution in terms of the curve's arc length and the distance traveled by its centroid.
- **Example: Surface Area of a Torus via Pappus's Theorem**
  - Find the surface area of the torus generated by revolving a circle of radius $a$ about the $y$-axis, where the center of the circle is at distance $b \geq a$ from the $y$-axis.
