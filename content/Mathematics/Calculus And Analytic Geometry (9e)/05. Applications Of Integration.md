# 5.1 AREAS BETWEEN CURVES

If we want to find the area of region that is bounded above by the function $f(x)$ & below by the function $g(x)$ & on the left and right by $x=a$ & $x=b$, then we can first start by approximating it using rectangles.

Consider $f(x)$ & $g(x)$, both cont. on $[a,b]$ & $f(x) \geq g(x)$. Let P be a partition of $[a,b]$, then we choose $c_k$ to be any number in $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$, then the height of rectangle is $f(c_k) - g(c_k)$, & width is $\Delta x_k$. This gives us the approx area of region as

<!-- Figure: A graph showing two curves f(x) and g(x) with shaded region between them from a to b, and a representative vertical rectangle -->
$$
A \approx \sum_{k=1}^n [f(c_k) - g(c_k)] \Delta x_k
$$
Since $f(x) - g(x)$ is also a continuous function, we have the limit of the sum as $|P| \rightarrow 0$ as
$$
A = \lim_{|P| \rightarrow 0} \sum_{k=1}^n [f(c_k) - g(c_k)] \Delta x_k = \int_a^b [f(x) - g(x)] dx
$$
**Definition:** If $f$ and $g$ are continuous functions with $f(x) \geq g(x)$ throughout $[a,b]$, then the area of region b/w $f(x)$, $g(x)$, $x=a$, $x=b$ is
$$
A = \int_a^b [f(x) - g(x)] dx
$$
**Example:** Find area b/w $y = \sec^2 x$ and $y = \sin x$ from $0$ to $\frac{\pi}{4}$

**sol:**
$$
A = \int_0^{\pi/4} [\sec^2 x - \sin x] dx = [\tan x + \cos x]_0^{\pi/4} = \left[1 + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right] - [0 + 1]
$$
---

### Curves That Intersect

When a region is determined by curves that intersect, the points of intersection give the limits of integration.

**Example:** Find area of region enclosed by the parabola $y = 2 - x^2$ and the line $y = -x$.

**sol:** The curves $y = 2 - x^2$ and $y = -x$ intersect at two points that we can get by solving,
$$
2 - x^2 = -x \implies  x^2 - x - 2 = 0
$$
<!-- Figure: Graph of downward parabola y=2-x^2 intersecting the line y=-x, with the shaded region between them -->
$$
\implies  (x-2)(x+1) = 0 \implies  x = 2, -1
$$
$$
\implies  A = \int_{-1}^2 [(2-x^2) - (-x)] dx
$$
$$
= \left[2x - \frac{x^3}{3} + \frac{x^2}{2}\right]_{-1}^2 = \left[4 - \frac{8}{3} + 2\right] - \left[-2 + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{2}\right]
$$
$$
= \frac{4}{3} + 2 + 2 - \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{2} = 5 - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{9}{2}
$$
### Bounding With Changing Formulas

If the formula for a bounding curve changes at one or more points, we partition the region into subregions that correspond to the formula changes and apply Eq(1) to each sub-region.

**Example:** Find the area of region in the first quadrant that is bounded above by $y = \sqrt{x}$ and below by the x-axis and the line $y = x-2$.

**sol:**
$$
A_1 = \int_0^2 \sqrt{x} dx = \left.\frac{x^{3/2}}{3/2}\right|_0^2 = \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3}
$$
<!-- Figure: Graph of y=sqrt(x) and y=x-2 in the first quadrant. The region under the curve is split into A1 (from x=0 to 2) and A2 (from x=2 to 4). -->

---
$$
A_2 = \int_2^4 [\sqrt{x} - (x-2)] dx = \left[\frac{x^{3/2}}{3/2} - \frac{x^2}{2} + 2x\right]_2^4
$$
$$
= \left(\frac{16}{3} - \frac{16}{2} + 8\right) - \left(\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3} - 2 + 4\right) = \frac{16}{3} - \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3} - 2
$$
$$
= \frac{10}{3} - \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3}
$$
$$
\implies  A = A_1 + A_2 = \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3} + \frac{10}{3} - \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3} = \frac{10}{3}
$$
### Integration with Respect to "y"

In a regions bounding curves are described by functions of y, the approximating rectangles are horizontal instead of vertical and the basic formula has y in place of x.
$$
A = \int_c^d [f(y) - g(y)] dy
$$
<!-- Figure: Graph showing a region bounded on left by x=g(y) and right by x=f(y) between y=c and y=d, with a horizontal representative rectangle -->

**Example:** Find the area asked in previous example, but this time use the functions of y.

**sol:**
<!-- Figure: Graph of the same region as the previous example, showing the curve x=y^2 and the line x=y+2, with y-limits from 0 to 2 -->
$$
\implies  A = \int_0^2 [(y+2) - y^2] dy
$$
$$
= \left[\frac{y^2}{2} + 2y - \frac{y^3}{3}\right]_0^2
$$
$$
= 2 + 4 - \frac{8}{3} = \frac{18-8}{3} = \frac{10}{3}
$$
Hence we get the same result.

### Combining Integrals with Formulas from Geometry

Sometimes the fastest way to find the area of a region may be to combine calculus and geometry

---

For example we can calculate the region in previous 2 examples using calculus & geometry.

<!-- Figure: Two graphs showing the area calculation. The first shows the region under y=sqrt(x) from x=0 to 4. The second shows the region subtracted by a triangle formed by y=x-2 under the x-axis. -->
$$
A = \int_0^4 \sqrt{x} dx - \frac{1}{2} \cdot (4-2) \cdot 2 = \left.\frac{x^{3/2}}{3/2}\right|_0^4 - \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 \cdot 2
$$
$$
= \frac{2}{3} \cdot 8 - 2 = \frac{16 - 6}{3} = \frac{10}{3}
$$
**Note on the Fastest Way:** The fastest way to calculate the area of a region depends entirely on the shape i.e the region and any one of the previous 3 methods can be used.

## 5.2 FINDING VOLUMES BY SLICING

From areas of regions with curved boundaries we can find the volume of cylinders with that area as the base by multiplying area $\times$ height.

With sums of volumes of such cylinders we can approximate the volume of any solid, and if we take the limit of the sum we can get exact volume using integral.

The setup is this, we assume the solid is arranged along x axis starting origin, then at point x let $A(x)$ be the area of cross-section, so to approximate the volume of the solid we will partition the $x$-axis into sub-intervals and use the area of cross-section at $x_k$ to approximate the area of the $k$-th section of the solid as shown in the figure.

<!-- Figure: A three-dimensional solid oriented along the x-axis, with a cross-sectional slice marked at position x having area A(x). -->

Total Volume (approx)
$$
= \sum_{k=1}^{n} A(x_k) \Delta x_k
$$
<!-- Figure: A 3D irregular solid shape representing the k-th section with width delta x_k and area A(x_k) on the right face -->

Now, if $A(x_k)$ is a continuous function then as we make $|P| \to 0$, we will get more and more accurate answers and the sum reaches the limit $\int_a^b A(x) dx$.

**Definition:** The volume of solid of known integrable cross-section area $A(x)$ from $x=a$ to $x=b$ is the integral of $A$ from $a$ to $b$.
$$
\implies V = \int_a^b A(x) dx
$$
**How to find Volume by Method of Slicing**
(i) Sketch the solid and typical cross-section.
(ii) Find a formula for $A(x)$
(iii) Find the limits of Integration
(iv) Integrate $A(x)$ to find the volume.

**Example:** A curved wedge is cut from a cylinder of radius 3 by two planes. One plane is $\perp^{\text{r}}$ to the axis of the cylinder. The second plane crosses the first plane at a $45^{\circ}$ angle at the center of the cylinder. Find the volume of the wedge.

**Sol:** Step 1: Sketching
<!-- Figure: Sketch of a curved wedge cut from a cylinder with radius 3, showing a triangular cross-section and semicircular base -->

$\implies A(x) = a \cdot b = x \cdot 2\sqrt{9-x^2} = 2x\sqrt{9-x^2}$
Step 2: $\uparrow$

Step 3: The limits of integration are from $x=0$ to $x=3$

Step 4: $\text{Volume} = \int_0^3 2x\sqrt{9-x^2} dx$,
Let $u = 9-x^2 \implies -du = 2x dx$
at $x=0, u=9$, &
at $x=3, u=0$

$\implies \text{Volume} = \int_9^0 \sqrt{u} (-du)$
$= -\left[ \frac{2}{3} u^{3/2} \right]_9^0 = -\frac{2}{3} \left[ 0 - 9^{3/2} \right] = -\frac{2}{3} \cdot -27$
$\implies \text{Volume} = 18$

### Cavalieri's Theorem

Bonaventura Cavalieri, a student of galileo, found that if two planes regions can be arranged to lie on the same interval of the x-axis in such a way that they have identical vertical cross sections at every point, then the regions have the same area.

<!-- Figure: Two 2D regions defined over the same interval [a, b] on the x-axis, showing a vertical cross-section of equal height for both regions at an arbitrary point x -->

The same principal was later extended to solids with same cross-sectional area at every point (not by cavalieri, but still given his name), such solids have the same volume.

**Ex:**
<!-- Figure: A cylinder and a sheared cylinder (oblique cylinder) shown having the same volume -->
same volume.

**Note:** If we look at the formula for volume by method of slicing i.e $V = \int_a^b A(x) dx$, it only depends on the cross-sectional area and hence both of the solids shown above have the same volume.

## 5.3 VOLUMES OF SOLIDS OF REVOLUTION

This is a direct application of the slicing method to find the volume of a solid, in this we generally see the solid to be obtained by revolving a region about an axis ($x$ or $y$).

**Defn:** The volume of solid generated by revolving about the x-axis the region between the x-axis and the graph of a continuous function $y=R(x)$, $a \le x \le b$, is
$$
V = \int_a^b \pi [R(x)]^2 dx
$$
<!-- Figure: A solid of revolution generated by revolving a function y=R(x) around the x-axis between a and b, showing a circular cross-section with radius R(x) -->

This is very easy to see the area of cross-section at $x$ is equal to area of circle with radius $R(x)$. $\implies A(x) = \pi R(x)^2$.

**Example:** The region b/w the curve $y=\sqrt{x}$, $0 \le x \le 4$, and the x-axis is revolved about the x-axis to generate a solid, find its volume.

**Sol:** $\text{Volume} = \int_0^4 \pi (\sqrt{x})^2 dx = \frac{\pi}{2} [x^2]_0^4$
$\implies \text{Volume} = 8\pi$

<!-- Figure: A solid generated by revolving y=sqrt(x) around the x-axis, showing a circular cross-section -->

**Example:** Find the volume of the solid generated by revolving the region bounded by $y=\sqrt{x}$ and the lines $y=1, x=4$ about $y=1$.

**Sol:** At point $x$ in $[1,4]$, the radius of cross section is $R(x) = \sqrt{x} - 1$

<!-- Figure: A graph showing the region bounded by y=sqrt(x), y=1, and x=4 revolved around the line y=1, generating a solid of revolution -->

$\implies A(x) = \pi (\sqrt{x} - 1)^2 = \pi (x + 1 - 2\sqrt{x})$
$\implies \text{Volume} = \int_1^4 \pi (x + 1 - 2\sqrt{x}) dx$
$= \pi \left( \frac{x^2}{2} + x - \frac{4}{3}x^{3/2} \right)_1^4 = \pi \left( \left[ 8 + 4 - \frac{32}{3} \right] - \left[ \frac{1}{2} + 1 - \frac{4}{3} \right] \right)$
$= \pi \left( \frac{4}{3} - \frac{3}{2} + \frac{4}{3} \right) = \pi \cdot \frac{7}{6}$
$\implies \text{Volume} = \frac{7\pi}{6}$

**Note:** The above example shows how can be calculate the volume of solid of revolution about any axis as long as we stick to the method of slicing.

**Example:** Find the volume of solid generated by revolving the region b/w the parabola $x = y^2 + 1$ and the line $x = 3$ about the line $x = 3$.

**Sol:** The radius at point $y$ is $3 - (y^2 + 1)$
$\implies A(y) = \pi (2 - y^2)^2$

<!-- Figure: A graph of the parabola x = y^2 + 1 and the line x = 3, with the region between them revolved around the line x = 3 to generate a solid -->

Now, the limits are $y^2 + 1 = 3 \implies y = \pm\sqrt{2}$

Hence, the volume is $V = \int_{-\sqrt{2}}^{\sqrt{2}} \pi (4 + y^4 - 4y^2) dy$
$\implies V = \pi \left( 4y + \frac{y^5}{5} - \frac{4}{3}y^3 \right)_{-\sqrt{2}}^{\sqrt{2}} = \pi \left[ \left( 4\sqrt{2} + \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{5} - \frac{8\sqrt{2}}{3} \right) - \left( -4\sqrt{2} - \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{5} + \frac{8\sqrt{2}}{3} \right) \right]$
$\implies V = 2\pi \left( 4\sqrt{2} + \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{5} - \frac{8\sqrt{2}}{3} \right) = \frac{2\sqrt{2}\pi}{15} (60 + 12 - 40)$
$V = \frac{2\sqrt{2}\pi}{15} \cdot 32 = \frac{64\sqrt{2}\pi}{15}$

### THE WASHER METHOD

If the region we revolve to generate a solid does not border or cross the axis of revolution, the solid has a hole in it, the volume of solid in that case can be calculated by the same method of slicing.
If the outer and inner radius at point x is $R(x)$ & $r(x)$, then the area function is
$$
A(x) = \pi (R(x)^2 - r(x)^2)
$$
$$
\implies \text{Volume} = \int_a^b \pi (R(x)^2 - r(x)^2) dx
$$
<!-- Figure: A solid of revolution with a hole (washer), showing outer radius R(x) and inner radius r(x) at an arbitrary point x -->

This is assuming $R(x)$ & $r(x)$ are cont. functions.

**Example:** The region bounded by the parabola $y = x^2$ and the line $y = 2x$ in the first quadrant is revolved about the y-axis to generate a solid. Find its volume.

**Sol:** We need to find the area of cross-section at point $y$ and the point of intersection.

At a point $y$ we have $r(y) = \frac{y}{2}$, $R(y) = \sqrt{y}$
<!-- Figure: A graph showing the curves x = y/2 and x = sqrt(y) bounding a region, with a horizontal cross section indicated at y. The y-axis goes up to 4, x-axis up to 4. -->
$\implies  A(y) = \pi \left( (\sqrt{y})^2 - \left(\frac{y}{2}\right)^2 \right) = \pi \left( y - \frac{y^2}{4} \right)$
$\implies  A(y) = \frac{\pi}{4} (4y - y^2)$
$\implies  \text{Volume} = \frac{\pi}{4} \int_0^4 (4y - y^2) dy = \frac{\pi}{4} \left[ 2y^2 - \frac{y^3}{3} \right]_0^4$
$= \frac{\pi}{4} \left( 32 - \frac{64}{3} \right) = \frac{8\pi}{3}$

## 5.4 CYLINDER SHELLS

The volume of the solid generated by revolving the region between the x-axis and the graph of a continuous function $y=f(x) \ge 0$, $0 \le a \le x \le b$, about the y-axis is
$$
V = \int_a^b 2\pi x f(x) dx
$$
<!-- Figure: A graph showing a region under a curve from a to b partitioned into vertical strips, illustrating the cylindrical shell method. -->

**Proof:** We partition the interval $(a,b)$, let $P$ be the partition, then the $k$-th region
rotates to generate a solid whose volume we can approximate using a cylinder of height $f(x_k)$ & thickness $\Delta x_k$.
Outer Radius(R) = $x_k$,
Inner Radius(r) = $x_k - \Delta x_k$
<!-- Figure: A diagram showing a cylindrical shell with inner radius, outer radius, and height, representing the volume element. -->
$\implies  \text{Volume} = V_k \approx \pi \times \text{height} \times (R^2 - r^2)$
$= \pi f(x_k) \left( x_k^2 - x_k^2 - \Delta x_k^2 + 2x_k\Delta x_k \right)$
$= \pi f(x_k) (2x_k\Delta x_k) - \pi f(x_k) \Delta x_k^2$

$\implies  \text{Volume} = \sum_{k=1}^n V_k \approx \sum_{k=1}^n 2\pi f(x_k) x_k \Delta x_k - \sum_{k=1}^n \pi f(x_k) \Delta x_k^2$

$\implies  \text{As } |P| \to 0 \implies  n \to \infty \text{ \& } \Delta x_k \to 0 \text{ \& } \Delta x_k^2 \to 0$
$\implies  \text{Volume} = \int_a^b 2\pi x f(x) dx - 0 = \int_a^b 2\pi x f(x) dx$
$\implies  \text{Hence, proved.}$

## 5.5 LENGTHS OF PLANE CURVES

**Defn:** A function with a continuous first derivative is said to be "smooth" and its graph is called a "smooth curve".
**Note:** Note if a function is differentiable on $(a,b)$, then that does not mean that it is also smooth. But even if $f'(x)$ is discontinuous, it will not
be discontinuity of type-1 i.e. jump/removable. It will be oscillatory all thanks to the darboux theorem or IVT of derivative.

### Length of Smooth Curve

**Defn:** If $f$ is smooth on $[a,b]$, the length of the curve $y=f(x)$ from $a$ to $b$ is
$$
L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + f'(x)^2} dx
$$
**Proof:** To approximate the length of the curve we will partition the interval $[a,b]$, let $P$ be the partition.
<!-- Figure: A graph of a curve from x=a to x=b, partitioned into small segments to approximate its length using straight line segments. -->
Now, for k-th sub-interval we will approximate the length of curve by line segment joining $(x_{k-1}, f(x_{k-1}))$, $(x_k, f(x_k))$.
$\implies  \Delta s_k = \sqrt{\Delta x_k^2 + \Delta y_k^2} = \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\Delta y_k}{\Delta x_k}\right)^2} \Delta x_k$
Now, since $f$ is smooth i.e. $f$ is differentiable on $[a,b]$, by using MVT there exists $c_k$ in $(x_{k-1}, x_k]$ such that $f'(c_k) = \frac{\Delta y_k}{\Delta x_k}$.
$\implies  \Delta s_k = \sqrt{1 + f'(c_k)^2} \Delta x_k$
$\implies  \text{Length of curve (approx) } L \approx \sum_{k=1}^n \sqrt{1 + f'(c_k)^2} \Delta x_k$.
$\implies $ As $|P| \to 0$, the limit of RHS will exist if $\sqrt{1 + f'(c_k)^2}$ is an integrable function, which it is in this case as $f'(x)$ is continuous, this means $\sqrt{1+f'(x)^2}$ is also continuous and hence integrable.
$$
\implies  L = \lim_{|P| \to 0} \sum_{k=1}^n \sqrt{1 + f'(c_k)^2} \Delta x_k = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + f'(x)^2} \, dx
$$
**Example:** Find the length of the curve $y = \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3} x^{3/2} - 1$, $0 \le x \le 1$.

**sol:** $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3} \cdot \frac{3}{2} \cdot x^{1/2} - 0 = 2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{x}$
$$
\implies  L = \int_0^1 \sqrt{1 + (2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{x})^2} \, dx = \int_0^1 \sqrt{1 + 8x} \, dx, \quad \begin{array}{l} u = 1+8x \implies  du = 8dx \\ \text{at } x=0, u=1 \, \& \\ \text{at } x=1, u=9 \end{array}
$$
$$
= \int_1^9 \sqrt{u} \, \frac{du}{8} = \frac{1}{8} \left[ \frac{2}{3} u^{3/2} \right]_1^9
$$
$$
= \frac{1}{12} [27 - 1] = \frac{13}{6}
$$
$$
\implies  L = \frac{13}{6}
$$
### Dealing with discontinuity in $\frac{dy}{dx}$

$\rightarrow$ Many times $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is not defined at certain points in this the interval and we cannot use the above formula, in those cases, we can look towards the function $x = f(y)$ & $\frac{dx}{dy}$ as it may be defined and continuous. In that case we get the length as
$$
L = \int_c^d \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dx}{dy}\right)^2} \, dy
$$
**Example:** Find the length of curve $y = \left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2/3}$ from $x=0$ to $x=2$.

**sol:** $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{-1/3} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{2}{x}\right)^{1/3}$

<!-- Figure: Plot of curve y = (x/2)^(2/3) from x=0 to x=2 -->

$\implies  \frac{dy}{dx}$ not defined at $x=0$.

$\implies  y = \left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2/3} \implies  x = 2y^{3/2} \implies  \frac{dx}{dy} = 3y^{1/2}$

$\implies  dx/dy$ is continuous in the interval $[0,1]$
$$
\implies  L = \int_0^1 \sqrt{1 + (3\sqrt{y})^2} \, dy = \int_0^1 \sqrt{1 + 9y} \, dy
$$
$$
= \frac{1}{9} \left[ \frac{2}{3} (1 + 9y)^{3/2} \right]_0^1 = \frac{1}{9} \cdot \frac{2}{3} \left[ (10^{3/2} - 1) \right]
$$
$$
= \frac{2}{27} (10\sqrt{10} - 1) \approx 2.27
$$
### THE ARC LENGTH DIFFERENTIAL AND DIFFERENTIAL FORMULA FOR ARC LENGTH

$\rightarrow$ The arc length differential is defined as $ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2}$, where $dx$ & $dy$ are the differentials.

<!-- Figure: Curve showing ds, dx, and dy forming a right triangle -->

$\rightarrow$ The formula for arc length of function $y = f(x)$ in arc length differential form is
$$
L = \int_a^b ds
$$
$\rightarrow$ It can be easily derived as follows, when $dx \neq 0$ we know $\frac{dy}{dx} = f'(x)$
$$
\implies  L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + f'(x)^2} \, dx = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + \frac{dy^2}{dx^2}} \, dx = \int_a^b \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2}
$$
$$
\implies  L = \int_a^b ds
$$
**Note:** This formula gives us a shorthand way to write the original formula, but essentially has the same meaning.

## 5.6 AREAS OF SURFACES OF REVOLUTION

Before discussing the actual solution we need to understand one thing which is what is wrong with the solution that I thought of.

### Problem With Cylinder Approximation
The problem with using cylinders to approximate the surface area is that it doesn't converge to the actual surface area.
We think the cylinder of radius $f(x_{k-1})$ and height $\Delta x_k$ is a good approximation of the surface created by revolving k-th region, but that is not the case.
<!-- Figure: A curve revolved around the x-axis, with a cylinder approximating a segment between x_{k-1} and x_k. -->
If this were true then we would have the surface area of surface of revolution as
$$
S = \lim_{|P| \to 0} \sum_{k=1}^n 2\pi f(c_k) \Delta x_k = \int_a^b 2\pi f(x) dx.
$$
A Failing Example:
Consider the following curve, it surface of revolution is a cone, as shown in the figure.
<!-- Figure: A line segment in the first quadrant from (0, r) to (h, 0), generating a cone when revolved. L=\sqrt{r^2+h^2} -->
The equation of line is $f(x) = -\frac{r}{h}(x-h)$
$\implies  S = \int_0^h 2\pi \left(-\frac{r}{h}(x-h)\right) dx = -\frac{2\pi r}{h} \left[\frac{x^2}{2} - hx\right]_0^h = -\frac{2\pi r}{h} \left(\frac{h^2}{2} - h^2\right)$
> ⚠️ **Possible Error:** In the last line, the integral evaluates to $-\frac{2\pi r}{h} (\frac{h^2}{2} - h^2)$, but the written expression cuts off before finishing the evaluation.

$\implies  \boxed{S = \pi r h}$

But we know that the surface area of this cone is $\pi r L$ and here the answer is not correct.

The reason for this failure is simply that cylinder uses horizontal lines, but these horizontal lines cannot approximate curved lines, no matter how small you make them.

### Then Why Cylinder And Rectangle Worked So Far?
Areas and volumes are about filling a space whose boundaries we approximate using cylinder & Rectangle. The bulk of the region always matched the approximations and errors were only near the edges which shrinked dramatically with finer partitions.
But in this case it is not about the space inside, it is about the surface i.e the boundary itself.
<!-- Figure: Two drawings comparing space filling approximations versus surface boundary approximations using rectangles/cylinders. -->

### First Order Vs Second Order Error
When we zoom in on a curve enough, every curve looks like a straight line, so we can approximate the error using the $\Delta$ shown in the figure. For area calculation the error is
<!-- Figure: A zoomed-in view of a curve looking like a straight line, forming a triangle with horizontal \Delta x_k and vertical \Delta y. -->
$E \cong \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{2} \Delta x \cdot \Delta y$ , but since $\Delta y = f'(x_k) \Delta x$
$\implies  E \cong \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{2} f'(x_k) \Delta x^2$

Similarly volume of the extra region is roughly the circumference times the area i.e
$E_V \cong \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{2} \Delta x \cdot \Delta y \cdot 2\pi f(x_k) = \sum_{k=1}^n \pi f(x_k) f'(x_k) \Delta x^2$

In both the cases the error is Second Order i.e proportional to $\Delta x^2$, this means that the area under curve & volume become infinitely more accurate if we make $\Delta x$ infinitely small.

But in case of surface area, the error is the difference of actual value and the calculated value, the example that we gave has k-th region as a frustum whose surface area is $\pi (r_1+r_2)L = \pi (f(x_k) + f(x_{k-1})) L$

What cylinder gives us is $2\pi f(x_k) \Delta x_k$
$\implies  E = \sum_{k=1}^n \pi \left[ (f(x_k) + f(x_{k-1})) \sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2} - 2 f(x_k) \Delta x_k \right]$
> ⚠️ **Possible Error:** In the last line, the term is written as `2 f(x_k) \Delta x_k` inside the brackets but previously it was `2\pi f(x_k) \Delta x_k`.

$\implies $ Hence, $E = \sum \pi \left[ (r_1+r_2)\sqrt{1+(\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x})^2} - 2 f(x_k) \right] \Delta x_k$

$\implies $ The error is proportional to $\Delta x$ and hence is of first order, this is the reason why cylinder Approach doesn't work.

### The Correct Approach
In the previous page we saw that when the partition is very fine, we have every section as almost like frustum, so we will use frustums to approximate the surface area.
The k-th sub-interval the approximating frustum has the area,
<!-- Figure: A frustum approximating the surface over the interval [x_{k-1}, x_k] with slant length L. -->
$S_k = \pi (r_1+r_2) \sqrt{\Delta x_k^2 + \Delta y_k^2}$

$\implies  S_k = \pi (f(x_{k-1}) + f(x_k)) \sqrt{1 + (\frac{\Delta y_k}{\Delta x_k})^2} \Delta x_k$

$\implies  S = \sum_{k=1}^n \pi (f(x_{k-1}) + f(x_k)) \sqrt{1 + (\frac{\Delta y_k}{\Delta x_k})^2} \Delta x_k$

$\implies $ Bliss's Theorem from advanced calculus proves that the above sum converges as $|P| \to 0$ to
$$
S = \int_a^b 2\pi f(x) \sqrt{1+f'(x)^2} dx
$$
**Example:** Find the area of the surface generated by revolving the curve $y=2\sqrt{x}$ , $1 \le x \le 2$ about the x axis.

## 5.7 MOMENTS AND CENTER OF MASS

### Moment of Mass or First Moment or Static Moment:
$\rightarrow$ The moment of a system of point masses about the origin is given by
$$
M_0 = \sum m_k x_k \quad , \quad m_k = \text{mass of kth point, } x_k = \text{signed distance}
$$
$\rightarrow$ It tells the tendency of the system to rotate about the origin under gravity.
$\rightarrow$ The torque due to weight of the kth mass is $ m_k x_k g $, hence total torque is
$$
\text{System Torque} = \sum m_k x_k g = M_0 g
$$
<!-- Figure: A fulcrum balancing a rod with masses m1, m2, m3, m4, m5 at various distances from the origin 0 -->
$\rightarrow$ About any other point other than the origin on x-axis, the moment will be
$$
M_x = \sum m_k (x_k - x) \text{ \& System Torque } = M_x g
$$
> ⚠️ **Possible Error:** In the equation $M_x = m_k (x_k - x)$, the summation symbol $\sum$ is missing.

$\rightarrow$ COM along x-axis: it is a point such that if we keep the fulcrum there the system torque is 0 and the system remains balanced.

$\rightarrow$ COM is where $ M_x = 0 \implies \sum m_k (x_k - \bar{x}) = 0 $
$$
\implies \bar{x} = \frac{\sum m_k x_k}{\sum m_k} = \frac{\text{Moment about origin}}{\text{Total mass}} = \frac{M_0}{M}
$$
### COM of Thin Rod:
$\rightarrow$ Thin here simply let's us assume the rod to be 1D, making the calculations easier.
$\rightarrow$ Assume the rod to be placed along the x axis in the interval $[a,b]$. To find it's center of mass we partition $[a,b]$ such that each interval is considered as point mass at a distance $x_k$ from origin and having mass $\Delta m_k$ (mass of k-th segment).
<!-- Figure: A 1D thin rod along the x-axis from x=a to x=b, with a segment Delta x_k at distance x_k -->
$$
\implies \bar{x} \approx \frac{\sum x_k \Delta m_k}{\sum \Delta m_k}
$$
$\rightarrow$ If the density at point $x_k$ is $\rho(x_k)$ and is continuous function of $x$, then
$$
\bar{x} \approx \frac{\sum x_k \rho(x_k) \Delta x_k}{\sum \rho(x_k) \Delta x_k}
$$
$\rightarrow$ As we make the partition finer and finer the approximation gets more and more accurate, this means that at $\lim_{|P|\to 0}$ the sums convert to integral and we get
$$
\bar{x} = \frac{\int_{a}^{b} x \rho(x) dx}{\int_{a}^{b} \rho(x) dx}
$$
$$
\rightarrow \text{Moment about origin} = M_0 = \int_{a}^{b} x \rho(x) dx
$$
$$
\text{Total Mass} = M = \int_{a}^{b} \rho(x) dx
$$
$$
\text{COM} = \frac{M_0}{M}
$$
**Example:** Show that the center of mass of a straight, thin strip or rod of constant density lies halfway between it's two ends.

**Sol:** Let the rod be of length L placed in the interval $[0,L]$, and the density at every point $\rho(x) = \rho$ (constant).
$$
\implies M_0 = \int_{0}^{L} x \rho dx = \rho \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} \right]_0^L = \frac{\rho L^2}{2}
$$
$$
M = \int_{0}^{L} \rho dx = \rho L
$$
$$
\implies \text{COM} = \frac{\rho L^2/2}{\rho L} = \frac{L}{2} \quad , \text{Hence proved.}
$$
### Masses Distributed Over Plane Region
$\rightarrow$ Unlike 1D, here we have point masses spread over a plane region and the moment will be calculated about axes and not point.
$\rightarrow$ The moment about any axes is the tendency of the system to rotate about that axis assuming that gravity is acting into the plane (z axis).
<!-- Figure: Point masses scattered on a 2D Cartesian plane, with one point marked as m_k (x_k, y_k) -->
$\rightarrow$ Moment about y-axis $= M_y = \sum m_k x_k$
$\rightarrow$ Moment about x-axis $= M_x = \sum m_k y_k$
$$
\implies \text{x-coordinate of COM} = \frac{M_y}{M} = \frac{\sum m_k x_k}{\sum m_k} = \bar{x}
$$
$$
\text{y-coordinate of COM} = \frac{M_x}{M} = \frac{\sum m_k y_k}{\sum m_k} = \bar{y}
$$
The COM $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$ is a point that balances the system from toppling out of the plane.

### Thin Flat Plate
Just like thin flat rod, "thin" here simply makes sure that the dimension of plate is 2D and not 3D.

$\rightarrow$ We place the plate in a coordinate plane and it occupies some region, as shown in the figure.
<!-- Figure: A 2D thin plate region in the first quadrant of a Cartesian plane, with a vertical strip of width between x_1 and x_2 marked -->
$\rightarrow$ Now, to find its COM, we start by approximating it using sums. Let's partition the interval $[x_1, x_2]$ and let the k-th interval carve out a thin strip $\|$ to y axis.
$\rightarrow$ We find the COM of this strip, if $\rho(x,y)$ is the density function of this plate and if it is continuous, then we have
$$
\text{y-coord COM of strip} = \frac{\int_{y_1}^{y_2} y \rho(x_k, y) dy}{\int_{y_1}^{y_2} \rho(x_k, y) dy} = \tilde{y}_k \quad, \text{x-coordinate} = x_k = \tilde{x}_k
$$
COM of strip is at $(\tilde{x}_k, \tilde{y}_k)$, let mass of the strip be $\Delta m_k$.
$\rightarrow$ Now, we replace the strip with a point element at $(\tilde{x}_k, \tilde{y}_k)$ and mass $\Delta m_k$. So, we have k elements with masses $\Delta m_k$ and position $(\tilde{x}_k, \tilde{y}_k)$, so the COM is
$$
\bar{x} \approx \frac{\sum \Delta m_k \tilde{x}_k}{\sum \Delta m_k} \quad , \quad \bar{y} \approx \frac{\sum \Delta m_k \tilde{y}_k}{\sum \Delta m_k}
$$
$\rightarrow$ Again if we make the partition finer and finer this means that the sum has limit equal to the integrals.
$$
\bar{x} = \frac{\int \tilde{x} dm}{\int dm} \quad , \quad \bar{y} = \frac{\int \tilde{y} dm}{\int dm}
$$
$$
\text{Moment about y-axis} = M_y = \int \tilde{x} dm \quad , \quad M_x = \int \tilde{y} dm \quad , \quad \text{Mass} = M = \int dm
$$
$$
\implies \text{COM} = (\bar{x}, \bar{y}) = \left(\frac{M_y}{M}, \frac{M_x}{M}\right)
$$
**Note:** Vertical & Horizontal strips both lead to the same final answer.

> ⚠️ **Possible Error:** There's an extra 'a' in the denominator of the handwritten correction $\frac{-dx}{y \cdot a}$, which is crossed out.

$\implies  M = \int_{-a}^a \rho ds = \int_{-a}^a \rho a d\theta = \int_{-a}^a \rho a \left(-\frac{dx}{y}\right) = -\rho a \int_{-a}^a \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2-x^2}} dx$
> ⚠️ **Possible Error:** The limits of integration for dx should be from a to -a given the substitution, but it's written as -a to a with a negative sign inside, which is equivalent.

$= -\rho a \left[ \sin^{-1} \left(\frac{x}{a}\right) \right]_{-a}^a = -\rho a \left[ \frac{\pi}{2} - \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right) \right] = -\rho a \pi$
> ⚠️ **Possible Error:** Mass should be positive, the bounds might have been handled incorrectly during substitution. The final result is written as $-\rho a \pi$.

$M_x = \int_{-a}^a \tilde{y} dm = \int_{-a}^a y \frac{-\rho a dx}{y} = -\rho a [x]_{-a}^a = -2\rho a^2$

$\implies  \bar{y} = \frac{M_x}{M} = \frac{2}{\pi} a$

**Note:** Here we used a formula which is $\int \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2-x^2}} dx = \sin^{-1}(\frac{x}{a}) + c$, this we can get by using the substitution $x = a\sin\alpha \implies  \alpha = \sin^{-1}(\frac{x}{a})$

$\implies  \int \frac{a\cos\alpha d\alpha}{\sqrt{a^2 - a^2\sin^2\alpha}} = \int \frac{a\cos\alpha d\alpha}{a\cos\alpha} = \alpha = \sin^{-1}(\frac{x}{a}) + c$

## 5.10 Delesse's Rule & Theorems of Pappus

### Delesse's Rule
Consider a solid cube made up of different kind of materials and we want to find the proportion of a particular material $A$ in the solid, the way we do this is we imagine the coordinate axes as shown in the figure, then we partition the interval so that at point $x$ we have a cube (thin) of volume $\Delta x_k L^2$.

<!-- Figure: A cube with side length L aligned with the coordinate axes. A thin slice of thickness dx_k is shown at position x_k. The area of material A in this slice is denoted A. -->

$\rightarrow$ Now, we need to find the amount of $A$ in this thin slice, we can put it under microscope to find the proportion $u(x)$ on the surface and since the slice is thin we can assume it to be the same throughout the slice.
$\implies $ amount of $A$ in k-th slice $= u(x_k) \cdot L^2 \cdot \Delta x_k$

total amount of A in cube $\approx \sum u(x_k) L^2 \Delta x_k$

$\rightarrow$ As the partition gets finer reaches the limit the integral and we get
total amount of $A = \int_0^L u(x) L^2 dx$

$\implies $ proportion of $A = \frac{\text{amount}}{\text{Volume}} = \frac{L^2 \int_0^L u(x) dx}{L^3} = \frac{1}{L} \int_0^L u(x) dx$

$\implies $ proportion of $A =$ average value of $u(x)$ over $[0, L]$.
$\implies $ This is Delesse's Rule.

### THE THEOREMS OF PAPPUS

**Theorem For Volumes:** If a plane region is revolved once about a line in the plane that does not cut through the region's interior, then the volume of the solid it generates is equal to the region's area times the distance travelled by the region's centroid during the revolution. If $\rho$ is distance from axis of revolution to centroid, then
$$
V = 2\pi \rho A
$$
**Proof:** We forgot to mention in the previous section that centroid is COM of a system with constant density, hence for a flat region the y coordinate of centroid will be
$$
\bar{y} = \frac{M_x}{M} = \frac{\int \tilde{y} dm}{\int dm} = \frac{\int y \rho \cdot dA}{\int \rho dA} = \frac{\int y dA}{\int dA}
$$
<!-- Figure: A generic region in the first quadrant, bounded by some curve. A horizontal strip of length L(y) and width dy is shown at height y. -->

$\implies  \bar{y} = \frac{\int_c^d y \cdot L(y) dy}{A} \implies  \int_c^d y L(y) dy = \bar{y} A$

$\rightarrow$ Now, we will find the volume of revolution using cylindrical shell method,
$\implies  V = \int_c^d 2\pi (\text{shell radius}) (\text{shell height}) dy = 2\pi \int_c^d y L(y) dy$

$\implies  V = 2\pi \bar{y} A$

$\implies $ Hence, proved.

**Example:** Find the volume of solid generated by rotating a circle about y-axis as shown in the figure. ($b \geq a$)

<!-- Figure: A circle of radius a in the first quadrant, with its center at distance b from the y-axis. -->

**sol:** distance of centroid from y-axis $= b$
$A = \pi a^2 \implies  V = 2\pi \rho A = 2\pi b \pi a^2 = 2\pi^2 a^2 b$

**Example:** Locate the centroid of semicircular region.

**sol:** Imagine we revolve the region about x axis, then the solid of revolution is sphere with radius $a$.

<!-- Figure: A semicircular region of radius a resting on the x-axis, centered at the origin. -->

$\implies  V = 2\pi \bar{y} A = \frac{4}{3} \pi a^3$

$\implies  2\pi \bar{y} \frac{\pi a^2}{2} = \frac{4}{3} \pi a^3 \implies  \bar{y} = \frac{4a}{3\pi}$
> ⚠️ **Possible Error:** The area of the semicircle is $\frac{1}{2}\pi a^2$. The equation is written as $2\pi \bar{y} \frac{\pi a^2}{2} = \frac{4}{3} \pi a^3$, solving this gives $\pi^2 a^2 \bar{y} = \frac{4}{3} \pi a^3 \implies  \bar{y} = \frac{4a}{3\pi}$. This is mathematically correct.

**Theorem For Surface Area:** If an arc of a smooth curve is revolved once about a line in the plane that does not intersect the arc's interior, then the area of the surface of revolution is the length of the arc times the distance travelled by the centroid of the arc. If $\rho$ is the distance of the centroid of curve from axis & L is its length, then
$$
A = 2\pi \rho L
$$
**Proof:** Consider the arc in the first quadrant and rotated about the x-axis.

<!-- Figure: An arc in the first quadrant from x=a to x=b. A small segment ds is shown at height y. -->

$\rightarrow$ y-coordinate of centroid $\bar{y} = \frac{\int_a^b \tilde{y} dm}{\int_a^b dm}$
$= \frac{\int_a^b y \rho ds}{\int_a^b \rho ds} = \frac{\int_a^b y ds}{L} \implies  \int_a^b y ds = \bar{y} L$

$\implies $ The area of surface of revolution $= \int_a^b 2\pi y \sqrt{1+y'^2} dx = \int_a^b 2\pi y ds$
$\implies  A = 2\pi \bar{y} L$

**Example:** Find the surface area of torus (donut) obtained by revolving the circle about y axis.

<!-- Figure: A circle of radius a with center at distance b from the y-axis, rotated about the y-axis to form a torus. A 3D sketch of the torus is also shown. -->

**sol:** $A = 2\pi \cdot b \cdot 2\pi a = 4\pi^2 a b$
