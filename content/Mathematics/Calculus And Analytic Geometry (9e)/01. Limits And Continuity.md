# 1.1 Average Rate of Change & Secant Lines

> [!info] Definition
> The average rate of change of $y = f(x)$ with respect to $x$ over the interval $[x_1, x_2]$ is
> $$
> \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{f(x_2) - f(x_1)}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{f(x_1 + h) - f(x_1)}{x_1 + h - x_1}
> $$
> $$
> \implies \quad \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{f(x_2) - f(x_1)}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{f(x_1 + h) - f(x_1)}{h}
> $$

> [!figure] Figure 1
> 
> ![](Pasted%20image%2020260920210608.png)
> 
> Secant line through P and Q. Graph of $y = f(x)$ with points $P$ (at $x_1$) and $Q$ (at $x_2$) marked on the curve, dotted vertical lines down to $x_1$ and $x_2$ on the $x$-axis, and the secant line joining $P$ and $Q$.

If we look at the graph of $y = f(x)$ and the line joining the points $P(x_1, f(x_1))$ and $Q(x_2, f(x_2))$, we see that the average value of the function between $x_1$ and $x_2$ is the **slope of the secant** joining the points $P$ and $Q$.

A **secant** is a line that cuts a curve at two different points.

### Instantaneous rate of change

The **instantaneous rate of change** of a function is the rate of change at a particular point. In the expression

$$\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{f(x_1 + h) - f(x_1)}{h}$$

we cannot put $h = 0$ to get the rate of change at the point $x_1$. But what we _can_ do is put $h$ smaller and smaller, as close to $0$ as we want, and if the ratio gets closer and closer to a value, then we can assume that the rate of change at $x_1$ is that value.

## Informal Definition of a Limit

> [!info] Definition — Limit (informal)
> Let $f(x)$ be defined on an open interval about $x_0$, except possibly at $x_0$ itself. If $f(x)$ gets arbitrarily close to $L$ for all $x$ sufficiently close to $x_0$, we say that $f$ approaches the limit $L$ as $x$ approaches $x_0$, and we write
> $$
> \lim_{x \to x_0} f(x) = L
> $$

> [!note]
> **(i)** If $f$ is the identity function, then the limit of $f$ at $x_0$ is $x_0$:
> $$
> \implies \quad \lim_{x \to x_0} f(x) = \lim_{x \to x_0} x = x_0
> $$
> 
> **(ii)** %% p. 3 %% If $f$ is a constant function, then the limit of $f$ at any point $x_0$ is $k$:
> $$
> \implies \quad \lim_{x \to x_0} f(x) = \lim_{x \to x_0} k = k
> $$
> 
> **(iii)** If $f$ is an algebraic function which is a combination of polynomial and trigonometric (etc.) functions, and $f$ is defined at $x_0$, then sometimes the limit of $f$ at $x_0$ is simply $f(x_0)$.
> 
> E.g. $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{3x + 4}{5x + 9} = \frac{4}{9}$

### A few examples where a limit does not exist

**(i)** Unit step function, at $x = 0$:
$$
U(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x < 0 \  \\  1, & x \ge 0 \end{cases}
$$
_Sol._ In this case, as $x$ approaches $0$ from the left, $U$ approaches $0$; but if $x$ approaches $0$ from the right, $U$ approaches $1$.

This function is called the **unit step function**, and its graph is given on the right.

**(ii)** At $x = 0$:
$$
g(x) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{x}, & x \neq 0 \\ 0, & x = 0 \end{cases}
$$
_Sol._ This function takes arbitrarily large values near $x = 0$ and does not approach any fixed value, hence we say that the function has no limit at $x = 0$.

**(iii)** At $x = 0$:

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x = 0 \\ \sin\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right), & x \neq 0 \end{cases}$$

_Sol._ This function wiggles infinitely many times near the point $x = 0$ and takes all values from $-1$ to $1$ in the process. Hence it approaches no single value and has no limit at $x = 0$.

# 1.2 Rules for Finding the Limits

### Limits of powers and algebraic combinations

> [!abstract] Theorem 1
> Properties of limits The following rules hold if $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$ and $\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = M$ ($L$ and $M$ are real numbers).
> 
> - **(i) Sum Rule:** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} [f(x) + g(x)] = L + M$
> - **(ii) Difference Rule:** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} [f(x) - g(x)] = L - M$
> - **(iii) Product Rule:** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} f(x) \cdot g(x) = L \cdot M$
> - **(iv) Constant Multiple Rule:** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} k \cdot f(x) = kL$, where $k$ can be any real number.
> - **(v) Quotient Rule:** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{L}{M}$, if $M \neq 0$
> - **(vi) Power Rule:** If $m$ and $n$ are integers, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} (f(x))^{m/n} = L^{m/n}$, provided $L^{m/n} \in \mathbb{R}$.

> [!note]
> The proofs to the above rules will be discussed in the next section.

> [!note]
> We know that $\lim_{x \to c} x = c$, and if we combine this with property **(vi)**, we get that $\lim_{x \to c} x^{m/n} = c^{m/n}$. Now, combining this with the other properties, we get the following theorems.

> [!abstract] Theorem 2
> Limits of polynomials Limits of polynomials can be found by substitution, i.e. if $P(x) = \displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n} c_k x^k$, then
> $$
> \lim_{x \to c} P(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} c_k (c)^k = P(c)
> $$

> [!abstract] Theorem 3
> Limits of rational functions Limits of rational functions can be found by substitution if the limit of the denominator is not $0$. If $P(x)$ and $Q(x)$ are polynomials and $Q(c) \neq 0$, then
> $$
> \implies \quad \lim_{x \to c} \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} = \frac{P(c)}{Q(c)}
> $$

### Eliminating zero denominators algebraically

If in a rational function the denominator is $0$ at the point $c$, then the limit can still exist if we are able to factor out and remove the factor that is leading to $0$, and then substitution will give the limit.

Notice that factoring out and cancelling does not change the function, as it is not defined at $x = c$, and at all other points we can always cancel out the factor.

> [!example] Example — Evaluate the following limits 
> **(i)** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^2 + x - 2}{x^2 - x}$ $\qquad$
> **(ii)** $\displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{2 + h} - \sqrt{2}}{h}$

_Sol._

**(i)** 
$$
\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{(x + 2)(x - 1)}{x(x - 1)} = \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x + 2}{x} = 3
$$
**(ii)** 
$$
\begin{align} \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{2 + h} - \sqrt{2}}{h} & = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2 + h - 2}{h\left(\sqrt{2 + h} + \sqrt{2}\right)} \\ & = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 + h} + \sqrt{2}}  \\  & = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\end{align}
$$

### Theorem 4 — The Sandwich Theorem

> [!abstract] Theorem 4
> Suppose that $g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x)$ for all $x$ in some open interval containing $c$, except possibly at $x = c$ itself. Suppose also that
> 
> $$\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = \lim_{x \to c} h(x) = L$$
> 
> Then $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$.

*Proof:* [(v) Proof of Sandwich Theorem](#(v)%20Proof%20of%20Sandwich%20Theorem)

> [!example] 
> Show that if $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} |f(x)| = 0$, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = 0$.

_Sol._ 
$$
-|f(x)| \le f(x) \le |f(x)|, \quad \forall x \in \mathbb{R}
$$
Hence by the Sandwich Theorem we have that
$$
\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = 0
$$
This is because $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} |f(x)| = \lim_{x \to c} -|f(x)| = 0$.

## 1.2 Exercises

Limits of the form $\displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}$ occur very frequently in calculus, as they are related to secant lines, tangents and instantaneous rates.

Evaluate this limit for the following functions.

**Problem 37 — $f(x) = x^2$**

_Sol._ 
$$
\begin{align}
\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} = \frac{x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2}{h} \\
\implies \quad \lim_{h \to 0} (2x + h) = 2x
\end{align}
$$

**Problem 39 — $f(x) = ax + b$**

_Sol._
$$
\begin{align}
\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} & = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{a(x + h) + b - (ax + b)}{h} \\
\implies \quad \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{ah}{h} & = \lim_{h \to 0} a = a
\end{align}
$$

**Problem 40 — $f(x) = 1/x$**

_Sol._
$$
\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\dfrac{1}{x + h} - \dfrac{1}{x}}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x - x - h}{(x + h),x,h} = \frac{-1}{x^2}
$$

**Problem 42 — $f(x) = \sqrt{ax + b}$**

_Sol._ 
$$
\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} = \frac{\sqrt{a(x + h) + b} - \sqrt{ax + b}}{h}
$$

$$= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{ah}{h\left(\sqrt{a(x + h) + b} + \sqrt{ax + b}\right)} = \frac{a}{2\sqrt{ax + b}}$$

**Problem 43 —** If $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{f(x) - 5}{x - 2} = 3$, then find $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} f(x)$.

_Sol._
$$
\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{f(x) - 5}{x - 2} \cdot \lim_{x \to 2} (x - 2) = 3 \cdot 0 = 0
$$
$$
\implies \quad \lim_{x \to 2} \left( \frac{f(x) - 5}{x - 2} \cdot (x - 2) \right) = \lim_{x \to 2} (f(x) - 5) = 0
$$
$$
\implies \quad \boxed{\lim_{x \to 2} f(x) = 5}
$$

> [!note]
> The above problem tells us that if $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$ exists and $g(c) = 0$, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} f(x)$ must also be $0$.

**Problem 53**

**(a)** Find $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} x \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)$. $\qquad$ **(b)** Find $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} x^2 \cos\left(\frac{1}{x^3}\right)$.

_Sol._ **(a)** We know that $-1 \le \sin\dfrac{1}{x} \le 1$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R} \setminus {0}$.
- If $x > 0 \implies -x \le x \sin\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right) \le x$, and
- if $x < 0 \implies -x \ge x \sin\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right) \ge x$.

In either case we can see that
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} x \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) = 0
$$
the result is an application of the squeeze theorem.

**(b)** In a similar way we can prove that $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} x^2 \cos\left(\frac{1}{x^3}\right) = 0$.

# 1.3 Target Values and Formal Definition of Limits

### Keeping outputs near target values

We sometimes need to know what input values will keep output values near a particular target value. How near depends on the requirement.

> [!example]
> How close to $x_0 = 4$ must we hold the input values $x$ to be sure that the output of $y = 2x - 1$ lies within $2$ units of $y_0 = 7$?

_Sol._
$$
|y - 7| < 2 \implies |(2x - 1) - 7| < 2 \implies |2x - 8| < 2
$$
$$
\implies \quad |x - 4| < 1
$$

Hence we must be within $1$ unit distance of $4$ in the input for the output to stay within $2$ unit distance of $7$.

> [!note]
> A graphical way to find the inputs for which the output will be within a certain distance of the target value is to draw horizontal lines around the target $y_0$ and find the part of the graph in between the lines.

> If the output target value is $y_0$ with distance $\varepsilon > 0$, then we have the following.

### A formal definition of limit

> [!info] Definition
> Let $f(x)$ be a function defined on an open interval about $x_0$, except possibly at $x_0$ itself. We say that $f(x)$ approaches the limit $L$ as $x$ approaches $x_0$ and write
> $$
> \lim_{x \to x_0} f(x) = L
> $$
> if, for every number $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a corresponding number $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x$
> $$
> 0 < |x - x_0| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon
> $$

> [!note]
> **(i)** The definition does not provide us with any method of calculating limits, but it does provide us a way to test the validity of a value that we suspect is the limit.
> **(ii)** The other important thing about the limit definition is that it allows us to prove the theorems, which will then help us compute different limit formulas.

> [!example]
> Show that $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 1} (5x - 3) = 2$.

_Sol._ Let $\varepsilon$ be any number such that $\varepsilon > 0$. Then for $2$ to be the limit we should be able to find a $\delta$ around $1$ such that
$$
\implies |5x - 3 - 2| < \varepsilon
$$
$$
\implies |5(x - 1)| = 5|x - 1| < \varepsilon
$$
$$
\implies |x - 1| < \frac{\varepsilon}{5}
$$
Hence, if $\delta = \dfrac{\varepsilon}{5}$, then if the input is within $\delta$ distance of $1$, the output is within $\varepsilon$ distance of $2$.

> [!example] 
> Two important limits Verify:
> **(a)** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to x_0} x = x_0$, $\qquad$
> **(b)** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to x_0} k = k$

_Sol._

**(a)** For any $\varepsilon > 0$, we have $$|y - x_0| < \varepsilon \implies |x - x_0| < \varepsilon \qquad (\because y = x)$$
Hence $\delta = \varepsilon$, which always exists.

**(b)** Again, for any $\varepsilon > 0$, we need
$$
|y - k| < \varepsilon \implies |k - k| < \varepsilon \implies 0 < \varepsilon
$$
Hence, in this case literally any $\delta > 0$ will work.

### How to find a δ for a given f, L, x₀ and ε > 0 algebraically

The process of finding a $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x$
$$
0 < |x - x_0| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon
$$
can be accomplished in two steps.

**Step 1:** Solve the inequality $|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$ to find an open interval $(a, b)$ about $x_0$ on which the inequality holds for all $x \neq x_0$.

**Step 2:** %% p. 14 %% Find a value $\delta > 0$ that places the open interval $(x_0 - \delta,, x_0 + \delta)$ centered at $x_0$ inside the interval $(a, b)$. The inequality $|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$ will hold for all $x \neq x_0$ in this $\delta$-interval.

> [!example] 
> Prove that $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} f(x) = 4$ if
> $$
> f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2, & x \neq 2 \\ 1, & x = 2 \end{cases}
> $$

_Sol._

**(i)** For any $\varepsilon > 0$ we must have the following:
$$
\begin{align}
|f(x) - 4| &  < \varepsilon \\
\implies -\varepsilon  & < x^2 - 4 < \varepsilon \\
\implies 4 - \varepsilon  & < x^2 < 4 + \varepsilon\\
\implies \sqrt{4 - \varepsilon} &  < x < \sqrt{4 + \varepsilon} \qquad (\text{we can assume } \varepsilon < 4)
\end{align}
$$
**(ii)** We can choose
$$
\delta = \min\left(2 - \sqrt{4 - \varepsilon}, \sqrt{4 + \varepsilon} - 2\right)
$$
and then the interval $(2 - \delta,, 2 + \delta)$ will be within the interval that we found in (i).

> [!note]
> Above we took $\varepsilon < 4$ and that is alright, because if $\varepsilon \ge 4$, then choosing some other $\varepsilon' < 4$ and solving for it will actually give us a $\delta$ which will be valid for the bigger $\varepsilon$ also. That is because if $\varepsilon' < \varepsilon$, then the interval $(f(x) - \varepsilon',, f(x) + \varepsilon')$ is actually inside of the interval $(f(x) - \varepsilon,, f(x) + \varepsilon)$. %% p. 15 %%

### Using the definition to prove theorems

Proof of the arithmetic operations on limits.
#### (i) Sum Rule

If $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = M$, then
$$
\lim_{x \to c} \big(f(x) + g(x)\big) = L + M
$$
**Proof.** For any $\varepsilon > 0$, we need to find $\delta > 0$ such that for all
$$
 0  < |x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) + g(x) - (L + M)| < \varepsilon
$$
$$
\implies  |(f(x) - L) + (g(x) - M)| \le |f(x) - L| + |g(x) - M|
$$
Now there exist $\delta_1, \delta_2 > 0$ such that
$$
\text{if } 0 < |x - c| < \delta_1 \implies |f(x) - L| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}
$$
$$
\text{if } 0 < |x - c| < \delta_2 \implies |g(x) - M| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}
$$
The above inequalities will hold if we choose any $\delta$ which is less than both $\delta_1, \delta_2$; let $\delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2)$.

Then if $0 < |x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \dfrac{\varepsilon}{2}$ and $|g(x) - M| < \dfrac{\varepsilon}{2}$
$$
\implies |f(x) - L| + |g(x) - M| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} = \varepsilon
$$
$$
\implies |f(x) + g(x) - (L + M)| < \varepsilon
$$
Hence, $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} \big(f(x) + g(x)\big) = L + M$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]
> The Difference Rule can be proved similarly

#### (ii) Product Rule
$$
\lim_{x \to c} f(x) \cdot g(x) = LM
$$
**Proof.** Let $D$ be the intersection of the domains of $f$ and $g$. Then for any $\varepsilon > 0$ we need to prove that there exists $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x$
$$
0 < |x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) \cdot g(x) - LM| < \varepsilon
$$
Write $f(x) = L + (f(x) - L)$ and $g(x) = M + (g(x) - M)$. Then
$$
f(x) \cdot g(x) = LM + L(g(x) - M) + M(f(x) - L) + (f(x) - L)\cdot(g(x) - M)
$$
$$
\implies f(x) \cdot g(x) - LM = L \cdot(g(x) - M) + M \cdot (f(x) - L) + (f(x) - L) \cdot (g(x) - M)
$$
$$
\implies |f(x) \cdot g(x) - LM| \le |L| \cdot |g(x) - M| + |M| \cdot |f(x) - L| + |f(x) - L| \cdot |g(x) - M|
$$
The above result follows from $|a + b + c| \leq |a| + |b| + |c|$. Now,
$$
|L| \cdot |g(x) - M| \le (1 + |L|) \cdot |g(x) - M|, \qquad |M| \cdot |f(x) - L| \le (1 + |M|) \cdot |f(x) - L|
$$
Since $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = M$, we can find $\delta_1, \delta_2, \delta_3$ and $\delta_4$ such that
$$
\text{if } 0 < |x - c| < \delta_1 \implies |f(x) - L| < \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{3}}
$$
$$
\text{if } 0 < |x - c| < \delta_2 \implies |g(x) - M| < \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{3}}
$$
$$
\text{if } 0 < |x - c| < \delta_3 \implies |f(x) - L| < \frac{\varepsilon}{3(1 + |M|)}
$$
$$
\text{if } 0 < |x - c| < \delta_4 \implies |g(x) - M| < \frac{\varepsilon}{3(1 + |L|)}
$$
If we choose $\delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2, \delta_3, \delta_4)$ we get that all of the above inequalities will be true for $0 < |x - c| < \delta$
$$
\begin{align} \implies |f(x) \cdot g(x) - LM| &\le (|L| + 1) \cdot |g(x) - M| \\ &\quad + (1 + |M|),|f(x) - L| \\ &\quad + |f(x) - L|,|g(x) - M| \\ &< \frac{\varepsilon}{3} + \frac{\varepsilon}{3} + \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{3}}\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{3}} = \varepsilon \end{align}
$$
$$
\implies |f(x) \cdot g(x) - LM| < \varepsilon \quad \text{for } \delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2, \delta_3, \delta_4) \qquad \blacksquare
$$

#### (iii) Quotient Rule
$$
\lim_{x \to c} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{L}{M}, \qquad M \neq 0
$$

**Proof.** If we are able to prove that $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} \frac{1}{g(x)} = \frac{1}{M}$, then using the product rule the quotient rule will follow.
If $0 < |x - c| < \delta \implies \left|\dfrac{1}{g(x)} - \dfrac{1}{M}\right| < \varepsilon$, we need to find a $\delta$ for every given $\varepsilon > 0$.
Now we have that
$$
\left|\frac{1}{g(x)} - \frac{1}{M}\right| = \left|\frac{M - g(x)}{g(x) \cdot M}\right| \le \frac{|M - g(x)|}{|g(x)| \cdot |M|} \tag{1}
$$
Now, $|M| > 0$, so there exists $\delta_1$ such that
$$
\text{if } 0 < |x - c| < \delta_1 \implies |g(x) - M| < \frac{|M|}{2}
$$
We also know that $\big||g(x)| - |M|\big| \le |g(x) - M|$
$$
\implies \big||g(x)| - |M|\big| < \frac{|M|}{2}
$$
$$
\implies -\frac{|M|}{2} < |g(x)| - |M| < \frac{|M|}{2} \implies \frac{|M|}{2} < |g(x)| < \frac{3|M|}{2}
$$
$$
\implies \frac{2}{3|M|} < \frac{1}{|g(x)|} < \frac{2}{|M|}
$$
Using this result in $(1)$:
$$
\implies \left|\frac{1}{g(x)} - \frac{1}{M}\right| \le \frac{|M - g(x)|}{|M|,|g(x)|} \le \frac{|M - g(x)|}{|M|^2} \cdot 2
$$
Now, we can find $\delta_2$ such that
$$
|g(x) - M| < \frac{\varepsilon \cdot |M|^2}{2}
$$
Choosing $\delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2)$ will make sure that both the inequalities are satisfied, and hence we get that
$$
\text{if } 0 < |x - c| < \delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2) \implies \left|\frac{1}{g(x)} - \frac{1}{M}\right| < \varepsilon
$$
for all $\varepsilon > 0$.

Hence, the quotient rule is proved. $\blacksquare$

>[!note]
> **Proof of $\big||x| - |y|\big| \leq |x - y|$**
> The upper bound of $|x| - |y|$ is,
> $$
> |x| = |x - y + y| \leq |x - y| + |y| \implies |x| - |y| \leq |x - y|
> $$
> Similarly, we can get the lower bound as,
> $$
> |y| = |y - x + x| \leq |x - y| + |x| \implies -|x - y| \leq |x| - |y|
> $$
> Hence, from the above results we get,
> $$
> -|x - y| \leq |x| - |y| \leq |x - y| \implies \big||x| - |y|\big| \leq |x - y|
> $$

#### (iv) Constant Multiple Rule
$$
\lim_{x \to c} k f(x) = kL, \quad \forall k \in \mathbb{R}
$$
**Proof.** Proving this is easier than the others that we saw so far. Let $\varepsilon > 0$ be any real number; then we have
$$
|k f(x) - kL| < \varepsilon \implies |k|,|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon
$$
$$
\implies |f(x) - L| < \frac{\varepsilon}{|k|}
$$
And we know there always exists a $\delta > 0$ such that if $0 < |x - c| < \delta$, then $|f(x) - L| < \dfrac{\varepsilon}{|k|}$; hence for that same $\delta$, we get $|k f(x) - kL| < \varepsilon$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]
> We will not be proving the power rule as it is beyond the scope of this book.

#### (v) Proof of Sandwich Theorem

Suppose that $g(x) \leq f(x) \leq h(x)$ for all $x$ in some open interval containing $c$, except possibly at $x = c$. Suppose also that $\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = \lim_{x \to c} h(x) = L$. Then
$$
\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L
$$

**Proof.**

**(i) Proof of Right-Hand Limits:** If $\lim_{x \to c^+} g(x) = \lim_{x \to c^+} h(x) = L$

then for any $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $\delta > 0$, such that for all $x$ satisfying $c < x < c+\delta$, we have
$$
|g(x)-L| < \varepsilon \implies -\varepsilon < g(x)-L < \varepsilon
$$
$$
\implies L-\varepsilon < g(x) < L+\varepsilon
$$
Similarly, $L-\varepsilon < h(x) < L+\varepsilon$, combining both of these with the inequality with $f(x)$, we get
$$
L-\varepsilon < g(x) \leq f(x) \leq h(x) < L+\varepsilon
$$
$$
\implies L-\varepsilon < f(x) < L+\varepsilon
$$
$$
\implies -\varepsilon < f(x)-L < \varepsilon \implies |f(x)-L| < \varepsilon
$$
Hence, we have that $\lim_{x \to c^+} f(x) = L$.

**(ii) Proof of Left-Hand Limit:** If $\lim_{x \to c^-} g(x) = \lim_{x \to c^-} h(x) = L$,

then again for any $\varepsilon > 0$, we get $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x$ in $c-\delta < x < c$, we have
$$
L-\varepsilon < g(x) < L+\varepsilon, \quad L-\varepsilon < h(x) < L+\varepsilon.
$$
Proceeding same as last time we get $\lim_{x \to c^-} f(x) = L$.

**(iii) Proof of Two Sided Limits:** If $\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = \lim_{x \to c} h(x) = L$
$$
\implies \lim_{x \to c^+} g(x) = \lim_{x \to c^+} h(x) = L \implies \lim_{x \to c^+} f(x) = L
$$
$$
\implies \lim_{x \to c^-} g(x) = \lim_{x \to c^-} h(x) = L \implies \lim_{x \to c^-} f(x) = L
$$
Hence, we get that $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$

Hence, proved the sandwich theorem. $\blacksquare$

## 1.4 Extensions of the Limit Concept

### Informal definition of right-hand and left-hand limits

> [!info] Right-hand limit
> Let $f(x)$ be defined on an interval $(a, b)$ where $a < b$. If $f(x)$ approaches arbitrarily close to $L$ as $x$ approaches $a$ from within that interval, then we say that $f$ has a **right-hand limit** at $a$, and we write
> $$
> \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L
> $$

> [!info] Left-hand limit
> Let $f(x)$ be defined on the interval $(c, a)$ where $c < a$. If $f(x)$ gets arbitrarily close to $M$ as $x$ approaches $a$ from within the interval $(c, a)$, then we say that $f(x)$ has a **left-hand limit** at $a$, and we write
> $$
> \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = M
> $$

> [!example]
> Find the left-hand and right-hand limits of $f(x) = \dfrac{x}{|x|}$ at $x = 0$.

_Sol._
$$
\lim_{x \to 0^-} \frac{x}{|x|} = \lim_{x \to 0^-} (-1) = -1, \qquad \text{similarly} \qquad \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{x}{|x|} = 1
$$
> [!note]
> **(i)** A function cannot have an ordinary limit at the end points of its domain, but it can have a one-sided limit.
> **(ii)** One-sided limits have the same properties as listed in Theorem 1, i.e. sum, difference, product, etc. Also the Sandwich Theorem holds true for one-sided limits.

### One-sided vs two-sided limits

> [!abstract] Theorem 5
> A function has a limit as $x$ approaches $c$ if and only if it has a left-hand and a right-hand limit at that point and both of them are equal.
> $$
> \lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L \iff \lim_{x \to c^+} f(x) = \lim_{x \to c^-} f(x) = L
> $$

### Precise definition of one-sided limits

> [!info] Definition
> We say that $f(x)$ has right-hand limit $L$ at $x_0$ and write
> $$
> \lim_{x \to x_0^+} f(x) = L
> $$
> if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x$
> $$
> x_0 < x < x_0 + \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon
> $$

> [!info] Left-hand limit
> We say that $f(x)$ has left-hand limit $L$ at $x_0$ and write
> $$
> \lim_{x \to x_0^-} f(x) = L
> $$
> if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x$
> $$
> x_0 - \delta < x < x_0 \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon
> $$

### The relation between one-sided and two-sided limits

Proving that if the limit exists then both the left-hand and right-hand limits exist and they are equal.

**Case I:**
$$
\lim_{x \to x_0} f(x) = L \implies \lim_{x \to x_0^+} f(x) = \lim_{x \to x_0^-} f(x) = L
$$
**Proof.** For every $\varepsilon > 0$, we have $\delta > 0$ such that 
$$
\text{if } 0 < |x - x_0| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon
$$
$$
\implies 0 < x - x_0 < \delta \implies x_0 < x < x_0 + \delta
$$
$$
\implies -\delta < x - x_0 < 0 \implies x_0 - \delta < x < x_0
$$
Hence, both the left-hand and right-hand limits exist and they are equal.

**Case II:**
$$
\lim_{x \to x_0^+} f(x) = \lim_{x \to x_0^-} f(x) = L \implies \lim_{x \to x_0} f(x) = L
$$
**Proof.** For every $\varepsilon$ there exist $\delta_1, \delta_2 > 0$ such that
$$
\text{if } x_0 < x < x_0 + \delta_1 \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon
$$
$$
\implies 0 < x - x_0 < \delta_1 \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon
$$
Similarly, if $x_0 - \delta_2 < x < x_0 \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$
$$
\implies -\delta_2 < x - x_0 < 0 \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon
$$
Let $\delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2)$. Hence we have that if $-\delta_2 < x - x_0 < 0$, also $\delta \le \delta_2 \implies -\delta_2 \le -\delta$.
$$
\implies \text{if } -\delta < x - x_0 < 0 \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon
$$
Similarly, if $0 < x - x_0 < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$
$$
\implies \text{if } 0 < |x - x_0| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon
$$
Hence, we have that $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to x_0} f(x) = L$. $\blacksquare$

### Precise definition of infinite limits

> [!info] Infinite limits
> **(i)** We say that $f(x)$ approaches infinity as $x$ approaches $x_0$ and write
> $$
> \lim_{x \to x_0} f(x) = \infty
> $$
> if for every positive $B$ there exists a corresponding $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x$
> $$
> 0 < |x - x_0| < \delta \implies f(x) > B
> $$
> **(ii)** We say that $f(x)$ approaches $-\infty$ as $x$ approaches $x_0$ and write
> $$
> \lim_{x \to x_0} f(x) = -\infty
> $$
> if for every negative real number $-B$, %% p. 24 %% there exists $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x$
> $$
> 0 < |x - x_0| < \delta \implies f(x) < -B
> $$

> [!note]
> Similarly we can form the definitions of infinite one-sided limits.

## 1.5 Continuity

### Continuity at a point

> [!info] Definition
> Continuity at a point A function $f$ is **continuous at an interior point** $x = c$ of its domain if
> $$
> \lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)
> $$

### Types of discontinuity

**(i) Removable:** when the limit at $x = c$ exists but the function is either not defined at $c$ or has a value other than the limit. In this case we can redefine the function to make it continuous.

E.g. $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 2}{x - \sqrt{2}}$ at $x = \sqrt{2}$

_Sol._ $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \sqrt{2}} f(x) = 2\sqrt{2}$; if we define $f(\sqrt{2}) = 2\sqrt{2}$, then we can make the function continuous.

> [!figure] Figure 4
> ![](Pasted%20image%2020260920232745.png)

**(ii) Jump discontinuity:** %% p. 25 %% this is when the graph has an abrupt jump at $x = c$; in this case one-sided limits exist but they are not equal.

E.g. $f(x) = [x] \implies$ has a jump discontinuity at all integers.

> [!figure] Figure 5 — Jump discontinuity
> ![](Pasted%20image%2020260920232653.png)

**(iii) Infinite discontinuity:** this is when the function has an infinite limit at $x = c$; in this case one-sided limits also don't exist.

E.g. $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x - 2}$, at $x = 2$

⟹ This type of discontinuity cannot be fixed, we are hopeless.

> [!figure] Figure 6 — Infinite discontinuity
> ![](Pasted%20image%2020260920232459.png)

**(iv) Oscillating discontinuity:** this is when the value of the function oscillates very aggressively around $x = c$.

E.g. $f(x) = \sin\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right)$

> [!figure] Figure 7 - Oscillating Discontinuity
> ![](Pasted%20image%2020260920232317.png) 

### Left and right continuity

A function $f$ is **continuous at a left endpoint** $x = a$ of its domain if
$$
\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = f(a)
$$
and **continuous at a right endpoint** $x = b$ of its domain if
$$
\lim_{x \to b^-} f(x) = f(b)
$$

> [!note]
> The previous definition talks about the continuity of a function at its endpoints, but in general $f$ is called **right continuous** at $a$ if
> $$
> \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = f(a)
> $$
> and is called **left continuous** at $a$ if
> $$
> \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = f(a)
> $$

> [!example]
> Discuss the continuity of $f(x) = [x]$ at $x = 1$.

_Sol._ At $x = 1$, we have $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x) = 1$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x) = 0$ and $f(1) = 1$.

Hence, $f$ is right continuous at $x = 1$ and is neither left continuous nor continuous at $x = 1$.

### Continuity of algebraic combinations

> [!abstract] Theorem 6
> If functions $f$ and $g$ are continuous at $x = c$, then the following functions are also continuous at $x = c$:
> 
> - **(i)** $f + g$
> - **(ii)** $f - g$
> - **(iii)** $fg$
> - **(iv)** $kf$, $\forall k \in \mathbb{R}$
> - **(v)** $\dfrac{f}{g}$, provided $g(c) \neq 0$
> - **(vi)** $(f(x))^{m/n}$, provided $(f(c))^{m/n} \in \mathbb{R}$

**Proof.** Let $f(c) = L$ and $g(c) = M$.
$$
\implies \lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c) = L, \qquad \lim_{x \to c} g(x) = g(c) = M
$$
Now, we know that $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} \big(f(x) + g(x)\big) = L + M$, also $f(c) + g(c) = L + M$. Hence $f(x) + g(x)$ is continuous at $x = c$. Similarly we can prove the others as well. $\blacksquare$

### Continuity of polynomials and rational functions

> [!abstract] Theorem 7
> Every polynomial is continuous at every point on the real number line, and every rational function is continuous at every point except where the denominator is $0$.

**Proof.** This theorem can be proved as a consequence of Theorem 6.

### Continuity of composites

> [!abstract] Theorem 8
> Composites If $f$ is continuous at $c$, and $g$ is continuous at $f(c)$, then $g \circ f$ is continuous at $c$.

**Proof.** Since $f$ is continuous at $x = c \implies \displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)$. But $g$ is continuous at $x = f(c) \implies \displaystyle\lim_{x \to f(c)} g(x) = g(f(c))$. Hence, for any $\varepsilon > 0$, there always exists a $\delta_g > 0$ such that for all $x$
$$
0 < |x - f(c)| < \delta_g \implies |g(x) - g(f(c))| < \varepsilon
$$
In this, if we replace $x$ with $f(x)$ satisfying the same condition, then we get for all $f(x)$ such that
$$
0 < |f(x) - f(c)| < \delta_g \implies |g(f(x)) - g(f(c))| < \varepsilon
$$
Now, we can always find $\delta_f > 0$ such that for all $x$
$$
0 < |x - c| < \delta_f \implies |f(x) - f(c)| < \delta_g
$$
Hence, for any $\varepsilon > 0$ we can find a $\delta_f > 0$ such that for all $x$
$$
0 < |x - c| < \delta_f \implies |g(f(x)) - g(f(c))| < \varepsilon
$$
$$
\implies \lim_{x \to c} g(f(x)) = g(f(c))
$$
Hence, $g \circ f$ is continuous at $x = c$. $\blacksquare$

This theorem can be visualized as follows.

> [!figure] Figure 8
> ![](Pasted%20image%2020260920233424.png)

> [!note]
> In general we have that if $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to L} g(x) = M$, then in a similar way we can prove that $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} g \circ f(x) = M$.

> [!example] Example - Continuity of the absolute value function

_Sol._ The function $y = f(x) = |x|$ is continuous everywhere on the real number line.

> [!example] Example — Continuity of trigonometric functions
> _Sol._ Later on we will see that the functions $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ are continuous everywhere on the real number line.
> 
> ⟹ Using Theorem 6, we get that ratios and reciprocals of $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ are also continuous:
> $$
> \tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}, \quad \cot x = \frac{\cos x}{\sin x}, \quad \sec x = \frac{1}{\cos x}, \quad \csc x = \frac{1}{\sin x}
> $$
> are all continuous functions in their respective domains.

> [!example]
> Discuss the continuity of $\dfrac{x \cos\left(x^{2/3}\right)}{1 + x^4}$.

_Sol._

- $x^{2/3}$ is continuous everywhere using Theorem 6.
- ⟹ $\cos\left(x^{2/3}\right)$ is continuous everywhere using Theorem 8.
- ⟹ $x \cdot \cos\left(x^{2/3}\right)$ is continuous, using Theorem 6.
- ⟹ $\dfrac{x \cos\left(x^{2/3}\right)}{1 + x^4}$ is continuous, using Theorem 6.

### Continuous extension to a point

If a function is discontinuous at $x = c$, but $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$ exists, then we can define a new function $F$ called the **continuous extension** of $f$ to $x = c$, as
$$
F(x) = \begin{cases} f(x), & \text{if } x \in D(f) \text{ and } x \neq c \\ L, & \text{if } x = c \end{cases}
$$

### Continuous on intervals

- A function is called **continuous** if it is continuous everywhere in its domain. A function not continuous in its domain may still be continuous when restricted to particular intervals within its domain.
- A function is said to be **continuous on an interval** $I$ in its domain if $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)$ for all interior points of the interval and it is one-sided continuous at any endpoints that $I$ may contain.
- A function that is continuous on $I$ is also continuous on any interval contained inside of $I$.

> [!example] Example
> The function $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$ is continuous on $(-\infty, 0)$ and on $(0, \infty)$.

### Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT)

> [!abstract] Theorem 9
> Suppose $f(x)$ is continuous on an interval $I$, and $a$ and $b$ are any two points inside $I$. Then if $y_0$ is any number between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, there exists a number $c$ between $a$ and $b$ such that $f(c) = y_0$.

**Proof.** Consider the case when $f(a) < y_0 < f(b)$; we will prove this for this case, for the other cases the proof is similar.

Let $S = {x \mid x \in [a, b] \text{ and } f(x) < y_0}$.

Since $S$ is non-empty, i.e. $a \in S$, and $S$ has an upper bound $b$, this means that $S$ has a supremum. Say $\sup S = c$.

Now we will prove that $f(c)$ must be equal to $y_0$.

**Case I:** $f(c) < y_0$.

In this case we can find an interval $(c - \delta, c + \delta)$ such that for all $x$
$$
0 < |x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(c)| < |f(c) - y_0|
$$
This is possible due to the fact that $f(x)$ is continuous on $[a, b]$.
$$
\implies |f(x) - f(c)| < y_0 - f(c) \qquad (\because f(c) < y_0)
$$
$$
\implies -(y_0 - f(c)) < f(x) - f(c) < y_0 - f(c)
$$
$$
\implies -(y_0 - 2f(c)) < f(x) < y_0 \implies \boxed{f(x) < y_0}
$$
If $c < x < c + \delta \implies f(x) < y_0$, hence all $x \in (c, c + \delta)$ belong to $S$, but this is a contradiction to the fact that $c = \sup S$. Hence, $f(c) < y_0$ is not possible.

**Case II:** $f(c) > y_0$.

Just like the last case we can find an interval $(c - \delta, c + \delta)$ in which $f(x) > y_0$.

Now, $s \in S \implies s \le c$; also $s \notin (c - \delta, c)$ since for all $x$ in this interval $f(x) > y_0$.
$$
\implies s \le c - \delta \implies c - \delta \text{ is an upper bound,}
$$
which is a contradiction to the fact that $c$ is the supremum. Hence $f(c) > y_0$ is also not possible.
$$
\implies f(c) = y_0
$$
hence proving the IVT. $\blacksquare$

### The consequence for root finding

If $f$ is a continuous function on the interval $[a, b]$ and $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ have different signs, then there exists at least one $c \in [a, b]$ such that $f(c) = 0$.

This is a direct consequence of Theorem 9.

## 1.6 Tangent Lines

### Some incorrect statements

**(i)** _"The line $L$ is tangent to curve $C$ at point $P$ if it meets $C$ at only one point $P$."_

This is wrong, as a line cutting the curve would also be called a tangent then.

**(ii)** _"A line that just touches the curve at $P$ and doesn't cross it."_

This is better but still not completely correct, as a line can be tangent but still cut the curve at other points.

**(iii)** _"A line that touches the curve and always lies on one side of the curve."_

The example disproves this.

>[!figure] Figure 11
>![](Pasted%20image%2020260920234135.png)

### Tangent as limit of secant slopes

A tangent line to curve $C$ at point $P$ can be defined in terms of the slopes of secant lines through $P$ to nearby points $Q$ as $Q$ approaches $P$ along the curve from either side.

> [!figure] Figure 12
> ![](Pasted%20image%2020260920234312.png)

> [!info] Definition
> The **slope** of the curve $y = f(x)$ at the point $P(x_0, f(x_0))$ is the number
> $$
> m = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h} \qquad (\text{provided the limit exists})
> $$
> The **tangent line** to the curve at $P$ is the line through $P$ with this slope.

### Rates of change

- The expression $\dfrac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h}$ is called the **difference quotient** of $f$ at $x_0$.
- The limit of the difference quotient as $h \to 0$ is called the **derivative** of $f$ at $x_0$.
- When looking at it as slopes, the difference quotient is the slope of the secant and the derivative is the slope of the tangent.
- When looking at this in terms of rate of change, we see that the difference quotient is the **average rate of change** and the derivative is the **instantaneous rate of change** of the function with respect to $x$ at $x_0$.

### Problem 58

Is it true that if you stretch a rubber band by moving one end to the right and one end to the left, some point of the band will end up in its original position?

_Sol._ Consider the function $f(x)$ which gives the new position of point $x$. We can see that this function is continuous, as there are no breaks in the rubber band after it is stretched.

- The function $d(x) = f(x) - x$ gives the deviation of a point from its original position $x$ to its final position $f(x)$.
- $d(x)$ is also continuous, and $d(x)$ for the left end is negative as $f(x) < x$, and for the right end it will be positive as $f(x) > x$.
- Hence, by the IVT there exists some point $c$ for which $d(c) = 0 \implies f(c) - c = 0 \implies f(c) = c$.

### Problem 59 — A fixed point theorem

Suppose that a function $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[0, 1]$ and that $0 \le f(x) \le 1$ for every $x$ in $[0, 1]$. Show that there exists $c \in [0, 1]$ such that $f(c) = c$. ($c$ is called a **fixed point** of $f$.)

_Sol._ Consider: if $f(0) = 0$ or $f(1) = 1$ then it is proved. But if that is not the case, then what we can do is create a function $d(x)$ as
$$
d(x) = f(x) - x
$$
Now $f(0) > 0$ and $f(1) < 1$, since $f(0) \neq 0$ and $f(1) \neq 1$ and $0 \le f(x) \le 1$.
$$
\implies d(0) = f(0) - 0 = f(0) > 0
$$
$$
\implies d(1) = f(1) - 1 < 0
$$
Now $d(x)$ is continuous on $[0, 1]$ and $d(0)$ and $d(1)$ have different signs, which means that $\exists, c \in [0, 1]$ such that $d(c) = 0$
$$
\implies f(c) - c = 0 \implies f(c) = c
$$

### Problem 60 — The sign-preserving property of continuous functions

Let $f$ be defined on an interval $(a, b)$ and suppose that $f(c) \neq 0$ at some $c$ where $f$ is continuous. Show that there is an interval $(c - \delta, c + \delta)$ about $c$ where $f$ has the same sign as $f(c)$.

_Sol._ $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)$, so for $\varepsilon = |f(c)|$ we have $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x$
$$
|x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(c)| < |f(c)|
$$
$$
\implies -|f(c)| < f(x) - f(c) < |f(c)| \implies f(c) - |f(c)| < f(x) < |f(c)| + f(c)
$$
- If $f(c) > 0 \implies |f(c)| = f(c) \implies 0 < f(x) < 2f(c)$
- If $f(c) < 0 \implies |f(c)| = -f(c) \implies 2f(c) < f(x) < 0$

Hence in the interval $(c - \delta, c + \delta)$ we have $f(c)$ and $f(x)$ having the same sign. $\blacksquare$

> [!info] Definition
> Vertical tangent We say that the curve $y = f(x)$ has a **vertical tangent** at $x = x_0$ if
> $$
> \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h} = \infty (\text{or}) -\infty
> $$

### Problem 13 — Antipodal points

Is there any reason to believe that there is always a pair of antipodal points on the earth's equator where the temperatures are the same? Explain.

_Sol._ This is an application of the intermediate value theorem. Let $T$ be a function that gives the temperature at any point on earth.

- Clearly $T$ must be a continuous function, as temperature doesn't just jump from one value to another; it varies continuously.
- Let $P$ be a point on the equator and $P'$ its antipodal point. Now let $f(P) = T(P) - T(P')$, which is a continuous function. If the temperatures at $P$ and $P'$ are the same, the problem is solved.
- But if the temperatures are not the same, then $f(P) > 0$ or $f(P) < 0$. As we move along the equator and reach $P'$, we will get $f(P')$ to have a different sign compared to $f(P)$, which means that at %% p. 39 %% some point on the way $f(x) = 0$, and that is the point that has the same temperature as its antipodal point.

> [!note] Borsuk–Ulam Theorem
> It is the general version of the previous problem. It states that at any given time there are at least two antipodal points on the earth's surface that have the exact same temperature and pressure simultaneously.
> 
> Proving the theorem just for temperature _or_ pressure is easy, but both at the same time requires some advanced maths concepts.

### Problem 14 — Uniqueness of limits

Show that a function cannot have different limits at the same point, that is, if $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to x_0} f(x) = L_1$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to x_0} f(x) = L_2$, then $L_1 = L_2$.

_Sol._ This can be proved using the fact that the function cannot get arbitrarily close to two different points at the same time.

Without loss of generality, let $L_1 < L_2$ and $\varepsilon = \dfrac{L_2 - L_1}{2}$. Then there exist $\delta_1$ and $\delta_2$ such that
$$
\text{if } 0 < |x - x_0| < \delta_1 \implies |f(x) - L_1| < \varepsilon
$$
$$
\text{if } 0 < |x - x_0| < \delta_2 \implies |f(x) - L_2| < \varepsilon
$$

If we choose $\delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2)$, then both of the above conditions must be true simultaneously, but that is not possible as the ranges $(L_1 - \varepsilon, L_1 + \varepsilon)$ and $(L_2 - \varepsilon, L_2 + \varepsilon)$ are disjoint. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 24 — The Dirichlet ruler function

If $x$ is a rational number, then $x$ can be written in a unique way as a quotient of integers $\dfrac{m}{n}$, where $n > 0$ and $m$ and $n$ have no common factor greater than $1$. We say that the fraction is in the **lowest form**. Let $f(x)$ be defined for all $x \in [0, 1]$ by
$$
f(x) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{n}, & \text{if } x = \dfrac{m}{n} \text{ in lowest form} \\[2mm] 0, & \text{if } x \text{ is irrational} \end{cases}
$$

**(a)** Show that $f$ is discontinuous at every rational number in $[0, 1]$.

**(b)** Show that $f$ is continuous at every irrational number in $[0, 1]$.

**(c)** Sketch the graph of $f$.

_Sol._ %% p. 41 %%

**(a)** Any interval we choose around a rational number will always have irrational numbers, making the function value $0$.

Let $x_0 = \dfrac{m}{n} \implies f(x_0) = \dfrac{1}{n}$. Let $\varepsilon = \dfrac{1}{2n}$; then some $\delta > 0$ must give us $$\left|f(x) - \frac{1}{n}\right| < \frac{1}{2n} \quad \forall x \in (x_0 - \delta,, x_0 + \delta)$$

For irrational $x$ in the range we get $$\left|0 - \frac{1}{n}\right| < \frac{1}{2n} \implies \frac{1}{n} < \frac{1}{2n} \implies 2 < 1$$ which is not possible.

**(b)** For this, consider $x_0$ irrational, so $f(x_0) = 0$. Now let $\varepsilon > 0$ be any number and let $N$ be the smallest integer such that $N\varepsilon > 1 \implies \varepsilon > \dfrac{1}{N}$. This means rationals with denominator $\le N$ should not appear in the range $(x_0 - \delta,, x_0 + \delta)$.

Now, since we are limited to the range $[0, 1]$, for any denominator there are only finitely many rationals in this range. E.g.

- $\dfrac{1}{1}$, with denominator $1$
- $\dfrac{1}{2}$, with denominator $2$
- $\dfrac{1}{3}, \dfrac{2}{3}$, with denominator $3$
- $\dfrac{1}{4}, \dfrac{3}{4}$, with denominator $4$

Let $r_1, r_2, \dots, r_p$ be the rationals with denominator $\le N$. Clearly they are finitely many, and hence choosing $$\delta = \min\big(|r_i - x_0|,; i = 1, \dots, p\big)$$ will give us a range that does not contain any $r_i$. Hence for all other rationals we get $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{n_1} < \varepsilon$, hence $f$ is continuous. $\blacksquare$

**(c)** To create a graph of the function we look at points where the function has a non-zero value, i.e. at rationals.

- $\dfrac{1}{2}$ is the one in the middle, with value $\dfrac{1}{2}$.
- Then come rationals with denominator $3$, i.e. $\dfrac{1}{3}, \dfrac{2}{3}$; both have function value $\dfrac{1}{3}$.
- Then come those with denominator $4$, i.e. $\dfrac{1}{4}, \dfrac{3}{4}$, having height $\dfrac{1}{4}$.
- Then come those with denominator $5$, i.e. $\dfrac{1}{5}, \dfrac{2}{5}, \dfrac{3}{5}, \dfrac{4}{5}$, all having height $\dfrac{1}{5}$.
- And so on; the heights keep decreasing as the denominator keeps increasing.

> [!figure] Figure 14
> ![](Pasted%20image%2020260921113022.png)

The function is called the **ruler function** as its graph looks like the markings on a physical ruler (scale).
