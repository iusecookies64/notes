# 0.1 Foundations: Sets, Relations, and Equivalence Classes

Most textbooks in real analysis begin on page one by handing you the set of real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ as a finished product, listing its field, order, and completeness axioms as if they were handed down on stone tablets. While that axiomatic shortcut lets you jump quickly into limits and continuity, it leaves a nagging foundational question unanswered: *why are we allowed to assume such a number system even exists, and how is it built from the ground up?*

In this preliminary chapter, we are going to build the real number system from scratch, following the classical foundational pipeline:
$$
\emptyset \longrightarrow \mathbb{N} \longrightarrow \mathbb{Z} \longrightarrow \mathbb{Q} \longrightarrow \mathbb{R}
$$
Before we can take the first step, we need a minimal toolkit of set-theoretic language—specifically **ordered pairs**, **equivalence relations**, and **quotient sets**—because every jump from one number system to the next ($\mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Q}$, and $\mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{R}$) relies on bundling simpler objects into sets or equivalence classes.

## Naive Set Theory Essentials

At the bedrock of mathematics lies the notion of a **set**, which we view intuitively as a well-defined collection of mathematical objects called **elements** or **members**. If an object $x$ belongs to a set $A$, we write $x \in A$; if it does not, we write $x \notin A$.

Two sets are identical if and only if they contain the exact same elements—a principle known in set theory as the **Axiom of Extensionality**.

> [!abstract] Definition: Subset and Set Equality
> Let $A$ and $B$ be sets. We say that $A$ is a **subset** of $B$, written $A \subseteq B$, if every element of $A$ is also an element of $B$:
> $$
> A \subseteq B \iff (\forall x, \; x \in A \implies x \in B)
> $$
> Two sets $A$ and $B$ are **equal**, written $A = B$, if and only if $A \subseteq B$ and $B \subseteq A$. If $A \subseteq B$ and $A \neq B$, we say $A$ is a **proper subset** of $B$ and write $A \subsetneq B$.

Notice how set equality gives us our standard strategy for proving that two sets $A$ and $B$ are the same: we show *mutual inclusion*, first proving $x \in A \implies x \in B$, and then proving $x \in B \implies x \in A$. We also postulate the existence of a unique set with no elements at all, called the **empty set** and denoted by $\emptyset$.

In standard set theory, a set $\{\text{a}, \text{b}\}$ is inherently *unordered*: by extensionality, $\{a, b\} = \{b, a\}$, and $\{a, a\} = \{a\}$. However, to build integers like $a - b$ or fractions like $a/b$, order matters—since $2 - 5$ is not the same as $5 - 2$. How can we encode *order* using only unordered sets? Kazimierz Kuratowski gave an ingenious solution in 1921.

> [!abstract] Definition: Ordered Pair and Cartesian Product
> Given two objects $a$ and $b$, the **ordered pair** $(a, b)$ is defined set-theoretically as
> $$
> (a, b) := \{\{a\}, \{a, b\}\}
> $$
> Given two sets $A$ and $B$, their **Cartesian product** $A \times B$ is the set of all ordered pairs whose first coordinate lies in $A$ and whose second coordinate lies in $B$:
> $$
> A \times B := \{(a, b) : a \in A \text{ and } b \in B\}
> $$

Why does Kuratowski's definition work? Because the first coordinate $a$ is singled out as the unique element belonging to *both* member sets $\{a\}$ and $\{a, b\}$, whereas $b$ belongs only to the second when $a \neq b$. This guarantees the fundamental property of ordered pairs:
$$
(a, b) = (c, d) \iff a = c \text{ and } b = d
$$
Once we have Cartesian products, both **relations** and **functions** become ordinary sets. A **binary relation** on a set $X$ is simply a subset $R \subseteq X \times X$, where we write $x \sim y$ (or $x R y$) as shorthand for $(x, y) \in R$. Similarly, a **function** $f: A \to B$ is a subset $f \subseteq A \times B$ such that for every $a \in A$, there exists a unique $b \in B$ with $(a, b) \in f$, which we write as $f(a) = b$.

## Set Operations, Indexed Families, and De Morgan's Laws

In real analysis, we rarely work with just two sets at a time; when studying open sets, compact sets, or Dedekind cuts, we routinely take unions and intersections of *infinitely many* sets indexed by some arbitrary index set $I$ (such as $I = \mathbb{N}$ or $I = \mathbb{R}$). Let's state the precise definitions of binary and indexed set operations.

> [!abstract] Definition: Set Operations and Arbitrary Indexed Unions/Intersections
> Let $A, B$ be subsets of a universal set $X$, and let $\{A_i\}_{i \in I}$ be a family of subsets of $X$ indexed by a non-empty set $I$.
> 1. **Binary Union, Intersection, and Set Difference:**
>    $$
>    \begin{aligned}
>    A \cup B &:= \{x \in X : x \in A \text{ or } x \in B\} \\
>    A \cap B &:= \{x \in X : x \in A \text{ and } x \in B\} \\
>    A \setminus B &:= \{x \in X : x \in A \text{ and } x \notin B\}
>    \end{aligned}
>    $$
>    The **complement** of $A$ in $X$ is $A^c := X \setminus A = \{x \in X : x \notin A\}$. Two sets $A$ and $B$ are **disjoint** if $A \cap B = \emptyset$.
> 2. **Arbitrary Indexed Union and Intersection:**
>    $$
>    \begin{aligned}
>    \bigcup_{i \in I} A_i &:= \{x \in X : \exists i \in I \text{ such that } x \in A_i\} \\
>    \bigcap_{i \in I} A_i &:= \{x \in X : \forall i \in I, \; x \in A_i\}
>    \end{aligned}
>    $$

Notice the clean duality between quantifiers: **union corresponds to $\exists$** ("belongs to *at least one* $A_i$"), whereas **intersection corresponds to $\forall$** ("belongs to *every* $A_i$"). Because negating an existential quantifier produces a universal quantifier ($\neg(\exists i, P(i)) \iff \forall i, \neg P(i)$) and vice versa, taking complements flips unions into intersections and intersections into unions.

> [!theorem] De Morgan's Laws for Arbitrary Indexed Families
> Let $\{A_i\}_{i \in I}$ be a family of subsets of $X$ indexed by a non-empty set $I$. Then:
> $$
> \left(\bigcup_{i \in I} A_i\right)^c = \bigcap_{i \in I} A_i^c \qquad \text{and} \qquad \left(\bigcap_{i \in I} A_i\right)^c = \bigcup_{i \in I} A_i^c
> $$

**Proof:**
To prove the first identity, we track an arbitrary element $x \in X$ through the quantifier definitions:
$$
\begin{aligned}
x \in \left(\bigcup_{i \in I} A_i\right)^c &\iff x \notin \bigcup_{i \in I} A_i \\
                                           &\iff \neg \left(\exists i \in I \text{ such that } x \in A_i\right) \\
                                           &\iff \forall i \in I, \; x \notin A_i \\
                                           &\iff \forall i \in I, \; x \in A_i^c \\
                                           &\iff x \in \bigcap_{i \in I} A_i^c
\end{aligned}
$$
The second identity follows by the exact same logic (or by applying the first identity to the complements $B_i = A_i^c$ and taking complements of both sides). $\blacksquare$

## Functions, Bijections, and Direct/Inverse Images

Given a function $f: A \to B$, the set $A$ is called the **domain** of $f$ and the set $B$ is called the **codomain** of $f$. Keep in mind that the codomain $B$ is the target space where outputs are *allowed* to land; the actual subset of elements in $B$ that get hit by $f$ is called the **range** (or **image**) of $f$, written $f(A) := \{f(a) : a \in A\} \subseteq B$.

> [!abstract] Definition: Injective, Surjective, and Bijective Functions
> Let $f: A \to B$ be a function.
> 1. **Injective (One-to-One):** $f$ is **injective** if distinct inputs always produce distinct outputs:
>    $$
>    \forall a_1, a_2 \in A, \quad f(a_1) = f(a_2) \implies a_1 = a_2
>    $$
>    (Equivalently, $a_1 \neq a_2 \implies f(a_1) \neq f(a_2)$.)
> 2. **Surjective (Onto):** $f$ is **surjective** if every element of the codomain $B$ is hit by at least one element of $A$:
>    $$
>    \forall b \in B, \; \exists a \in A \text{ such that } f(a) = b
>    $$
>    (Equivalently, $f(A) = B$.)
> 3. **Bijective (One-to-One Correspondence):** $f$ is **bijective** if it is both injective and surjective. That is, for every $b \in B$, there exists a *unique* $a \in A$ such that $f(a) = b$.

Why are bijections so important? Because a function $f: A \to B$ has a two-sided **inverse function** $f^{-1}: B \to A$ (satisfying $f^{-1}(f(a)) = a$ for all $a \in A$ and $f(f^{-1}(b)) = b$ for all $b \in B$) **if and only if $f$ is bijective**! Surjectivity ensures $f^{-1}(b)$ has *at least one* value for every $b \in B$, and injectivity ensures $f^{-1}(b)$ has *at most one* value.

Even when a function $f: A \to B$ is **not** bijective (so no point-to-point inverse function $f^{-1}: B \to A$ exists), we can still push subsets of $A$ forward into $B$ and pull subsets of $B$ backward into $A$.

> [!abstract] Definition: Direct Image and Inverse Image (Preimage)
> Let $f: A \to B$ be any function.
> 1. For any subset $E \subseteq A$, the **direct image** of $E$ under $f$ is the subset of $B$ defined by:
>    $$
>    f(E) := \{f(x) : x \in E\} = \{y \in B : \exists x \in E \text{ such that } f(x) = y\}
>    $$
> 2. For any subset $H \subseteq B$, the **inverse image** (or **preimage**) of $H$ under $f$ is the subset of $A$ defined by:
>    $$
>    f^{-1}(H) := \{x \in A : f(x) \in H\}
>    $$

> [!warning] Caution: Preimages Are Much Better Behaved Than Direct Images!
> Notice a subtle abuse of notation: in $f^{-1}(H)$, the symbol $f^{-1}$ does *not* require $f$ to be invertible! It is always defined for any function $f: A \to B$ and any subset $H \subseteq B$.
>
> Even more importantly for Real Analysis (especially when you study continuous functions and open sets): **inverse images $f^{-1}(\cdot)$ preserve ALL set operations** (unions, intersections, and complements), whereas **direct images $f(\cdot)$ preserve unions but generally FAIL to preserve intersections and complements**!

> [!theorem] Behavior of Direct and Inverse Images under Set Operations
> Let $f: A \to B$ be a function, let $\{E_i\}_{i \in I}$ be a family of subsets of $A$, and let $\{H_i\}_{i \in I}$ and $H$ be subsets of $B$.
> 1. **Inverse Images Preserve Everything:**
>    $$
>    f^{-1}\left(\bigcup_{i \in I} H_i\right) = \bigcup_{i \in I} f^{-1}(H_i), \qquad f^{-1}\left(\bigcap_{i \in I} H_i\right) = \bigcap_{i \in I} f^{-1}(H_i), \qquad f^{-1}(B \setminus H) = A \setminus f^{-1}(H)
>    $$
> 2. **Direct Images Preserve Unions, but Only Contained in Intersections:**
>    $$
>    f\left(\bigcup_{i \in I} E_i\right) = \bigcup_{i \in I} f(E_i), \qquad f\left(\bigcap_{i \in I} E_i\right) \subseteq \bigcap_{i \in I} f(E_i)
>    $$
>    Moreover, equality $f\left(\bigcap_{i \in I} E_i\right) = \bigcap_{i \in I} f(E_i)$ holds for all families of subsets if and only if $f$ is **injective**.

**Proof:**
1. Each inverse image identity is a direct one-line translation of logic because $x \in f^{-1}(H) \iff f(x) \in H$:
   - $x \in f^{-1}\left(\bigcup_{i \in I} H_i\right) \iff f(x) \in \bigcup_{i \in I} H_i \iff \exists i \in I, f(x) \in H_i \iff \exists i \in I, x \in f^{-1}(H_i) \iff x \in \bigcup_{i \in I} f^{-1}(H_i)$.
   - Replacing $\exists$ with $\forall$ proves the intersection identity, and $x \in f^{-1}(B \setminus H) \iff f(x) \notin H \iff x \notin f^{-1}(H) \iff x \in A \setminus f^{-1}(H)$.
2. For direct images of unions: $y \in f\left(\bigcup_{i \in I} E_i\right) \iff \exists x \in \bigcup_{i \in I} E_i \text{ with } f(x) = y \iff \exists i \in I, \exists x \in E_i \text{ with } f(x) = y \iff y \in \bigcup_{i \in I} f(E_i)$.
   For intersections, suppose $y \in f\left(\bigcap_{i \in I} E_i\right)$. Then there exists a *single* $x \in \bigcap_{i \in I} E_i$ such that $f(x) = y$. Since $x \in E_i$ for every $i \in I$, we have $y = f(x) \in f(E_i)$ for every $i \in I$, so $y \in \bigcap_{i \in I} f(E_i)$.
   Why can the reverse inclusion fail when $f$ is not injective? If $y \in \bigcap_{i \in I} f(E_i)$, then for each $i \in I$ there is some $x_i \in E_i$ with $f(x_i) = y$, but **different sets $E_i$ might use different inputs $x_i$ that collide at the same output $y$** (for example, $f(x) = x^2$ on $E_1 = [-1, 0]$ and $E_2 = [0, 1]$ has $f(E_1 \cap E_2) = \{0\}$ whereas $f(E_1) \cap f(E_2) = [0, 1]$). If $f$ is injective, $f(x_i) = y = f(x_j)$ forces all $x_i$ to be the same element $x \in \bigcap_{i \in I} E_i$, restoring equality. $\blacksquare$

## Equivalence Relations and Quotients

Why do we care so much about relations when constructing numbers? Think about how you already work with fractions. The expressions $\frac{1}{2}$, $\frac{2}{4}$, and $\frac{3}{6}$ are written using different pairs of integers—$(1, 2)$, $(2, 4)$, and $(3, 6)$—yet they represent the *exact same rational number*. What we are really doing when we define a rational number is taking the set of all integer pairs $(p, q)$ and gluing together all pairs that have the same ratio into a single object.

The mathematical machinery for "gluing together different representations of the same object" is an **equivalence relation**.

> [!abstract] Definition: Equivalence Relation and Quotient Set
> A binary relation $\sim$ on a set $X$ is called an **equivalence relation** if it satisfies the following three properties for all $x, y, z \in X$:
> 1. **Reflexivity:** $x \sim x$.
> 2. **Symmetry:** If $x \sim y$, then $y \sim x$.
> 3. **Transitivity:** If $x \sim y$ and $y \sim z$, then $x \sim z$.
>
> Given an equivalence relation $\sim$ on $X$ and an element $x \in X$, the **equivalence class** of $x$, denoted $[x]$, is the set of all elements in $X$ equivalent to $x$:
> $$
> [x] := \{y \in X : y \sim x\}
> $$
> Any element $y \in [x]$ is called a **representative** of the equivalence class $[x]$. The set of all equivalence classes of $X$ under $\sim$ is called the **quotient set** of $X$ by $\sim$, denoted $X / {\sim}$:
> $$
> X / {\sim} := \{[x] : x \in X\}
> $$

The fundamental geometric insight behind an equivalence relation is that it carves the set $X$ into disjoint, non-overlapping buckets that cover all of $X$. Two equivalence classes are either completely identical or completely disjoint.

> [!theorem] Fundamental Theorem of Equivalence Relations
> Let $\sim$ be an equivalence relation on a set $X$. Then for any $x, y \in X$:
> 1. $x \in [x]$ (every equivalence class is non-empty).
> 2. $[x] = [y]$ if and only if $x \sim y$.
> 3. Either $[x] = [y]$ or $[x] \cap [y] = \emptyset$.

**Proof:**
For (1), reflexivity gives $x \sim x$, so $x \in [x]$ by definition.

For (2), suppose first that $[x] = [y]$. Since $x \in [x]$, we have $x \in [y]$, which means $x \sim y$. Conversely, suppose $x \sim y$. To show $[x] = [y]$, take any $z \in [x]$. Then $z \sim x$. Since $x \sim y$, transitivity implies $z \sim y$, so $z \in [y]$. Thus $[x] \subseteq [y]$. By symmetry, $y \sim x$, and the exact same transitivity argument shows $[y] \subseteq [x]$, yielding $[x] = [y]$.

For (3), suppose $[x] \cap [y] \neq \emptyset$. Then there exists some element $z \in [x] \cap [y]$. By definition, $z \sim x$ and $z \sim y$. By symmetry, $x \sim z$, and by transitivity with $z \sim y$, we obtain $x \sim y$. By part (2), this implies $[x] = [y]$. $\blacksquare$

> [!warning] Caution: The "Well-Definedness" Requirement on Quotient Sets
> Whenever we define an operation (like addition or multiplication) on equivalence classes $[x] \in X / {\sim}$ by choosing a specific representative $x$ and computing with $x$, we face a critical danger: **what if someone else picks a different representative $x'$ from the same equivalence class $[x]$?**
>
> For a proposed operation $[x] \oplus [y] := [f(x, y)]$ to be **well-defined** (meaning it actually defines a valid function on the equivalence classes themselves rather than an artifact of how we wrote them down), we must prove that whenever $[x] = [x']$ and $[y] = [y']$, the resulting outputs lie in the same equivalence class:
> $$
> x \sim x' \text{ and } y \sim y' \implies f(x, y) \sim f(x', y')
> $$
> Keep your eyes on this requirement: verifying well-definedness will be the single most important check we perform when constructing $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$.

# 0.2 The Natural Numbers

Now we are ready to build our first number system: the **natural numbers** $\mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\}$.

> [!note] Convention on Zero
> In foundational set theory and modern analysis (such as Terence Tao's *Analysis I*), $0$ is included in the natural numbers $\mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\}$, while the positive integers $\{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$ are denoted by $\mathbb{N}^+$ or $\mathbb{Z}^+$. Including $0$ from the very beginning makes recursive definitions cleaner and provides an immediate additive identity.

How should we bring $\mathbb{N}$ into existence? There are two classic perspectives:
1. **The Set-Theoretic Construction (Von Neumann Ordinals):** Build $0, 1, 2, \dots$ explicitly out of the empty set $\emptyset$ so we know with certainty that a set behaving like the natural numbers exists.
2. **The Axiomatic Interface (Peano Axioms):** Specify the five structural rules that govern counting and induction, and derive all of arithmetic from those five rules alone.

The cleanest approach is a hybrid of both: we first show briefly how John von Neumann constructed the natural numbers out of pure sets (so $\mathbb{N}$ is not an ungrounded assumption), and then we state the **Peano Axioms** as our clean working interface for proving all arithmetic and order properties of $\mathbb{N}$.

## Set-Theoretic Realization: Von Neumann Ordinals

Suppose all we have in our universe is the empty set $\emptyset$ and the ability to form new sets using unions and braces. How can we count? John von Neumann proposed a wonderfully concrete rule: define each natural number $n$ to be the **set of all previous natural numbers**, so that a set representing the number $n$ literally has $n$ elements!

> [!abstract] Definition: Von Neumann Construction of the Naturals
> Define $0$ to be the empty set, and for any set $n$, define its **successor** $S(n)$ by adjoining $n$ as an element to the set $n$ itself:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> 0 &:= \emptyset \\
> S(n) &:= n \cup \{n\}
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> Applying the successor operation repeatedly produces the individual natural numbers:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> 0 &= \emptyset \\
> 1 &= S(0) = 0 \cup \{0\} = \{\emptyset\} = \{0\} \\
> 2 &= S(1) = 1 \cup \{1\} = \{\emptyset, \{\emptyset\}\} = \{0, 1\} \\
> 3 &= S(2) = 2 \cup \{2\} = \{\emptyset, \{\emptyset\}, \{\emptyset, \{\emptyset\}\}\} = \{0, 1, 2\} \\
> &\;\;\vdots \\
> n &= \{0, 1, 2, \dots, n - 1\}
> \end{aligned}
> $$

In Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory (ZFC), the **Axiom of Infinity** guarantees the existence of at least one "inductive set"—a set $I$ that contains $\emptyset$ and contains $n \cup \{n\}$ whenever it contains $n$. Taking the intersection of all such inductive sets yields the minimal inductive set $\mathbb{N}$.

Having seen that $\mathbb{N}$, $0$, and the successor map $S: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$ can be realized concretely from the empty set, we never again need to peer inside the set-theoretic braces $\{\emptyset, \{\emptyset\}\}$. Instead, we extract the five essential properties of $(\mathbb{N}, 0, S)$ as the **Peano Axioms** and do all of our mathematics through them.

## The Peano Axioms

In 1889, Giuseppe Peano distilled the entire structure of the natural numbers into three primitive ingredients—a set $\mathbb{N}$, a starting element $0$, and a **successor function** $S: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$ (which you should think of intuitively as "the next number after $n$")—governed by five axioms.

> [!abstract] Definition: The Peano Axioms
> The **natural numbers** consist of a set $\mathbb{N}$, an element $0 \in \mathbb{N}$, and a function $S: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$ satisfying:
> 1. **Zero is a Natural Number:** $0 \in \mathbb{N}$.
> 2. **Closure under Successor:** If $n \in \mathbb{N}$, then $S(n) \in \mathbb{N}$.
> 3. **Zero is Not a Successor:** For every $n \in \mathbb{N}$, $S(n) \neq 0$.
> 4. **Injectivity of Successor:** For all $n, m \in \mathbb{N}$, if $S(n) = S(m)$, then $n = m$.
> 5. **Principle of Mathematical Induction:** If $P \subseteq \mathbb{N}$ is a subset (or $P(n)$ is a property of natural numbers) such that:
>    - **Base Case:** $0 \in P$ (i.e., $P(0)$ is true), and
>    - **Inductive Step:** For every $n \in \mathbb{N}$, if $n \in P$ then $S(n) \in P$ (i.e., $P(n) \implies P(S(n))$),
>    then $P = \mathbb{N}$ (i.e., $P(n)$ is true for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$).

Why is every single one of these five axioms necessary?
- Without **Axiom 3**, the number line could loop back onto $0$ (like a clock modulo $4$ where $S(3) = 0$).
- Without **Axiom 4**, the number line could collapse into a loop later on (for instance, $0 \to 1 \to 2 \to 1$).
- Without **Axiom 5** (Induction), the set $\mathbb{N}$ could contain "rogue" unreachable elements floating disconnected from the chain $0, S(0), S(S(0)), \dots$. Induction guarantees that $\mathbb{N}$ contains *only* the elements reachable from $0$ by applying $S$ finitely many times.

Let's immediately put Induction to work by proving a simple structural fact: every non-zero natural number is the successor of some unique predecessor.

> [!theorem] Existence and Uniqueness of Predecessors
> For every $n \in \mathbb{N}$ with $n \neq 0$, there exists a unique $m \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $S(m) = n$.

**Proof:**
Let $P(n)$ be the statement: *"either $n = 0$, or there exists $m \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $S(m) = n$."* We prove $P(n)$ holds for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$ by induction.
- **Base Case ($n = 0$):** The statement $P(0)$ is trivially true because its first clause ($0 = 0$) holds.
- **Inductive Step:** Suppose $P(n)$ holds for some $n \in \mathbb{N}$. We must show $P(S(n))$ holds. By choosing $m = n \in \mathbb{N}$, we immediately have $S(m) = S(n)$, so the second clause of $P(S(n))$ is satisfied!

By the Principle of Mathematical Induction, $P(n)$ holds for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$. Thus every $n \neq 0$ has at least one predecessor $m$ with $S(m) = n$. Uniqueness follows immediately from Peano Axiom 4: if $S(m) = n$ and $S(m') = n$, then $S(m) = S(m')$, so $m = m'$. $\blacksquare$

## Recursive Definition of Addition and Its Algebraic Laws

Notice that the Peano Axioms mention only the successor function $S(n)$—there is no $+$ or $\cdot$ symbol anywhere in the axioms! Thanks to the **Recursion Theorem** (which guarantees that any function defined at $0$ and specified at $S(m)$ in terms of its value at $m$ is uniquely determined on all of $\mathbb{N}$), we can define addition by repeatedly applying the successor operation.

> [!abstract] Definition: Addition of Natural Numbers
> Given a fixed $n \in \mathbb{N}$, we define $n + m$ for all $m \in \mathbb{N}$ recursively on the second variable $m$:
> 1. **Base Step:** $n + 0 := n$.
> 2. **Recursive Step:** $n + S(m) := S(n + m)$.

Before you jump ahead, pause and notice an asymmetry here! By definition, $n + 0 = n$ is an immediate one-line rule, and $n + S(m) = S(n + m)$ is an immediate one-line rule. However, what about $0 + n = n$ or $S(n) + m = S(n + m)$, where the change is in the *first* slot? We cannot assume addition is commutative ($n + m = m + n$) before we prove it! And to prove commutativity, we need two helper lemmas that let us move $0$ and $S$ from the left slot to the right slot.

> [!theorem] Lemma: Left-Addition of Zero and Successors
> For all $n, m \in \mathbb{N}$:
> 1. $0 + n = n$.
> 2. $S(n) + m = S(n + m)$.

**Proof:**
We prove (1) by induction on $n$.
- **Base Case ($n = 0$):** By the base step of addition, $0 + 0 = 0$.
- **Inductive Step:** Suppose $0 + n = n$. Using the recursive step of addition and the inductive hypothesis,
  $$
  0 + S(n) = S(0 + n) = S(n)
  $$
  which closes the induction for (1).

We prove (2) by fixing $n \in \mathbb{N}$ and inducting on $m$.
- **Base Case ($m = 0$):** Using the base step of addition twice,
  $$
  S(n) + 0 = S(n) = S(n + 0)
  $$
- **Inductive Step:** Suppose $S(n) + m = S(n + m)$ holds for $m$. Then for $S(m)$,
  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  S(n) + S(m) &= S(S(n) + m) && \text{(by recursive definition of }+ \text{)} \\
              &= S(S(n + m)) && \text{(by inductive hypothesis)} \\
              &= S(n + S(m)) && \text{(by recursive definition of }+ \text{)}
  \end{aligned}
  $$
Thus $S(n) + m = S(n + m)$ holds for all $n, m \in \mathbb{N}$. $\blacksquare$

With these two helper rules in hand, the fundamental algebraic laws of addition—associativity, commutativity, and cancellation—fall into place cleanly by induction.

> [!theorem] Algebraic Laws of Addition on $\mathbb{N}$
> For all $a, b, c \in \mathbb{N}$:
> 1. **Associativity:** $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$.
> 2. **Commutativity:** $a + b = b + a$.
> 3. **Cancellation Law:** If $a + c = b + c$, then $a = b$.

**Proof:**
1. **Associativity:** Fix $a, b \in \mathbb{N}$ and induct on $c$.
   - *Base Case ($c = 0$):* $(a + b) + 0 = a + b = a + (b + 0)$.
   - *Inductive Step:* Suppose $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$. Then
     $$
     (a + b) + S(c) = S((a + b) + c) = S(a + (b + c)) = a + S(b + c) = a + (b + S(c))
     $$
2. **Commutativity:** Fix $a \in \mathbb{N}$ and induct on $b$.
   - *Base Case ($b = 0$):* By definition and Part (1) of our previous lemma, $a + 0 = a = 0 + a$.
   - *Inductive Step:* Suppose $a + b = b + a$. Using the recursive definition of addition and Part (2) of our previous lemma,
     $$
     a + S(b) = S(a + b) = S(b + a) = S(b) + a
     $$
3. **Cancellation Law:** Fix $a, b \in \mathbb{N}$ and induct on $c$.
   - *Base Case ($c = 0$):* If $a + 0 = b + 0$, then $a = b$ immediately.
   - *Inductive Step:* Suppose that $a + c = b + c \implies a = b$. Now assume $a + S(c) = b + S(c)$. By the recursive definition of addition, this reads $S(a + c) = S(b + c)$. Since $S$ is injective (Peano Axiom 4), we deduce $a + c = b + c$, and by the inductive hypothesis, $a = b$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note] Why Cancellation Is Our Lifeline Before Subtraction Exists
> Notice that in $\mathbb{N}$, we cannot prove $a + c = b + c \implies a = b$ by "subtracting $c$ from both sides," because negative numbers and subtraction do not exist yet! Instead, Peano Axiom 4 (injectivity of $S$) acts as our microscopic "subtract $1$ from both sides" tool, and induction upgrades it into full additive cancellation.

## Recursive Definition of Multiplication and Its Algebraic Laws

Just as addition is repeated succession, multiplication is repeated addition.

> [!abstract] Definition: Multiplication of Natural Numbers
> Given a fixed $n \in \mathbb{N}$, we define $n \cdot m$ (also written $nm$) recursively on $m \in \mathbb{N}$:
> 1. **Base Step:** $n \cdot 0 := 0$.
> 2. **Recursive Step:** $n \cdot S(m) := (n \cdot m) + n$.

Observe that if we set $1 := S(0)$, our definitions immediately yield $n + 1 = n + S(0) = S(n + 0) = S(n)$, and $n \cdot 1 = n \cdot S(0) = n \cdot 0 + n = 0 + n = n$. Just as with addition, the recursive definition acts on the right factor first, so one proves $0 \cdot n = 0$ and $S(n) \cdot m = (n \cdot m) + m$ by a quick induction on $m$, and then establishes the full suite of multiplicative laws.

> [!theorem] Algebraic Laws of Multiplication on $\mathbb{N}$
> For all $a, b, c \in \mathbb{N}$:
> 1. **Multiplicative Identity:** $a \cdot 1 = 1 \cdot a = a$.
> 2. **Distributivity:** $a \cdot (b + c) = a \cdot b + a \cdot c$ and $(b + c) \cdot a = b \cdot a + c \cdot a$.
> 3. **Commutativity:** $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$.
> 4. **Associativity:** $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$.
> 5. **No Zero Divisors:** $a \cdot b = 0$ if and only if $a = 0$ or $b = 0$.
> 6. **Multiplicative Cancellation:** If $a \cdot c = b \cdot c$ and $c \neq 0$, then $a = b$.

**Proof:**
We prove Distributivity and No Zero Divisors in full detail, as they are the load-bearing pillars for our later constructions; the remaining inductive checks follow the exact same pattern as addition.

To prove left distributivity $a \cdot (b + c) = a \cdot b + a \cdot c$, fix $a, b \in \mathbb{N}$ and induct on $c$.
- *Base Case ($c = 0$):* $a \cdot (b + 0) = a \cdot b = a \cdot b + 0 = a \cdot b + a \cdot 0$.
- *Inductive Step:* Suppose $a \cdot (b + c) = a \cdot b + a \cdot c$. Using the recursive definitions of addition and multiplication along with associativity of addition:
  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  a \cdot (b + S(c)) &= a \cdot S(b + c) \\
                     &= a \cdot (b + c) + a \\
                     &= (a \cdot b + a \cdot c) + a \\
                     &= a \cdot b + (a \cdot c + a) \\
                     &= a \cdot b + a \cdot S(c)
  \end{aligned}
  $$
Commutativity ($ab = ba$) then follows by induction on $b$ using $0 \cdot a = 0$ and $S(b) \cdot a = ba + a$, and associativity follows by induction on $c$ using distributivity in the inductive step:
$$
(ab) S(c) = (ab)c + ab = a(bc) + ab = a(bc + b) = a(b S(c))
$$

Finally, to prove **No Zero Divisors**, suppose $a \neq 0$ and $b \neq 0$. By the Existence of Predecessors theorem, we can write $a = S(n)$ and $b = S(m)$ for some $n, m \in \mathbb{N}$. Then
$$
a \cdot b = S(n) \cdot S(m) = S(n) \cdot m + S(n) = S(S(n) \cdot m + n)
$$
By Peano Axiom 3, no successor can equal $0$, so $a \cdot b \neq 0$. Taking the contrapositive shows that $a \cdot b = 0 \implies a = 0 \text{ or } b = 0$. $\blacksquare$

## Order on the Natural Numbers

How do we define $a \le b$ when all we have is addition of natural numbers? Notice that in $\mathbb{N}$, there are no negative numbers. Thus, $b$ is at least as large as $a$ precisely when you can get from $a$ to $b$ by adding some natural number $k \in \mathbb{N}$.

> [!abstract] Definition: Order on $\mathbb{N}$
> Let $a, b \in \mathbb{N}$. We say that $a$ is **less than or equal to** $b$, written $a \le b$ (or $b \ge a$), if there exists some $k \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $a + k = b$.
>
> We say that $a$ is **strictly less than** $b$, written $a < b$ (or $b > a$), if $a \le b$ and $a \neq b$ (equivalently, if $a + k = b$ for some non-zero $k = S(m) \in \mathbb{N}$).

Let's verify that this algebraic definition behaves exactly like the familiar number line order.

> [!theorem] Fundamental Order Properties of $\mathbb{N}$
> For all $a, b, c \in \mathbb{N}$:
> 1. **Partial Order:**
>    - *Reflexivity:* $a \le a$.
>    - *Antisymmetry:* If $a \le b$ and $b \le a$, then $a = b$.
>    - *Transitivity:* If $a \le b$ and $b \le c$, then $a \le c$.
> 2. **Trichotomy Law:** Exactly one of the following three relations holds: $a < b$, $a = b$, or $a > b$.
> 3. **Order Compatibility with Arithmetic:**
>    - $a < b \iff a + c < b + c$.
>    - If $c \neq 0$, then $a < b \iff a \cdot c < b \cdot c$.
> 4. **Well-Ordering Principle:** Every non-empty subset $E \subseteq \mathbb{N}$ has a **least element** (a minimum), i.e., an element $m \in E$ such that $m \le x$ for all $x \in E$.

**Proof:**
1. **Partial Order:** Reflexivity holds since $a + 0 = a$. For transitivity, if $a + k = b$ and $b + l = c$, then $a + (k + l) = c$, so $a \le c$. For antisymmetry, suppose $a + k = b$ and $b + l = a$ for some $k, l \in \mathbb{N}$. Substituting the first into the second gives
   $$
   a + (k + l) = (a + k) + l = b + l = a = a + 0
   $$
   By the additive cancellation law, $k + l = 0$. If $l \neq 0$, then $l = S(m)$, so $k + l = S(k + m) \neq 0$, a contradiction. Thus $l = 0$ and $k = 0$, which means $a = b$.
2. **Trichotomy:** Fix $a \in \mathbb{N}$ and induct on $b$.
   - *Base Case ($b = 0$):* Since $0 + a = a$, we have $0 \le a$, so either $a = 0$ or $0 < a$.
   - *Inductive Step:* Suppose trichotomy holds between $a$ and $b$. If $a \le b$, then $a + k = b$, so $a + S(k) = S(b)$ with $S(k) \neq 0$, giving $a < S(b)$. On the other hand, if $b < a$, then $b + S(k) = a$ for some $k \in \mathbb{N}$. Then $S(b) + k = S(b + k) = a$. If $k = 0$, we get $a = S(b)$; if $k \neq 0$, we get $S(b) < a$. This proves at least one relation holds; antisymmetry ensures at most one can hold.
3. **Compatibility:** If $a < b$, then $a + k = b$ for some $k \neq 0$. Adding $c$ gives $(a + c) + k = b + c$, so $a + c < b + c$ (and conversely by cancellation). Similarly, multiplying by $c \neq 0$ gives $ac + kc = bc$ where $kc \neq 0$ (since $\mathbb{N}$ has no zero divisors), so $ac < bc$. Note how this immediately implies Multiplicative Cancellation: if $a \neq b$, trichotomy gives $a < b$ or $b < a$, so $ac < bc$ or $bc < ac$, proving $ac = bc \implies a = b$.
4. **Well-Ordering Principle:** Suppose for contradiction that $E \subseteq \mathbb{N}$ is a subset with *no* least element; we will show $E = \emptyset$. Let $P(n)$ be the statement: *"for all $k \le n$, we have $k \notin E$."*
   - *Base Case ($n = 0$):* Could $0 \in E$? Since $0 \le x$ for every $x \in \mathbb{N}$, if $0$ were in $E$, then $0$ would be the least element of $E$, contradicting our assumption. Thus $0 \notin E$, and $P(0)$ holds.
   - *Inductive Step:* Suppose $P(n)$ holds, meaning no number $k \le n$ lies in $E$. Could $S(n) \in E$? Since no element of $E$ is $\le n$, every $x \in E$ must satisfy $x \ge S(n)$ by trichotomy. Thus, if $S(n)$ belonged to $E$, it would be the least element of $E$—again a contradiction! Hence $S(n) \notin E$, so $P(S(n))$ holds.
   By induction, $n \notin E$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$, which means $E = \emptyset$. $\blacksquare$

# 0.3 Constructing the Integers from the Naturals

## Why Extend the Natural Numbers?

The natural numbers $(\mathbb{N}, +, \cdot, \le)$ form a remarkably clean algebraic system, but they suffer from an obvious algebraic defect: **you cannot always subtract**. Given $a, b \in \mathbb{N}$, the linear equation
$$
b + x = a
$$
has a solution $x \in \mathbb{N}$ if and only if $b \le a$. When $a < b$—say, $5 + x = 2$—no natural number $x$ satisfies the equation.

To fix this defect, we want to enlarge $\mathbb{N}$ to the set of **integers** $\mathbb{Z}$ by adjoining formal differences $a - b$ for *all* pairs of natural numbers $a, b \in \mathbb{N}$.

## Equivalence Classes of Formal Differences

Wait: how can we define an integer as $a - b$ when the minus sign "$-$" does not exist yet? Instead of writing $a - b$, we represent this intended difference by the **ordered pair** $(a, b) \in \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$. Think of $(a, b)$ informally as "having $a$ dollars in cash and $b$ dollars in debt."

Of course, many different pairs represent the exact same net balance: having $\$5$ cash and $\$2$ debt is the same net worth as having $\$3$ cash and $\$0$ debt, and having $\$2$ cash and $\$5$ debt is the same as having $\$0$ cash and $\$3$ debt. In the world where subtraction already exists, we would say
$$
a - b = c - d \iff a + d = b + c
$$
Look closely at the right-hand side: $a + d = b + c$ uses **only addition of natural numbers**, which we have already constructed and proved rigorous! That gives us our equivalence relation on $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$.

> [!abstract] Definition: The Equivalence Relation for Integers
> Define a binary relation $\sim$ on $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ by declaring that for any $(a, b), (c, d) \in \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$:
> $$
> (a, b) \sim (c, d) \iff a + d = b + c
> $$
> The set of **integers**, denoted $\mathbb{Z}$, is the quotient set of $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ by this equivalence relation:
> $$
> \mathbb{Z} := (\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}) / {\sim}
> $$
> We write $[a, b]$ for the equivalence class $[(a, b)] \in \mathbb{Z}$ of the pair $(a, b)$.

Before we can call $\mathbb{Z}$ a quotient set, we must verify that $\sim$ actually is an equivalence relation.

> [!theorem] Equivalence of Formal Differences
> The relation $\sim$ on $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ defined by $(a, b) \sim (c, d) \iff a + d = b + c$ is an equivalence relation.

**Proof:**
Let $(a, b), (c, d), (e, f) \in \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$.
1. **Reflexivity:** By commutativity of addition in $\mathbb{N}$, $a + b = b + a$, so $(a, b) \sim (a, b)$.
2. **Symmetry:** Suppose $(a, b) \sim (c, d)$, so $a + d = b + c$. Using commutativity and symmetry of equality in $\mathbb{N}$, we get $c + b = d + a$, which means $(c, d) \sim (a, b)$.
3. **Transitivity:** Suppose $(a, b) \sim (c, d)$ and $(c, d) \sim (e, f)$. Then
   $$
   a + d = b + c \quad \text{and} \quad c + f = d + e
   $$
   Adding the second equation to the first and rearranging using associativity and commutativity of addition in $\mathbb{N}$ gives:
   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   (a + d) + (c + f) &= (b + c) + (d + e) \\
   (a + f) + (c + d) &= (b + e) + (c + d)
   \end{aligned}
   $$
   By the **Cancellation Law** of addition in $\mathbb{N}$, we can cancel $(c + d)$ from both sides to obtain $a + f = b + e$, which means $(a, b) \sim (e, f)$. $\blacksquare$

## Integer Arithmetic and Well-Definedness

How should we add and multiply two equivalence classes $[a, b]$ and $[c, d]$? To discover the right formulas, we do a quick scratchpad calculation pretending $[a, b]$ is $a - b$ and $[c, d]$ is $c - d$:
- **Intended Sum:** $(a - b) + (c - d) = (a + c) - (b + d)$, which corresponds to the pair $(a + c, b + d)$.
- **Intended Product:** $(a - b)(c - d) = (ac + bd) - (ad + bc)$, which corresponds to the pair $(ac + bd, ad + bc)$.

Notice again that the right-hand pairs $(a + c, b + d)$ and $(ac + bd, ad + bc)$ involve only operations in $\mathbb{N}$!

> [!abstract] Definition: Addition and Multiplication on $\mathbb{Z}$
> For any two integers $[a, b], [c, d] \in \mathbb{Z}$, we define their **sum** and **product** by:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> [a, b] + [c, d] &:= [a + c, b + d] \\
> [a, b] \cdot [c, d] &:= [ac + bd, ad + bc]
> \end{aligned}
> $$

Now comes the essential test we highlighted in Section 0.1: since these definitions pick specific representatives $(a, b)$ and $(c, d)$ out of their equivalence classes, we *must* prove that the resulting equivalence classes do not change if we swap in equivalent representatives $(a', b') \sim (a, b)$ and $(c', d') \sim (c, d)$.

> [!theorem] Well-Definedness of Integer Arithmetic
> Addition and multiplication on $\mathbb{Z}$ are well-defined: if $[a, b] = [a', b']$ and $[c, d] = [c', d']$, then
> $$
> [a + c, b + d] = [a' + c', b' + d'] \quad \text{and} \quad [ac + bd, ad + bc] = [a'c' + b'd', a'd' + b'c']
> $$

**Proof:**
Suppose $(a, b) \sim (a', b')$ and $(c, d) \sim (c', d')$. By definition of $\sim$, we have
$$
a + b' = b + a' \quad \text{and} \quad c + d' = d + c'
$$
**1. Well-Definedness of Addition:**
Adding these two equations in $\mathbb{N}$ and rearranging by associativity and commutativity yields
$$
(a + c) + (b' + d') = (a + b') + (c + d') = (b + a') + (d + c') = (b + d) + (a' + c')
$$
By definition of $\sim$, this says $(a + c, b + d) \sim (a' + c', b' + d')$, so $[a + c, b + d] = [a' + c', b' + d']$.

**2. Well-Definedness of Multiplication:**
By transitivity, it suffices to show that replacing $(a, b)$ by $(a', b')$ while keeping $(c, d)$ fixed leaves the product class unchanged (replacing $(c, d)$ by $(c', d')$ then follows by symmetry). We want to show $(ac + bd, ad + bc) \sim (a'c + b'd, a'd + b'c)$, which means showing
$$
(ac + bd) + (a'd + b'c) = (ad + bc) + (a'c + b'd)
$$
Using distributivity and commutativity in $\mathbb{N}$, along with the hypothesis $a + b' = b + a'$, we compute:
$$
\begin{aligned}
(ac + bd) + (a'd + b'c) &= (a + b')c + (b + a')d \\
                        &= (b + a')c + (a + b')d \\
                        &= (bc + a'c) + (ad + b'd) \\
                        &= (ad + bc) + (a'c + b'd)
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus $(ac + bd, ad + bc) \sim (a'c + b'd, a'd + b'c)$, proving multiplication is well-defined. $\blacksquare$

What algebraic structure have we just unlocked on $\mathbb{Z}$? Unlike $\mathbb{N}$, every element of $\mathbb{Z}$ now has an **additive inverse**! Simply flipping the coordinates of $[a, b]$ to $[b, a]$ reverses the balance of cash and debt:
$$
[a, b] + [b, a] = [a + b, b + a] = [0, 0]
$$
because $(a + b) + 0 = (b + a) + 0$. In abstract algebra, a set equipped with addition and multiplication satisfying the properties below is called a **commutative ring**, and because $\mathbb{Z}$ also has no zero divisors, it is called an **integral domain**.

> [!theorem] Algebraic Structure of $\mathbb{Z}$ (Commutative Ring and Integral Domain)
> Define $0_{\mathbb{Z}} := [0, 0]$, $1_{\mathbb{Z}} := [1, 0]$, and for each $x = [a, b] \in \mathbb{Z}$, define its **negation** (additive inverse) by $-x := [b, a]$. Then for all $x, y, z \in \mathbb{Z}$:
> 1. **Additive Abelian Group:**
>    - *Associativity:* $(x + y) + z = x + (y + z)$.
>    - *Commutativity:* $x + y = y + x$.
>    - *Identity:* $x + 0_{\mathbb{Z}} = x$.
>    - *Inverses:* $x + (-x) = 0_{\mathbb{Z}}$.
> 2. **Multiplicative Commutative Monoid & Distributivity:**
>    - *Associativity:* $(x \cdot y) \cdot z = x \cdot (y \cdot z)$.
>    - *Commutativity:* $x \cdot y = y \cdot x$.
>    - *Identity:* $x \cdot 1_{\mathbb{Z}} = x$.
>    - *Distributivity:* $x \cdot (y + z) = x \cdot y + x \cdot z$.
> 3. **No Zero Divisors (Integral Domain):** If $x \cdot y = 0_{\mathbb{Z}}$, then $x = 0_{\mathbb{Z}}$ or $y = 0_{\mathbb{Z}}$.

**Proof:**
Associativity, commutativity, and distributivity follow directly by writing $x = [a, b]$, $y = [c, d]$, $z = [e, f]$, applying the definitions of $+$ and $\cdot$ on $\mathbb{Z}$, and invoking the corresponding laws of $\mathbb{N}$. For the identities and inverse:
- $x + 0_{\mathbb{Z}} = [a, b] + [0, 0] = [a + 0, b + 0] = [a, b] = x$.
- $x + (-x) = [a, b] + [b, a] = [a + b, b + a] = [0, 0] = 0_{\mathbb{Z}}$ since $(a + b) + 0 = (b + a) + 0$.
- $x \cdot 1_{\mathbb{Z}} = [a, b] \cdot [1, 0] = [a \cdot 1 + b \cdot 0, a \cdot 0 + b \cdot 1] = [a, b] = x$.

We prove (3) (**No Zero Divisors**) after establishing the canonical form of integers in the next subsection, as trichotomy makes it effortless. $\blacksquare$

> [!note] Subtraction Defined
> Once every $y \in \mathbb{Z}$ has a unique additive inverse $-y$, **subtraction** is no longer a separate primitive operation—it is simply defined as adding the additive inverse:
> $$
> x - y := x + (-y)
> $$
> Moreover, for any $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$, the equation $b + x = a$ now has the unique solution $x = a + (-b) = a - b$ in $\mathbb{Z}$!

## Embedding the Naturals and Ordering the Integers

Strictly speaking, an element of $\mathbb{N}$ (like $3$) and an element of $\mathbb{Z}$ (an equivalence class $[3, 0]$ of pairs of naturals) are different set-theoretic objects. How are we justified in thinking of $\mathbb{N}$ as a subset of $\mathbb{Z}$?

We connect them via a **canonical embedding** $\iota: \mathbb{N} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Z}$ defined by
$$
\iota(n) := [n, 0]
$$
Let's check that $\iota$ is injective and preserves all arithmetic operations:
- **Injectivity:** $\iota(n) = \iota(m) \iff [n, 0] = [m, 0] \iff n + 0 = 0 + m \iff n = m$.
- **Preserves Addition:** $\iota(n) + \iota(m) = [n, 0] + [m, 0] = [n + m, 0] = \iota(n + m)$.
- **Preserves Multiplication:** $\iota(n) \cdot \iota(m) = [n, 0] \cdot [m, 0] = [nm + 0, 0 + 0] = [nm, 0] = \iota(nm)$.

Furthermore, by the Trichotomy Law on $\mathbb{N}$, for any integer $x = [a, b] \in \mathbb{Z}$, exactly one of three cases holds for the pair $(a, b)$:
1. $a > b$: Then $a = b + n$ for a unique non-zero $n \in \mathbb{N}$, so $(a, b) \sim (n, 0)$, meaning $x = [n, 0] = \iota(n)$ is a **positive integer**.
2. $a = b$: Then $(a, b) \sim (0, 0)$, meaning $x = [0, 0] = 0_{\mathbb{Z}}$ is **zero**.
3. $a < b$: Then $b = a + n$ for a unique non-zero $n \in \mathbb{N}$, so $(a, b) \sim (0, n)$, meaning $x = [0, n] = -[n, 0] = -\iota(n)$ is a **negative integer**.

Because $\iota$ is a structure-preserving copy of $\mathbb{N}$ inside $\mathbb{Z}$, we henceforth identify each $n \in \mathbb{N}$ with $[n, 0] \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $-n$ with $[0, n] \in \mathbb{Z}$. Notice how this three-way classification immediately finishes the proof that **$\mathbb{Z}$ has no zero divisors**: if $x \neq 0$ and $y \neq 0$, then $x = \pm n$ and $y = \pm m$ for non-zero $n, m \in \mathbb{N}$, so $xy = \pm(nm) \neq 0$ because $nm \neq 0$ in $\mathbb{N}$!

> [!abstract] Definition: Order on $\mathbb{Z}$
> Let $x = [a, b]$ and $y = [c, d]$ be integers. We define $x \le y$ if and only if $a + d \le b + c$ in $\mathbb{N}$, and $x < y$ if and only if $a + d < b + c$ in $\mathbb{N}$ (equivalently, $y - x = [k, 0]$ for some non-zero $k \in \mathbb{N}$).

Because $y - x$ is well-defined on $\mathbb{Z}$, the order relation $<$ is automatically well-defined and satisfies the **Trichotomy Law** (for any $x, y \in \mathbb{Z}$, applying the three-way classification to $y - x$ shows that either $y - x > 0$, $y - x = 0$, or $y - x < 0$), **Transitivity**, **Additive Compatibility** ($x < y \implies x + z < y + z$), and **Multiplicative Compatibility with Positives** ($x < y \text{ and } z > 0 \implies xz < yz$).

# 0.4 Constructing the Rational Numbers from the Integers

## Why Extend the Integers?

Passing from $\mathbb{N}$ to $\mathbb{Z}$ cured the impossibility of subtraction, giving us a commutative ring where every equation $b + x = a$ is solvable. Yet $\mathbb{Z}$ still suffers from a second algebraic limitation: **you cannot always divide**. Given integers $p, q \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $q \neq 0$, the linear equation
$$
q \cdot x = p
$$
rarely has a solution $x \in \mathbb{Z}$ (for instance, $2x = 1$ has no integer solution). Only $1$ and $-1$ have multiplicative inverses in $\mathbb{Z}$.

To cure this defect, we apply the exact same philosophical blueprint that took us from $\mathbb{N}$ to $\mathbb{Z}$: we represent an intended fraction $p / q$ as an **ordered pair** $(p, q)$ of integers with $q \neq 0$, and we glue together pairs that represent the same ratio using an equivalence relation.

## Equivalence Classes of Formal Fractions

When do two fractions $p / q$ and $r / s$ represent the same number? When cross-multiplying produces equal integers:
$$
\frac{p}{q} = \frac{r}{s} \iff p \cdot s = q \cdot r
$$
Notice that the equation $ps = qr$ uses **only multiplication of integers**, which we have already constructed! Let $\mathbb{Z}^* := \mathbb{Z} \setminus \{0\}$ denote the set of non-zero integers.

> [!abstract] Definition: The Equivalence Relation for Rationals
> Define a binary relation $\sim$ on $\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}^*$ by declaring that for any $(p, q), (r, s) \in \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}^*$:
> $$
> (p, q) \sim (r, s) \iff p \cdot s = q \cdot r
> $$
> The set of **rational numbers**, denoted $\mathbb{Q}$, is the quotient set
> $$
> \mathbb{Q} := (\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}^*) / {\sim}
> $$
> We denote the equivalence class $[(p, q)] \in \mathbb{Q}$ either by $[p, q]$ or by the familiar fraction notation $\frac{p}{q}$ (or $p/q$).

Let's verify that $\sim$ is an equivalence relation—and pay close attention to why $q \neq 0$ and the absence of zero divisors in $\mathbb{Z}$ are indispensable in the proof of transitivity!

> [!theorem] Equivalence of Formal Fractions
> The relation $\sim$ on $\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}^*$ defined by $(p, q) \sim (r, s) \iff ps = qr$ is an equivalence relation.

**Proof:**
Let $(p, q), (r, s), (t, u) \in \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}^*$. Note that $q \neq 0$, $s \neq 0$, and $u \neq 0$.
1. **Reflexivity:** By commutativity of multiplication in $\mathbb{Z}$, $pq = qp$, so $(p, q) \sim (p, q)$.
2. **Symmetry:** If $(p, q) \sim (r, s)$, then $ps = qr$, so $rq = sp$, which means $(r, s) \sim (p, q)$.
3. **Transitivity:** Suppose $(p, q) \sim (r, s)$ and $(r, s) \sim (t, u)$. Then
   $$
   ps = qr \quad \text{and} \quad ru = st
   $$
   Multiplying the first equation by $u$ and using $ru = st$ alongside associativity and commutativity in $\mathbb{Z}$, we obtain:
   $$
   (pu)s = (ps)u = (qr)u = q(ru) = q(st) = (qt)s
   $$
   Subtracting $(qt)s$ from both sides gives $(pu - qt)s = 0$. Now here is the crucial step: because $(r, s) \in \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}^*$, we know $s \neq 0$, and because $\mathbb{Z}$ has **no zero divisors**, $(pu - qt)s = 0$ forces $pu - qt = 0$, or $pu = qt$. Thus $(p, q) \sim (t, u)$. $\blacksquare$

> [!warning] Caution: Why Zero Cannot Be a Denominator
> Look at what would happen in the transitivity proof if we allowed $(0, 0)$ into our set of pairs! Since $p \cdot 0 = q \cdot 0$ is always $0 = 0$, *every* fraction $(p, q)$ would be equivalent to $(0, 0)$, and by transitivity every fraction $(p, q)$ would be equivalent to every other fraction $(t, u)$—collapsing the entire rational number system into a single point! Excluding $0$ from the second coordinate and using the fact that $\mathbb{Z}$ has no zero divisors prevents this collapse.

## Rational Arithmetic and Field Structure

How do we add and multiply fractions? From elementary arithmetic, we know that finding a common denominator for $\frac{p}{q} + \frac{r}{s}$ gives $\frac{ps + qr}{qs}$, and multiplying gives $\frac{pr}{qs}$. Notice first that because $q \neq 0$ and $s \neq 0$, the no-zero-divisors property of $\mathbb{Z}$ guarantees $qs \neq 0$, so $(ps + qr, qs)$ and $(pr, qs)$ legitimately belong to $\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}^*$.

> [!abstract] Definition: Addition and Multiplication on $\mathbb{Q}$
> For any two rational numbers $[p, q], [r, s] \in \mathbb{Q}$, we define their **sum** and **product** by:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> [p, q] + [r, s] &:= [ps + qr, qs] \\
> [p, q] \cdot [r, s] &:= [pr, qs]
> \end{aligned}
> $$

Once again, we must prove that these operations do not depend on the choice of representatives.

> [!theorem] Well-Definedness of Rational Arithmetic
> Addition and multiplication on $\mathbb{Q}$ are well-defined: if $[p, q] = [p', q']$ and $[r, s] = [r', s']$, then
> $$
> [ps + qr, qs] = [p's' + q'r', q's'] \quad \text{and} \quad [pr, qs] = [p'r', q's']
> $$

**Proof:**
Suppose $(p, q) \sim (p', q')$ and $(r, s) \sim (r', s')$, so that
$$
pq' = qp' \quad \text{and} \quad rs' = sr'
$$
**1. Well-Definedness of Addition:**
We must show $(ps + qr)q's' = qs(p's' + q'r')$. Expanding the left-hand side using distributivity, associativity, and commutativity in $\mathbb{Z}$, and substituting $pq' = qp'$ and $rs' = sr'$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
(ps + qr)q's' &= (pq')(ss') + (qq')(rs') \\
              &= (qp')(ss') + (qq')(sr') \\
              &= qs(p's' + q'r')
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus $(ps + qr, qs) \sim (p's' + q'r', q's')$.

**2. Well-Definedness of Multiplication:**
We must show $(pr)(q's') = (qs)(p'r')$. Using associativity and commutativity in $\mathbb{Z}$:
$$
(pr)(q's') = (pq')(rs') = (qp')(sr') = (qs)(p'r')
$$
Thus $(pr, qs) \sim (p'r', q's')$, completing the proof. $\blacksquare$

What about embedding $\mathbb{Z}$ into $\mathbb{Q}$? The map $\iota: \mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Q}$ given by $\iota(n) := [n, 1] = \frac{n}{1}$ is injective ($[n, 1] = [m, 1] \iff n \cdot 1 = 1 \cdot m \iff n = m$) and preserves both addition ($[n, 1] + [m, 1] = [n + m, 1]$) and multiplication ($[n, 1] \cdot [m, 1] = [nm, 1]$). Thus $\mathbb{Z}$ sits canonically inside $\mathbb{Q}$ as the fractions with denominator $1$.

Most importantly, every non-zero rational number $x = [p, q] \neq [0, 1]$ (meaning $p \neq 0$) now has a **multiplicative inverse** (reciprocal) $x^{-1} := [q, p] \in \mathbb{Q}$, since $p \neq 0$ allows $p$ to sit in the denominator, and
$$
[p, q] \cdot [q, p] = [pq, qp] = [1, 1] = 1_{\mathbb{Q}}
$$

## Ordered Fields and Positivity

With the construction of $\mathbb{Q}$, we have reached our first example of a **field**—and when equipped with its natural order, an **ordered field**. Because both $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{R}$ are ordered fields, it is immensely clarifyng to state the axioms of an ordered field in general so that any consequence we prove holds simultaneously for $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{R}$.

> [!abstract] Definition: Field
> A **field** is a set $F$ equipped with two binary operations $+: F \times F \to F$ (addition) and $\cdot: F \times F \to F$ (multiplication) satisfying the following axioms:
> 1. **Additive Axioms:**
>    - *(A1) Associativity:* $(x + y) + z = x + (y + z)$ for all $x, y, z \in F$.
>    - *(A2) Commutativity:* $x + y = y + x$ for all $x, y \in F$.
>    - *(A3) Additive Identity:* There exists an element $0 \in F$ such that $x + 0 = x$ for all $x \in F$.
>    - *(A4) Additive Inverses:* For every $x \in F$, there exists an element $-x \in F$ such that $x + (-x) = 0$.
> 2. **Multiplicative Axioms:**
>    - *(M1) Associativity:* $(x \cdot y) \cdot z = x \cdot (y \cdot z)$ for all $x, y, z \in F$.
>    - *(M2) Commutativity:* $x \cdot y = y \cdot x$ for all $x, y \in F$.
>    - *(M3) Multiplicative Identity:* There exists an element $1 \in F$ with $1 \neq 0$ such that $x \cdot 1 = x$ for all $x \in F$.
>    - *(M4) Multiplicative Inverses:* For every $x \in F$ with $x \neq 0$, there exists an element $x^{-1} \in F$ such that $x \cdot x^{-1} = 1$.
> 3. **Distributive Law:**
>    - *(D) Distributivity:* $x \cdot (y + z) = x \cdot y + x \cdot z$ for all $x, y, z \in F$.

> [!abstract] Definition: Ordered Field
> An **ordered field** is a field $F$ equipped with a relation $<$ satisfying:
> 1. *(O1) Trichotomy:* For every $x, y \in F$, exactly one of $x < y$, $x = y$, or $y < x$ holds.
> 2. *(O2) Transitivity:* For all $x, y, z \in F$, if $x < y$ and $y < z$, then $x < z$.
> 3. *(O3) Additive Preservation:* For all $x, y, z \in F$, if $x < y$, then $x + z < y + z$.
> 4. *(O4) Multiplicative Preservation:* For all $x, y \in F$, if $x > 0$ and $y > 0$, then $x \cdot y > 0$.

How do we define the order $<$ on $\mathbb{Q}$? Notice that every rational number $[p, q] \in \mathbb{Q}$ can be written with a **positive denominator** $q > 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ (since if $q < 0$, we have $[p, q] = [-p, -q]$ where $-q > 0$). We say that a rational number $x = [p, q]$ (with $q > 0$) is **positive** ($x > 0$) if $p > 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ (or equivalently, for arbitrary denominator sign, if $p \cdot q > 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}$). We then define $x < y \iff y - x > 0$.

> [!theorem] $\mathbb{Q}$ Is an Ordered Field, and Elementary Consequences of Ordered Field Axioms
> Equipped with $+, \cdot, <$ defined above, $\mathbb{Q}$ is an ordered field. Moreover, in *any* ordered field $F$, the following properties hold for all $x, y, z \in F$:
> 1. If $x > 0$, then $-x < 0$, and vice versa.
> 2. If $x < y$ and $z < 0$, then $xz > yz$ (multiplying by a negative reverses inequalities).
> 3. For every $x \neq 0$, $x^2 > 0$. In particular, $1 > 0$.
> 4. If $0 < x < y$, then $0 < y^{-1} < x^{-1}$.

**Proof:**
The field axioms for $\mathbb{Q}$ follow immediately from the ring axioms of $\mathbb{Z}$ and our verification that $[q, p]$ is the multiplicative inverse of $[p, q] \neq 0$. Positivity on $\mathbb{Q}$ is well-defined because if $[p, q] = [p', q']$ with $q, q' > 0$, then $pq' = qp'$ with $q, q' > 0$, so $p > 0 \iff p' > 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}$; properties (O1)–(O4) then inherit directly from the order properties of $\mathbb{Z}$.

Now let $F$ be any ordered field:
1. If $x > 0$, adding $-x$ to both sides of $0 < x$ via (O3) yields $-x = 0 + (-x) < x + (-x) = 0$.
2. If $x < y$ and $z < 0$, then $y - x > 0$ and $-z > 0$. By (O4), $(y - x)(-z) > 0$, which expands to $xz - yz > 0$, so $xz > yz$.
3. By trichotomy, if $x \neq 0$, either $x > 0$ or $x < 0$. If $x > 0$, (O4) gives $x^2 = x \cdot x > 0$. If $x < 0$, then $-x > 0$, so $x^2 = (-x)(-x) > 0$. Since $1 \neq 0$ and $1 = 1^2$, we have $1 > 0$.
4. Suppose $0 < x < y$. First, could $x^{-1} \le 0$? If $x^{-1} < 0$, then $1 = x \cdot x^{-1} < 0$, contradicting $1 > 0$. Thus $x^{-1} > 0$ and similarly $y^{-1} > 0$, so $x^{-1} y^{-1} > 0$. Multiplying $x < y$ by $x^{-1} y^{-1} > 0$ gives $y^{-1} < x^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$

## The Incompleteness of the Rationals

At this point, we have built $\mathbb{Q}$, an ordered field where we can add, subtract, multiply, and divide by any non-zero number. Even better, between any two rational numbers $r < s$, there is always another rational number—namely their midpoint $\frac{r + s}{2}$—so the rationals are densely packed along the number line. Why isn't $\mathbb{Q}$ good enough for doing calculus and analysis?

Because despite being densely packed, **$\mathbb{Q}$ is riddled with microscopic "holes"**! The ancient Pythagoreans discovered the most famous hole: if you draw a unit square ($1 \times 1$), its diagonal has length $x$ satisfying $x^2 = 1^2 + 1^2 = 2$, yet *no rational number* has a square equal to $2$.

> [!theorem] Irrationality of $\sqrt{2}$ (No Rational Square Equals $2$)
> There is no rational number $x \in \mathbb{Q}$ such that $x^2 = 2$.

**Proof:**
Suppose for the sake of contradiction that there exists $x \in \mathbb{Q}$ with $x^2 = 2$. By taking $|x|$ and canceling any common factors between numerator and denominator (which must terminate by the Well-Ordering Principle on $\mathbb{N}$), we can write $x = p / q$ where $p, q \in \mathbb{N}^+$ have **no common factors** (in particular, $p$ and $q$ are not both even).

Squaring $p / q = x$ gives $p^2 / q^2 = 2$, or
$$
p^2 = 2q^2
$$
This implies $p^2$ is even (divisible by $2$). Could $p$ be odd? If $p = 2k + 1$, then $p^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1$ is odd, which is impossible. Thus $p$ itself must be even, so we can write $p = 2m$ for some $m \in \mathbb{N}^+$. Substituting $p = 2m$ back into $p^2 = 2q^2$ yields
$$
4m^2 = 2q^2 \implies q^2 = 2m^2
$$
By the exact same reasoning, $q^2$ is even, which forces $q$ to be even! Thus both $p$ and $q$ are even, contradicting our choice of $p$ and $q$ in lowest terms. $\blacksquare$

To understand what a "hole" in an ordered set looks like *intrinsically* (without referring to numbers outside the set), we need the concepts of **upper bounds** and the **least upper bound (supremum)**.

> [!abstract] Definition: Boundedness, Supremum, Infimum, and the Least Upper Bound Property
> Let $(S, <)$ be an ordered set and let $E \subseteq S$ be a subset.
> 1. **Upper Bound:** We say $E$ is **bounded above** if there exists an element $\beta \in S$ such that $x \le \beta$ for all $x \in E$. Any such $\beta$ is called an **upper bound** of $E$.
> 2. **Supremum (Least Upper Bound):** An element $\alpha \in S$ is called the **least upper bound** or **supremum** of $E$, written $\alpha = \sup E$, if:
>    - $\alpha$ is an upper bound of $E$ ($x \le \alpha$ for all $x \in E$), and
>    - If $\gamma < \alpha$, then $\gamma$ is *not* an upper bound of $E$ (meaning $\alpha \le \beta$ for every upper bound $\beta$ of $E$).
> 3. **Lower Bound and Infimum (Greatest Lower Bound):** Similarly, $E$ is **bounded below** if there exists $\beta \in S$ with $\beta \le x$ for all $x \in E$, and the greatest such lower bound (if it exists) is called the **infimum** of $E$, written $\inf E$.
> 4. **Least Upper Bound Property (Dedekind Completeness):** An ordered set $S$ is said to have the **Least Upper Bound Property** if *every* non-empty subset $E \subseteq S$ that is bounded above has a supremum $\sup E \in S$.

Now we can see the exact analytical catastrophe in $\mathbb{Q}$: we can construct a non-empty subset of $\mathbb{Q}$ that is bounded above, yet has **no supremum in $\mathbb{Q}$** because its supremum *wants* to land right on the $\sqrt{2}$ hole!

> [!theorem] Failure of the Least Upper Bound Property in $\mathbb{Q}$
> Consider the subsets of positive rationals:
> $$
> A := \{p \in \mathbb{Q} : p > 0 \text{ and } p^2 < 2\}, \qquad B := \{p \in \mathbb{Q} : p > 0 \text{ and } p^2 > 2\}
> $$
> Then $A$ is non-empty and bounded above in $\mathbb{Q}$, but $A$ has **no largest element**, $B$ has **no smallest element**, and $A$ has **no supremum in $\mathbb{Q}$**.

**Proof:**
Clearly $1 \in A$ (since $1^2 = 1 < 2$), so $A$ is non-empty, and every element of $B$ (such as $2 \in B$) is an upper bound for $A$ because $p \in A, q \in B \implies p^2 < 2 < q^2 \implies p < q$.

To show that $A$ has no largest element and $B$ has no smallest element, we construct a recipe that associates to every positive rational $p \in \mathbb{Q}$ a *better* rational approximation $q \in \mathbb{Q}$ to $\sqrt{2}$:
$$
q := p - \frac{p^2 - 2}{p + 2} = \frac{2p + 2}{p + 2}
$$
Notice first that since $p > 0$, we immediately have $q > 0$. Moreover, let us compute $q^2 - 2$:
$$
q^2 - 2 = \left(\frac{2p + 2}{p + 2}\right)^2 - 2 = \frac{4p^2 + 8p + 4 - 2(p^2 + 4p + 4)}{(p + 2)^2} = \frac{2(p^2 - 2)}{(p + 2)^2}
$$
Look at what these two equations tell us:
1. **If $p \in A$ (so $p^2 - 2 < 0$):**
   - From $q = p - \frac{p^2 - 2}{p + 2}$, subtracting a negative number makes $q > p$.
   - From $q^2 - 2 = \frac{2(p^2 - 2)}{(p + 2)^2}$, the numerator is negative, so $q^2 - 2 < 0$, meaning $q \in A$!
   Thus, for every $p \in A$, we have found a strictly larger rational $q \in A$ with $p < q$. Hence $A$ has **no largest element**.
2. **If $p \in B$ (so $p^2 - 2 > 0$):**
   - From $q = p - \frac{p^2 - 2}{p + 2}$, subtracting a positive number makes $q < p$.
   - From $q^2 - 2 = \frac{2(p^2 - 2)}{(p + 2)^2}$, the numerator is positive, so $q^2 - 2 > 0$, meaning $q \in B$!
   Thus, for every $p \in B$, we have found a strictly smaller rational $q \in B$ with $q < p$. Hence $B$ has **no smallest element**.

Finally, could $A$ have a supremum $\alpha = \sup A \in \mathbb{Q}$? Since $1 \in A$, any upper bound $\alpha$ must satisfy $\alpha \ge 1 > 0$. Since no rational satisfies $\alpha^2 = 2$, trichotomy forces either $\alpha \in A$ or $\alpha \in B$.
- If $\alpha \in A$, then since $A$ has no largest element, there exists $q \in A$ with $\alpha < q$, contradicting the fact that $\alpha$ is an upper bound of $A$!
- If $\alpha \in B$, then since $B$ has no smallest element, there exists $q \in B$ with $q < \alpha$. Since $q \in B$, $q$ is still an upper bound for all of $A$, contradicting the requirement that $\alpha$ is the *least* upper bound of $A$!

Therefore, $\sup A$ does not exist in $\mathbb{Q}$. $\blacksquare$

# 0.5 Constructing the Real Numbers via Dedekind Cuts

## The Geometry and Definition of a Dedekind Cut

Look back at the set $A = \{p \in \mathbb{Q} : p > 0 \text{ and } p^2 < 2\}$ (or better yet, include all negative rationals too: $\alpha = \{p \in \mathbb{Q} : p \le 0 \text{ or } p^2 < 2\}$). Even though $\sqrt{2}$ does not exist inside $\mathbb{Q}$, the set $\alpha \subsetneq \mathbb{Q}$ of all rational numbers *strictly to the left of the gap* exists completely inside $\mathbb{Q}$!

In 1872, Richard Dedekind had a stroke of genius: **if every point on the continuous number line (rational or irrational) uniquely slices $\mathbb{Q}$ into a "left half" and a "right half," why not *define* a real number to BE its left half $\alpha \subset \mathbb{Q}$?**

Let's examine what properties the open left ray $(-\infty, x) \cap \mathbb{Q}$ of a point $x$ has as a subset $\alpha \subset \mathbb{Q}$:
1. It is not the empty set $\emptyset$ (there are rationals to the left of $x$), nor is it all of $\mathbb{Q}$ (there are rationals to the right of $x$).
2. It is **downward closed**: if a rational $p$ is in the left half $\alpha$, then every smaller rational $q < p$ is also in the left half $\alpha$.
3. It has **no largest element**: just like an open interval $(-\infty, x)$, for any rational $p \in \alpha$, you can always nudge slightly to the right and find another rational $r \in \alpha$ with $p < r$.

These three properties define a **Dedekind cut**.

> [!abstract] Definition: Dedekind Cut and the Real Numbers $\mathbb{R}$
> A subset $\alpha \subset \mathbb{Q}$ is called a **Dedekind cut** (or simply a **cut**) if it satisfies the following three properties:
> 1. **Non-Triviality:** $\alpha \neq \emptyset$ and $\alpha \neq \mathbb{Q}$.
> 2. **Downward Closure:** If $p \in \alpha$ and $q \in \mathbb{Q}$ with $q < p$, then $q \in \alpha$.
> 3. **No Greatest Element:** If $p \in \alpha$, then there exists some $r \in \alpha$ such that $p < r$.
>
> The set of **real numbers**, denoted $\mathbb{R}$, is defined to be the set of all Dedekind cuts of $\mathbb{Q}$:
> $$
> \mathbb{R} := \{\alpha \subset \mathbb{Q} : \alpha \text{ is a Dedekind cut}\}
> $$

Let's immediately note two elementary consequences of the definition of a cut $\alpha$:
- If $p \in \alpha$ and $q \notin \alpha$, then $p < q$ (for if $q \le p$, downward closure would force $q \in \alpha$, a contradiction).
- Every rational number $r \in \mathbb{Q}$ gives rise to a **rational cut** $r^* \in \mathbb{R}$ consisting of all rationals strictly less than $r$:
  $$
  r^* := \{p \in \mathbb{Q} : p < r\}
  $$
  Let's check that $r^*$ is indeed a cut: (1) $r - 1 \in r^*$ so $r^* \neq \emptyset$, and $r \notin r^*$ so $r^* \neq \mathbb{Q}$; (2) if $q < p < r$, then $q < r$ by transitivity, so $q \in r^*$; (3) if $p < r$, then the midpoint $s = \frac{p + r}{2}$ satisfies $p < s < r$, so $s \in r^*$ is strictly larger than $p$. Meanwhile, our gap at $\sqrt{2}$ is captured by the **irrational cut** $\alpha = \{p \in \mathbb{Q} : p \le 0 \text{ or } p^2 < 2\}$.

## Ordering the Real Numbers and the Least Upper Bound Property

Here is where Dedekind cuts shine brighter than any other construction of $\mathbb{R}$. If a real number $\alpha$ is the set of all rationals to its left, what does it mean for one real number $\alpha$ to be strictly to the left of another real number $\beta$? It means the left ray $\alpha$ is a **proper subset** of the left ray $\beta$!

> [!abstract] Definition: Order on $\mathbb{R}$
> For any two real numbers (cuts) $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$, we define
> $$
> \alpha \le \beta \iff \alpha \subseteq \beta
> $$
> and we define $\alpha < \beta$ if $\alpha$ is a proper subset of $\beta$ ($\alpha \subsetneq \beta$, meaning $\alpha \subseteq \beta$ and there exists some rational $p \in \beta$ with $p \notin \alpha$).

Let's verify that set inclusion $\subsetneq$ satisfies the **Trichotomy Law** and **Transitivity**, and then prove the crown jewel of the Dedekind construction: **$\mathbb{R}$ has the Least Upper Bound Property**.

> [!theorem] Order Properties and Completeness (Least Upper Bound Property) of $\mathbb{R}$
> 1. The relation $<$ on $\mathbb{R}$ satisfies **Trichotomy** (for any $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$, exactly one of $\alpha < \beta$, $\alpha = \beta$, or $\beta < \alpha$ holds) and **Transitivity** ($\alpha < \beta \text{ and } \beta < \gamma \implies \alpha < \gamma$).
> 2. **Least Upper Bound Property (Dedekind Completeness):** Every non-empty subset $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ that is bounded above in $\mathbb{R}$ has a supremum $\gamma = \sup A \in \mathbb{R}$, given explicitly by the set union of all cuts in $A$:
>    $$
>    \gamma = \bigcup_{\alpha \in A} \alpha
>    $$

**Proof:**
**1. Trichotomy and Transitivity:**
Transitivity of $\subsetneq$ is immediate from set theory: if $\alpha \subsetneq \beta$ and $\beta \subsetneq \gamma$, then $\alpha \subsetneq \gamma$. For trichotomy, clearly at most one of $\alpha \subsetneq \beta$, $\alpha = \beta$, or $\beta \subsetneq \alpha$ can hold by the definition of set equality. To see that at least one holds, suppose $\alpha \not\subseteq \beta$. Then there exists some rational $p \in \alpha$ such that $p \notin \beta$. Now take *any* $q \in \beta$. Since $q \in \beta$ and $p \notin \beta$, our earlier remark on cuts implies $q < p$. Because $p \in \alpha$ and $\alpha$ is downward closed, $q < p \implies q \in \alpha$! Since $q \in \beta$ was arbitrary, this proves $\beta \subseteq \alpha$ (and since $p \in \alpha \setminus \beta$, we have $\beta \subsetneq \alpha$, i.e., $\beta < \alpha$).

**2. Least Upper Bound Property:**
Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be a non-empty set of cuts, and suppose $\beta \in \mathbb{R}$ is an upper bound for $A$ (meaning $\alpha \subseteq \beta$ for all $\alpha \in A$). Define the candidate supremum $\gamma \subset \mathbb{Q}$ to be the union of all cuts in $A$:
$$
\gamma := \bigcup_{\alpha \in A} \alpha = \{p \in \mathbb{Q} : \exists \alpha \in A \text{ such that } p \in \alpha\}
$$
First, we must verify that $\gamma$ is a valid Dedekind cut ($\gamma \in \mathbb{R}$):
- *Non-Triviality:* Since $A$ is non-empty, pick some $\alpha_0 \in A$. Because $\alpha_0 \neq \emptyset$, $\gamma \supseteq \alpha_0$ is also non-empty. Moreover, since $\alpha \subseteq \beta$ for every $\alpha \in A$, we have $\gamma = \bigcup_{\alpha \in A} \alpha \subseteq \beta$. Because $\beta \neq \mathbb{Q}$, there exists $q \in \mathbb{Q} \setminus \beta$, so $q \notin \gamma$, proving $\gamma \neq \mathbb{Q}$.
- *Downward Closure:* Let $p \in \gamma$ and $q < p$. By definition of union, $p \in \alpha$ for some $\alpha \in A$. Since $\alpha$ is a cut, downward closure of $\alpha$ gives $q \in \alpha$, and therefore $q \in \gamma$.
- *No Greatest Element:* Let $p \in \gamma$. Again, $p \in \alpha$ for some $\alpha \in A$. Since $\alpha$ is a cut and has no greatest element, there exists $r \in \alpha$ with $p < r$. Since $r \in \alpha \subseteq \gamma$, we have $r \in \gamma$ with $p < r$.

Thus $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}$ is a legitimate real number! Now we verify that $\gamma = \sup A$:
- *$\gamma$ is an upper bound of $A$:* By construction, $\alpha \subseteq \bigcup_{\alpha' \in A} \alpha' = \gamma$ for every $\alpha \in A$, which translates to $\alpha \le \gamma$ for all $\alpha \in A$.
- *$\gamma$ is the least upper bound:* Suppose $\delta \in \mathbb{R}$ is any cut with $\delta < \gamma$ (i.e., $\delta \subsetneq \gamma$). Then there exists a rational $p \in \gamma$ such that $p \notin \delta$. Since $p \in \gamma = \bigcup_{\alpha \in A} \alpha$, there is some cut $\alpha_1 \in A$ such that $p \in \alpha_1$. Because $p \in \alpha_1$ and $p \notin \delta$, we have $\delta < \alpha_1$, so $\delta$ is *not* an upper bound of $A$.

Therefore, $\gamma = \sup A$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note] Why Dedekind Cuts Make Completeness Effortless
> Pause and appreciate the elegance of that proof! Because we ordered cuts by subset inclusion ($\le$ is $\subseteq$), finding the least upper bound of a bounded family of cuts $A$ reduced to the simplest operation in set theory: **taking their union** $\bigcup_{\alpha \in A} \alpha$, which is automatically the smallest set containing every $\alpha \in A$.

## Addition of Dedekind Cuts

Now let's equip $\mathbb{R}$ with addition and verify the additive field axioms. How should we add two cuts $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$? Simply add every rational $r \in \alpha$ to every rational $s \in \beta$!

> [!abstract] Definition: Addition, Zero, and Negation of Cuts
> Let $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$.
> 1. **Sum:** We define the sum $\alpha + \beta$ to be the set of all pairwise sums of rationals from $\alpha$ and $\beta$:
>    $$
>    \alpha + \beta := \{r + s : r \in \alpha \text{ and } s \in \beta\}
>    $$
> 2. **Zero Element:** We define the additive identity of $\mathbb{R}$ to be the rational cut $0^* := \{p \in \mathbb{Q} : p < 0\}$.
> 3. **Additive Inverse (Negation):** For any cut $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$, we define $-\alpha$ by:
>    $$
>    -\alpha := \{p \in \mathbb{Q} : \exists r \in \mathbb{Q} \text{ with } r > 0 \text{ such that } -p - r \notin \alpha\}
>    $$

Why does the definition of $-\alpha$ have that extra nudge $r > 0$ ("$-p - r \notin \alpha$") instead of simply $\{p \in \mathbb{Q} : -p \notin \alpha\}$?

> [!warning] Caution: Why $-p \notin \alpha$ Alone Fails for Negation
> Suppose $\alpha = 2^* = \{q \in \mathbb{Q} : q < 2\}$. If we naively defined $-\alpha$ as $\{p \in \mathbb{Q} : -p \notin 2^*\}$, then $-p \notin 2^*$ means $-p \ge 2$, which is $p \le -2$. That set $\{p \in \mathbb{Q} : p \le -2\}$ **has a greatest element** ($p = -2$), violating Property 3 of a Dedekind cut! By requiring that there exist some small margin $r > 0$ such that $-p - r \notin \alpha$ (meaning $-p$ is *strictly* above the boundary of $\alpha$, not right on the boundary), we chop off that potential endpoint $-2$ and guarantee that $-\alpha$ has no greatest element.

Let's prove that $\alpha + \beta$ and $-\alpha$ are cuts and satisfy the additive field axioms, utilizing a neat "straddling lemma" for cuts: for any cut $\alpha$ and any positive rational $\varepsilon > 0$, one can always find $r \in \alpha$ and $s \notin \alpha$ whose distance $s - r$ is as small as we like ($s - r < \varepsilon$).

> [!theorem] Additive Group Structure of $\mathbb{R}$ and Order Compatibility
> For all $\alpha, \beta, \gamma \in \mathbb{R}$:
> 1. $\alpha + \beta$ and $-\alpha$ are Dedekind cuts.
> 2. Addition on $\mathbb{R}$ is associative, commutative, has identity $0^*$, and satisfies $\alpha + (-\alpha) = 0^*$.
> 3. **Order Preservation (O3):** If $\alpha < \beta$, then $\alpha + \gamma < \beta + \gamma$.

**Proof:**
**1. $\alpha + \beta$ is a cut:**
- *Non-Triviality:* Since $\alpha, \beta \neq \emptyset$, $\alpha + \beta \neq \emptyset$. Pick $p \notin \alpha$ and $q \notin \beta$. For every $r \in \alpha$ and $s \in \beta$, we have $r < p$ and $s < q$, so $r + s < p + q$. Thus $p + q \notin \alpha + \beta$, proving $\alpha + \beta \neq \mathbb{Q}$.
- *Downward Closure:* Suppose $x \in \alpha + \beta$ (so $x = r + s$ with $r \in \alpha, s \in \beta$) and $y < x$. Then $y - s < r$. Since $r \in \alpha$ and $\alpha$ is downward closed, $y - s \in \alpha$. Thus $y = (y - s) + s \in \alpha + \beta$.
- *No Greatest Element:* If $x = r + s \in \alpha + \beta$, since $\alpha$ has no greatest element there exists $r' \in \alpha$ with $r < r'$. Then $r' + s \in \alpha + \beta$ and $x = r + s < r' + s$.

**2. Identity $\alpha + 0^* = \alpha$, Commutativity, and Associativity:**
Commutativity and associativity of $\alpha + \beta$ follow immediately from commutativity and associativity of addition in $\mathbb{Q}$. For $\alpha + 0^* = \alpha$:
- If $r \in \alpha$ and $s \in 0^*$ (so $s < 0$), then $r + s < r$, so $r + s \in \alpha$ by downward closure. Thus $\alpha + 0^* \subseteq \alpha$.
- Conversely, if $x \in \alpha$, choose $r \in \alpha$ with $x < r$ (no greatest element). Then $x - r < 0$, so $x - r \in 0^*$, and $x = r + (x - r) \in \alpha + 0^*$. Thus $\alpha \subseteq \alpha + 0^*$, giving $\alpha + 0^* = \alpha$.

**3. Inverse $-\alpha$ is a cut and $\alpha + (-\alpha) = 0^*$:**
Pick $s \notin \alpha$ and let $p = -s - 1$; with $r = 1 > 0$, $-p - r = s \notin \alpha$, so $p \in -\alpha \neq \emptyset$. Pick $q \in \alpha$; for any $p \le -q$ and $r > 0$, $-p - r < q \in \alpha$, so $-q \notin -\alpha \neq \mathbb{Q}$. Downward closure holds because if $q < p \in -\alpha$ with $-p - r \notin \alpha$, then $-q - r > -p - r \notin \alpha$, so $-q - r \notin \alpha$. And if $p \in -\alpha$ with $-p - r \notin \alpha$ ($r > 0$), setting $p' = p + r/2 > p$ and $r' = r/2 > 0$ gives $-p' - r' = -p - r \notin \alpha$, so $p' \in -\alpha$ is strictly larger than $p$. Thus $-\alpha$ is a cut.

To prove $\alpha + (-\alpha) = 0^*$:
- First, take any $r \in \alpha$ and $p \in -\alpha$. By definition of $-\alpha$, $-p > -p - t \notin \alpha$ for some $t > 0$, so $-p \notin \alpha$. Since $r \in \alpha$ and $-p \notin \alpha$, we have $r < -p$, which means $r + p < 0$, so $r + p \in 0^*$. Thus $\alpha + (-\alpha) \subseteq 0^*$.
- Conversely, take any $v \in 0^*$ (so $v < 0$), and set $d = -v / 2 > 0$. Pick $a_0 \in \alpha$ and $b_0 \notin \alpha$. By stepping across the interval $[a_0, b_0]$ in rational increments of size $d$ (using the fact that any bounded subset of $\mathbb{Z}$ is finite), there exists a last step $n \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $r := a_0 + nd \in \alpha$ while $a_0 + (n + 1)d \notin \alpha$ and $a_0 + (n + 2)d \notin \alpha$. Now set $p := -a_0 - (n + 2)d$. Because $-p - d = a_0 + (n + 1)d \notin \alpha$ with $d > 0$, we have $p \in -\alpha$! And adding $r \in \alpha$ and $p \in -\alpha$ gives
  $$
  r + p = (a_0 + nd) + (-a_0 - (n + 2)d) = -2d = v
  $$
  Thus $v \in \alpha + (-\alpha)$, proving $0^* \subseteq \alpha + (-\alpha)$ and hence $\alpha + (-\alpha) = 0^*$.

Finally, **Order Preservation (O3)** follows immediately from the group laws: if $\alpha \subseteq \beta$, then clearly $\alpha + \gamma \subseteq \beta + \gamma$; and if $\alpha + \gamma = \beta + \gamma$, adding $-\gamma$ to both sides yields $\alpha = \beta$, so $\alpha < \beta \implies \alpha + \gamma < \beta + \gamma$. $\blacksquare$

## Multiplication of Dedekind Cuts and the Complete Ordered Field

Why do we need a tiny bit of care when multiplying two cuts $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$? Because every cut $\alpha$ contains arbitrarily large negative rationals (like $-1000 \in \alpha$), and the product of two negative numbers is *positive*! If we tried to define $\alpha \cdot \beta$ naively as $\{r \cdot s : r \in \alpha, s \in \beta\}$, multiplying two large negative rationals in $\alpha$ and $\beta$ would produce arbitrarily large positive rationals, turning $\{r \cdot s\}$ into the entire set $\mathbb{Q}$!

The fix is completely natural: we first define multiplication for **positive cuts** $\alpha > 0^*$ and $\beta > 0^*$ using only their positive rational elements $r > 0, s > 0$, and then we extend multiplication to negative cuts using standard sign rules!

> [!abstract] Definition: Multiplication of Dedekind Cuts
> 1. **Product of Positive Cuts:** For any two positive cuts $\alpha > 0^*$ and $\beta > 0^*$, we define their product $\alpha \cdot \beta$ by:
>    $$
>    \alpha \cdot \beta := \{p \in \mathbb{Q} : p \le 0 \text{ or } \exists r \in \alpha, s \in \beta \text{ with } r > 0, s > 0 \text{ such that } p = r \cdot s\}
>    $$
> 2. **Extension to All Cuts:** For general $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$, we define:
>    $$
>    \alpha \cdot \beta := \begin{cases}
>    0^* & \text{if } \alpha = 0^* \text{ or } \beta = 0^* \\
>    -( (-\alpha) \cdot \beta ) & \text{if } \alpha < 0^* \text{ and } \beta > 0^* \\
>    -( \alpha \cdot (-\beta) ) & \text{if } \alpha > 0^* \text{ and } \beta < 0^* \\
>    (-\alpha) \cdot (-\beta) & \text{if } \alpha < 0^* \text{ and } \beta < 0^*
>    \end{cases}
>    $$
> 3. **Multiplicative Identity and Inverse:** We define the multiplicative identity to be $1^* := \{q \in \mathbb{Q} : q < 1\}$. For any positive cut $\alpha > 0^*$, we define its **multiplicative inverse** $\alpha^{-1}$ by:
>    $$
>    \alpha^{-1} := \{p \in \mathbb{Q} : p \le 0\} \cup \left\{p \in \mathbb{Q} : p > 0 \text{ and } \exists r \in \mathbb{Q} \text{ with } r > 0 \text{ such that } \frac{1}{p} - r \notin \alpha\right\}
>    $$
>    and for $\alpha < 0^*$, we define $\alpha^{-1} := -((-\alpha)^{-1})$.

Notice how the definition of $\alpha \cdot \beta$ and $\alpha^{-1}$ for $\alpha, \beta > 0^*$ mirrors our proof for $\alpha + \beta$ and $-\alpha$ on the positive rationals $\mathbb{Q}^+$ (since $\mathbb{Q}^+$ under multiplication has the exact same structure as $\mathbb{Q}$ under addition!).

> [!theorem] $\mathbb{R}$ Is a Complete Ordered Field Extending $\mathbb{Q}$
> 1. Equipped with $+, \cdot, <$ defined on Dedekind cuts, $\mathbb{R}$ is an **ordered field** that satisfies the **Least Upper Bound Property** (i.e., $\mathbb{R}$ is a **complete ordered field**).
> 2. The map $\iota: \mathbb{Q} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}$ given by $r \mapsto r^* = \{p \in \mathbb{Q} : p < r\}$ is an **order-preserving field embedding**: for all $r, s \in \mathbb{Q}$,
>    $$
>    (r + s)^* = r^* + s^*, \qquad (r \cdot s)^* = r^* \cdot s^*, \qquad r < s \iff r^* < s^*
>    $$

**Proof:**
For $\alpha, \beta > 0^*$, $\alpha \cdot \beta$ is a cut by the exact same check as addition: it contains $0$ (so $\neq \emptyset$), is bounded above by $p \cdot q$ for $p \notin \alpha, q \notin \beta$ (so $\neq \mathbb{Q}$), is downward closed, and has no greatest element because if $p = rs \in \alpha \cdot \beta$ with $r, s > 0$, choosing $r' \in \alpha$ with $r < r'$ gives $r's > rs$. Moreover, $\alpha \cdot \beta > 0^*$ immediately because $\alpha > 0^*, \beta > 0^*$ contain positive rationals $r > 0, s > 0$, so $rs > 0$ lies in $\alpha \cdot \beta \setminus 0^*$, proving Axiom (O4). Associativity, commutativity, $\alpha \cdot 1^* = \alpha$, $\alpha \cdot \alpha^{-1} = 1^*$, and distributivity hold for positive cuts directly from the corresponding properties of $\mathbb{Q}^+$ and extend to all cuts by standard sign case analysis.

Finally, for the embedding $r \mapsto r^*$:
- $r < s \implies r \in s^* \setminus r^* \implies r^* \subsetneq s^* \implies r^* < s^*$.
- If $p \in r^*$ and $q \in s^*$, then $p + q < r + s$, so $r^* + s^* \subseteq (r + s)^*$. Conversely, if $x < r + s$, set $h = (r + s - x)/2 > 0$; then $x = (r - h) + (s - h) \in r^* + s^*$. Thus $r^* + s^* = (r + s)^*$, and the exact same argument on positive factors proves $(r \cdot s)^* = r^* \cdot s^*$. $\blacksquare$

## Rewards of Completeness: The Archimedean Property, Density of $\mathbb{Q}$, and $n$-th Roots

Now that we have constructed $\mathbb{R}$ as a **complete ordered field** and identified $\mathbb{Q}$ with the subfield of rational cuts $\{r^* : r \in \mathbb{Q}\} \subset \mathbb{R}$, we never have to look back at the internal scaffolding of sets, pairs, or cuts again! Even better, one proves in abstract algebra that **any two complete ordered fields are uniquely isomorphic**—so whether someone builds $\mathbb{R}$ via Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences, they end up with the exact same mathematical universe up to relabeling.

To close our preliminary construction, let's prove three foundational theorems of Real Analysis directly from the **Least Upper Bound Property** of $\mathbb{R}$, showing that our new number system has no infinitesimals, contains a rational between any two reals, and fills the $\sqrt{2}$ hole (and all positive $n$-th roots!).

> [!theorem] The Archimedean Property of $\mathbb{R}$
> If $x, y \in \mathbb{R}$ and $x > 0$, then there exists a positive integer $n \in \mathbb{N}^+$ such that
> $$
> nx > y
> $$
> Equivalently, $\mathbb{N}$ is not bounded above in $\mathbb{R}$, and for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $n \in \mathbb{N}^+$ such that $\frac{1}{n} < \varepsilon$.

**Proof:**
Let $A := \{nx : n \in \mathbb{N}^+\}$ be the set of all positive integer multiples of $x$. Suppose for the sake of contradiction that $nx \le y$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}^+$. Then $A$ is a non-empty subset of $\mathbb{R}$ that is bounded above by $y$.

By the **Least Upper Bound Property** of $\mathbb{R}$, $A$ has a supremum $\alpha = \sup A \in \mathbb{R}$. Since $x > 0$, we have $\alpha - x < \alpha$. Because $\alpha$ is the *least* upper bound of $A$, $\alpha - x$ cannot be an upper bound of $A$! Therefore, there exists some element $mx \in A$ (with $m \in \mathbb{N}^+$) such that
$$
\alpha - x < mx
$$
Adding $x$ to both sides yields
$$
\alpha < mx + x = (m + 1)x
$$
However, since $m + 1 \in \mathbb{N}^+$, the number $(m + 1)x$ is an element of $A$ that is strictly greater than $\alpha = \sup A$—a contradiction! Thus $nx > y$ for some $n \in \mathbb{N}^+$. $\blacksquare$

> [!theorem] Density of $\mathbb{Q}$ in $\mathbb{R}$
> If $x, y \in \mathbb{R}$ and $x < y$, then there exists a rational number $r \in \mathbb{Q}$ such that
> $$
> x < r < y
> $$

**Proof:**
Since $x < y$, we have $y - x > 0$. By the Archimedean Property applied to $y - x > 0$ and $1$, there exists a positive integer $n \in \mathbb{N}^+$ such that
$$
n(y - x) > 1 \implies ny - nx > 1
$$
Intuitively, since $nx$ and $ny$ are more than $1$ unit apart, there must be an integer $m \in \mathbb{Z}$ sitting strictly between them! To prove this rigorously, apply the Archimedean Property again to choose $m_1, m_2 \in \mathbb{N}^+$ such that $m_1 > nx$ and $m_2 > -nx$, so that
$$
-m_2 < nx < m_1
$$
Consider the set of integers $E := \{k \in \mathbb{Z} : -m_2 \le k \le m_1 \text{ and } k > nx\}$. By shifting by $m_2$ and applying the Well-Ordering Principle of $\mathbb{N}$, this non-empty set of integers bounded below has a **least element** $m \in \mathbb{Z}$.

Because $m$ is the *smallest* integer strictly greater than $nx$, we have $nx < m$, while its predecessor $m - 1$ must satisfy $m - 1 \le nx$. Combining this with $ny - nx > 1$ gives:
$$
nx < m \le nx + 1 < ny
$$
Dividing the inequality $nx < m < ny$ by the positive integer $n > 0$ yields a rational number $r = \frac{m}{n} \in \mathbb{Q}$ satisfying
$$
x < \frac{m}{n} < y
$$
which completes the proof. $\blacksquare$

Finally, let us verify that the hole we found in $\mathbb{Q}$ at $x^2 = 2$ (and more generally $x^n = y$ for any real $y > 0$ and integer $n \ge 1$) is completely sealed in $\mathbb{R}$.

> [!theorem] Existence and Uniqueness of Positive $n$-th Roots in $\mathbb{R}$
> For every real number $y > 0$ and every positive integer $n \in \mathbb{N}^+$, there exists a unique real number $x > 0$ such that
> $$
> x^n = y
> $$
> This number $x$ is denoted by $\sqrt[n]{y}$ or $y^{1/n}$.

**Proof:**
**Uniqueness:** If $0 < x_1 < x_2$, then repeated application of order preservation (O4) gives $x_1^n < x_2^n$, so at most one positive real $x$ can satisfy $x^n = y$.

**Existence:** Consider the subset of $\mathbb{R}$:
$$
E := \{t \in \mathbb{R} : t > 0 \text{ and } t^n < y\}
$$
First, let us show $E$ is non-empty and bounded above:
- Let $t_0 := \frac{y}{1 + y}$. Then $0 < t_0 < 1$ and $t_0 < y$, so $t_0^n \le t_0 < y$. Thus $t_0 \in E$, and $E \neq \emptyset$.
- Let $M := 1 + y > 1$. For any $t > M$, we have $t > 1$ and $t > y$, so $t^n \ge t > y$, meaning $t \notin E$. Thus $M = 1 + y$ is an upper bound for $E$.

By the **Least Upper Bound Property** of $\mathbb{R}$, $E$ has a supremum:
$$
x := \sup E \in \mathbb{R}
$$
Since $t_0 \in E$ with $t_0 > 0$, we have $x \ge t_0 > 0$. By Trichotomy, either $x^n < y$, $x^n > y$, or $x^n = y$. We rule out both inequalities by showing that if $x^n \neq y$, we can nudge $x$ slightly by a small $h > 0$ without crossing $y$, using the binomial bound $(x + h)^n - x^n \le h n (x + h)^{n-1}$ (which follows from $b^n - a^n = (b - a)(b^{n-1} + b^{n-2}a + \dots + a^{n-1}) \le (b - a) n b^{n-1}$ for $0 < a < b$):
1. **Suppose $x^n < y$:** Choose $h \in (0, 1)$ small enough that
   $$
   h < \frac{y - x^n}{n(x + 1)^{n-1}}
   $$
   Then applying $b^n - a^n \le (b - a) n b^{n-1}$ with $a = x$ and $b = x + h < x + 1$ gives:
   $$
   (x + h)^n - x^n \le h n (x + h)^{n-1} < h n (x + 1)^{n-1} < y - x^n
   $$
   Thus $(x + h)^n < y$, which means $x + h \in E$! But $x + h > x = \sup E$, contradicting the fact that $x$ is an upper bound of $E$.
2. **Suppose $x^n > y$:** Set $k := \frac{x^n - y}{n x^{n-1}}$. Since $x > 0$ and $x^n > y > 0$, we have $0 < k < x$. For any $t \ge x - k$, we have $x - t \le k$, so applying $b^n - a^n \le (b - a) n b^{n-1}$ gives:
   $$
   x^n - t^n \le (x - t) n x^{n-1} \le k n x^{n-1} = x^n - y \implies t^n \ge y
   $$
   Thus no element of $E$ can be $\ge x - k$, which means $x - k < x$ is an upper bound for $E$—contradicting the fact that $x$ is the *least* upper bound of $E$!

Since both $x^n < y$ and $x^n > y$ are impossible, Trichotomy forces $x^n = y$. $\blacksquare$

> [!summary] Summary of the Number System Pipeline
> Let's step back and admire the complete architecture we have built from the empty set:
> 1. **Sets $\to \mathbb{N}$ (Counting & Induction):** Starting from $0 = \emptyset$ and $S(n) = n \cup \{n\}$, the **Peano Axioms** give us mathematical induction, recursive definitions of $+$ and $\cdot$, cancellation laws, no zero divisors, and the Well-Ordering Principle.
> 2. **$\mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{Z}$ (Additive Inverses / Subtraction):** Taking equivalence classes $[a, b]$ of formal differences $(a, b) \in \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ under $a + d = b + c$ upgrades $\mathbb{N}$ to an ordered **integral domain** (commutative ring with no zero divisors) where every equation $b + x = a$ is solvable.
> 3. **$\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Q}$ (Multiplicative Inverses / Division):** Taking equivalence classes $[p, q]$ of formal fractions $(p, q) \in \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}^*$ under $ps = qr$ upgrades $\mathbb{Z}$ to an **ordered field** where every linear equation $qx = p$ ($q \neq 0$) is solvable, though $\mathbb{Q}$ remains incomplete (lacking $\sqrt{2}$ and failing the Least Upper Bound Property).
> 4. **$\mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{R}$ (Analytical Completeness / Limits & Supremums):** Taking **Dedekind cuts** $\alpha \subset \mathbb{Q}$ fills every gap in $\mathbb{Q}$, producing the unique **complete ordered field** $\mathbb{R}$ in which every non-empty set bounded above has a supremum $\sup A = \bigcup_{\alpha \in A} \alpha$.

# 0.6 Absolute Value, Distance, and the Extended Reals

With the real number system $\mathbb{R}$ constructed, we now assemble the daily analytical toolkit that you will use on virtually every page of a Real Analysis textbook: **absolute value**, **the Triangle Inequality**, **$\varepsilon$-neighborhoods**, **the $\varepsilon$-characterization of supremums**, and the **extended real number system** $\overline{\mathbb{R}} = \mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty, +\infty\}$.

## Absolute Value and the Triangle Inequality

Order tells us whether $x$ is to the left or right of $y$, but analysis is fundamentally about **closeness** and **approximation**—how far apart $x$ and $y$ are, regardless of direction. The tool that turns order into distance is the **absolute value**.

> [!abstract] Definition: Absolute Value and Distance on $\mathbb{R}$
> For any real number $x \in \mathbb{R}$, its **absolute value** $|x|$ is defined piecewise by:
> $$
> |x| := \begin{cases}
> x & \text{if } x \ge 0 \\
> -x & \text{if } x < 0
> \end{cases}
> $$
> The **distance** between two real numbers $x, y \in \mathbb{R}$ is defined as $d(x, y) := |x - y|$.

Why does every single $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ or sequence convergence proof in analysis revolve around $|x - y| < \varepsilon$? Because of a fundamental equivalence that translates a single absolute-value inequality into a two-sided sandwich!

> [!theorem] Fundamental Properties of Absolute Value and the Triangle Inequality
> For all $x, y, z, c \in \mathbb{R}$:
> 1. **Non-Negativity and Definiteness:** $|x| \ge 0$, and $|x| = 0 \iff x = 0$.
> 2. **Multiplicativity:** $|x \cdot y| = |x| \cdot |y|$, and $|-x| = |x|$.
> 3. **Sandwich Equivalence:** For any $c \ge 0$,
>    $$
>    |x| \le c \iff -c \le x \le c \qquad \text{and} \qquad |x| < c \iff -c < x < c
>    $$
>    In particular, $-|x| \le x \le |x|$.
> 4. **The Triangle Inequality:**
>    $$
>    |x + y| \le |x| + |y|
>    $$
> 5. **The Reverse Triangle Inequality:**
>    $$
>    \big| |x| - |y| \big| \le |x - y|
>    $$
> 6. **Metric Triangle Inequality for Distance:**
>    $$
>    |x - z| \le |x - y| + |y - z|
>    $$

**Proof:**
Properties (1) and (2) follow immediately by checking the sign cases of $x$ and $y$ using the ordered field laws.

For (3), suppose $|x| \le c$. Since both $x \le |x|$ and $-x \le |x|$ hold in all sign cases, transitivity gives $x \le c$ and $-x \le c$ (which is $x \ge -c$), so $-c \le x \le c$. Conversely, if $-c \le x \le c$, then both $x \le c$ and $-x \le c$ hold; since $|x|$ equals either $x$ or $-x$, we have $|x| \le c$.

For (4) (**The Triangle Inequality**), we apply $-|a| \le a \le |a|$ to $x$ and $y$ separately:
$$
-|x| \le x \le |x| \quad \text{and} \quad -|y| \le y \le |y|
$$
Adding these two inequalities term-by-term gives
$$
-(|x| + |y|) \le x + y \le |x| + |y|
$$
By Property (3) with $c = |x| + |y|$, this two-sided inequality is equivalent to $|x + y| \le |x| + |y|$!

For (5) (**The Reverse Triangle Inequality**), write $x = (x - y) + y$ and apply the Triangle Inequality:
$$
|x| = |(x - y) + y| \le |x - y| + |y| \implies |x| - |y| \le |x - y|
$$
Swapping the roles of $x$ and $y$ gives $|y| - |x| \le |y - x| = |x - y|$, or $-(|x| - |y|) \le |x - y|$. Combining both via Property (3) yields $\big| |x| - |y| \big| \le |x - y|$.

Finally, (6) follows by writing $x - z = (x - y) + (y - z)$ and applying (4). $\blacksquare$

> [!note] Geometric Interpretation: $\varepsilon$-Neighborhoods
> Given a center point $a \in \mathbb{R}$ and a radius $\varepsilon > 0$, Property (3) shows that the set of points within distance $\varepsilon$ of $a$ is simply the open interval centered at $a$:
> $$
> V_\varepsilon(a) := \{x \in \mathbb{R} : |x - a| < \varepsilon\} = (a - \varepsilon, a + \varepsilon)
> $$
> This set $V_\varepsilon(a)$ is called the **$\varepsilon$-neighborhood** of $a$. Whenever you see $|x - a| < \varepsilon$ in an analysis proof, read it geometrically as: *"$x$ lies in the $\varepsilon$-neighborhood $(a - \varepsilon, a + \varepsilon)$ around $a$."*

## The $\varepsilon$-Principle and Supremum Characterization

In algebra, you prove $a = b$ by manipulating equations directly. In analysis, direct equality is rare; instead, you almost always prove $a \le b$ (or $a = b$) by showing that $a$ cannot exceed $b$ by any positive margin of error $\varepsilon > 0$, no matter how tiny! Let's record this principle and the $\varepsilon$-characterization of the supremum—two tools you will use in nearly every chapter of Real Analysis.

> [!theorem] The $\varepsilon$-Principle for Inequalities and Equality
> Let $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$.
> 1. If $a \le b + \varepsilon$ for **every** $\varepsilon > 0$, then $a \le b$.
> 2. If $|a - b| \le \varepsilon$ (or $|a - b| < \varepsilon$) for **every** $\varepsilon > 0$, then $a = b$.

**Proof:**
For (1), suppose for the sake of contradiction that $a > b$. Then the gap $\varepsilon_0 := \frac{a - b}{2}$ is strictly positive ($\varepsilon_0 > 0$). By our hypothesis applied to $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0$, we must have
$$
a \le b + \frac{a - b}{2} = \frac{a + b}{2} < \frac{a + a}{2} = a
$$
giving $a < a$, a contradiction! Thus $a \le b$. Part (2) follows immediately by applying (1) to $|a - b| \le 0 + \varepsilon$, which forces $|a - b| \le 0$ and hence $a = b$. $\blacksquare$

> [!theorem] The $\varepsilon$-Characterization of the Supremum
> Let $E \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be a non-empty subset that is bounded above, and let $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$ be an upper bound of $E$. Then $\alpha = \sup E$ if and only if for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists at least one element $x \in E$ such that
> $$
> \alpha - \varepsilon < x \le \alpha
> $$

**Proof:**
- **($\implies$):** Suppose $\alpha = \sup E$, and let $\varepsilon > 0$ be arbitrary. Because $\alpha - \varepsilon < \alpha$ and $\alpha$ is the *least* upper bound of $E$, the number $\alpha - \varepsilon$ cannot be an upper bound of $E$. Therefore, there must exist at least one element $x \in E$ that exceeds $\alpha - \varepsilon$, i.e., $\alpha - \varepsilon < x \le \alpha$.
- **($\Longleftarrow$):** Suppose $\alpha$ is an upper bound of $E$ satisfying the $\varepsilon$-condition, and let $\gamma < \alpha$ be any number strictly less than $\alpha$. Setting $\varepsilon := \alpha - \gamma > 0$, our hypothesis provides an element $x \in E$ with $\gamma = \alpha - \varepsilon < x$, which proves $\gamma$ is not an upper bound of $E$. Hence $\alpha = \sup E$. $\blacksquare$

## Infimums and the Extended Real Number System

When we defined the Least Upper Bound Property, we asked whether every non-empty set bounded *above* has a supremum $\sup E$. What about a non-empty set $E \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ that is bounded *below*? Does it automatically have a greatest lower bound (infimum) $\inf E$, or do we need a separate axiom?

We get $\inf E$ completely for free by reflecting the set $E$ across $0$!

> [!theorem] Existence of Infimums (Greatest Lower Bound Property of $\mathbb{R}$)
> Every non-empty subset $E \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ that is bounded below in $\mathbb{R}$ has an infimum $\inf E \in \mathbb{R}$. Specifically, if we define the reflected set $-E := \{-x : x \in E\}$, then $-E$ is bounded above and
> $$
> \inf E = -\sup(-E)
> $$
> Moreover, an lower bound $\beta$ of $E$ equals $\inf E$ if and only if for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $x \in E$ such that $\beta \le x < \beta + \varepsilon$.

**Proof:**
Suppose $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}$ is a lower bound of $E$, meaning $\gamma \le x$ for all $x \in E$. Multiplying by $-1$ reverses the inequality to $-x \le -\gamma$ for all $-x \in -E$, so $-E$ is non-empty and bounded above by $-\gamma$. By the Least Upper Bound Property of $\mathbb{R}$, $\alpha := \sup(-E)$ exists in $\mathbb{R}$. Let $\beta := -\alpha$.
- Since $-x \le \alpha$ for all $x \in E$, we have $x \ge -\alpha = \beta$ for all $x \in E$, so $\beta$ is a lower bound of $E$.
- If $\gamma$ is any lower bound of $E$, then $-\gamma$ is an upper bound of $-E$, so $\alpha \le -\gamma$, which gives $\gamma \le -\alpha = \beta$. Thus $\beta$ is the *greatest* lower bound of $E$, i.e., $\beta = \inf E$. $\blacksquare$

Finally, what if a non-empty subset $E \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is **not** bounded above, or **not** bounded below? To make $\sup E$ and $\inf E$ defined for *every* non-empty subset of $\mathbb{R}$ without exception, analysts adjoin two formal symbols $-\infty$ and $+\infty$ to $\mathbb{R}$.

> [!abstract] Definition: The Extended Real Number System $\overline{\mathbb{R}}$
> The **extended real number system** is the set $\overline{\mathbb{R}} := \mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty, +\infty\}$, equipped with the extended order relation
> $$
> -\infty < x < +\infty \quad \text{for all } x \in \mathbb{R}
> $$
> For any non-empty subset $E \subseteq \mathbb{R}$:
> - If $E$ is **not bounded above** in $\mathbb{R}$, we define $\sup E := +\infty$.
> - If $E$ is **not bounded below** in $\mathbb{R}$, we define $\inf E := -\infty$.

> [!warning] Caution: $\overline{\mathbb{R}}$ Is Not a Field!
> The symbols $+\infty$ and $-\infty$ are **not** real numbers ($+\infty, -\infty \notin \mathbb{R}$), and $\overline{\mathbb{R}}$ is not a field! While most textbooks adopt convenient shorthand conventions when limits diverge (such as $x + (+\infty) = +\infty$ for $x \in \mathbb{R}$ and $x / (\pm\infty) = 0$), expressions like $(+\infty) - (+\infty)$, $0 \cdot (+\infty)$, and $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$ are **strictly undefined indeterminate forms** because they violate the additive and multiplicative cancellation laws of a field.

# 0.7 Cardinality: Countable and Uncountable Sets

We conclude our preliminary foundation with one of the most breathtaking discoveries of 19th-century mathematics, due to Georg Cantor: **not all infinities are the same size**.

When we expanded $\mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{R}$, did our sets actually grow in "infinite size" at each step? Remarkably, $\mathbb{N}$, $\mathbb{Z}$, and $\mathbb{Q}$ all have the **exact same cardinality** (they are all *countably infinite*—you can list all rational numbers in a sequence $q_1, q_2, q_3, \dots$ without missing a single one!). However, when we pass from $\mathbb{Q}$ to $\mathbb{R}$ via Dedekind cuts, the size of infinity explodes: **$\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable**, meaning no sequence can ever list all real numbers.

## Equinumerosity and Finite vs. Infinite Sets

How can we compare the sizes of two infinite sets $A$ and $B$ without counting them to the end? Imagine an auditorium filled with people and chairs. You do not need to count the people or the chairs to know whether there are the same number of each—you just ask everyone to sit down, one person per chair! If every person gets a chair and no chair is left empty, you have a **bijection** between people and chairs.

> [!abstract] Definition: Equinumerosity, Finite Sets, and Countable Sets
> Let $A$ and $B$ be sets. We say that $A$ and $B$ are **equinumerous** (or have the **same cardinality**), written $A \sim B$, if there exists a **bijection** $f: A \to B$.
>
> Using the initial segments $\mathbb{N}_n := \{1, 2, \dots, n\}$ for $n \in \mathbb{N}^+$:
> 1. **Finite Set:** A set $A$ is **finite** if $A = \emptyset$ (cardinality $0$) or $A \sim \mathbb{N}_n$ for some $n \in \mathbb{N}^+$ (cardinality $n$).
> 2. **Infinite Set:** A set $A$ is **infinite** if it is not finite.
> 3. **Countably Infinite (Denumerable) Set:** A set $A$ is **countably infinite** if $A \sim \mathbb{N}^+$ (or equivalently $A \sim \mathbb{N}$, since $n \mapsto n + 1$ is a bijection $\mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}^+$).
> 4. **Countable (At Most Countable) Set:** A set $A$ is **countable** if it is either finite or countably infinite.
> 5. **Uncountable Set:** A set $A$ is **uncountable** if it is infinite and not countably infinite.

Notice that a set $A$ is countably infinite ($A \sim \mathbb{N}^+$) if and only if its elements can be arranged in an infinite **sequence** $a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots$ (where $a_n = f(n)$) in which every element of $A$ appears exactly once!

## Countability of $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$, $\mathbb{Z}$, and $\mathbb{Q}$

With finite sets, a proper subset $B \subsetneq A$ is always strictly smaller in cardinality than $A$. With infinite sets, however, a proper subset can have the *exact same cardinality* as the whole set! For example, the set of even positive integers $E = \{2, 4, 6, 8, \dots\} \subsetneq \mathbb{N}^+$ is in bijection with $\mathbb{N}^+$ via $f(n) = 2n$. In fact, $\mathbb{N}^+$ is the *smallest* possible infinity: every infinite subset of $\mathbb{N}^+$ is countably infinite.

> [!theorem] Every Subset of a Countable Set Is Countable
> Every subset $E \subseteq \mathbb{N}^+$ is countable (either finite or countably infinite). Consequently, a non-empty set $A$ is countable if and only if there exists an injection $g: A \hookrightarrow \mathbb{N}^+$ (or a surjection $h: \mathbb{N}^+ \twoheadrightarrow A$).

**Proof:**
If $E \subseteq \mathbb{N}^+$ is finite, we are done. Suppose $E$ is infinite. We construct a bijection $f: \mathbb{N}^+ \to E$ recursively using the **Well-Ordering Principle** of $\mathbb{N}$:
- Since $E$ is non-empty, let $f(1) := \min E$.
- Having defined $f(1), \dots, f(n)$, the remaining set $E_n := E \setminus \{f(1), \dots, f(n)\}$ is non-empty (because $E$ is infinite), so by the Well-Ordering Principle we define $f(n + 1) := \min E_n$.

By construction, $f(1) < f(2) < f(3) < \dots$ is strictly increasing, hence injective. To see that $f$ is surjective onto $E$, take any $m \in E$. Since $f(1) < f(2) < \dots$, an easy induction shows $f(k) \ge k$ for all $k \in \mathbb{N}^+$. Thus $m$ must be chosen as $\min E_n$ at or before step $m$, so $m \in f(\mathbb{N}^+)$. Therefore $E \sim \mathbb{N}^+$. $\blacksquare$

Now let's show that **$\mathbb{Z}$**, **$\mathbb{N}^+ \times \mathbb{N}^+$**, **countable unions of countable sets**, and **$\mathbb{Q}$** are all countable!

> [!theorem] Countability of $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{N}^+ \times \mathbb{N}^+$, Countable Unions, and $\mathbb{Q}$
> 1. The set of integers $\mathbb{Z}$ is countably infinite.
> 2. The Cartesian product $\mathbb{N}^+ \times \mathbb{N}^+$ is countably infinite.
> 3. If $\{A_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$ is a sequence of countable sets, then their countable union $A = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n$ is countable.
> 4. The set of rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$ is countably infinite.

**Proof:**
**1. Countability of $\mathbb{Z}$:**
We list $\mathbb{Z}$ by alternating between $0$, positive integers, and negative integers:
$$
0, \; 1, \; -1, \; 2, \; -2, \; 3, \; -3, \dots
$$
Explicitly, the function $f: \mathbb{N}^+ \to \mathbb{Z}$ defined by $f(2k) = k$ and $f(2k - 1) = -(k - 1)$ for $k \in \mathbb{N}^+$ is a bijection, so $\mathbb{Z} \sim \mathbb{N}^+$.

**2. Countability of $\mathbb{N}^+ \times \mathbb{N}^+$:**
Imagine arranging all pairs $(m, n) \in \mathbb{N}^+ \times \mathbb{N}^+$ in an infinite two-dimensional grid. If we tried to count row-by-row, we would never finish the first row! Instead, we sweep along the finite **diagonals** where $m + n = k$ for $k = 2, 3, 4, \dots$:
$$
(1, 1), \; (1, 2), (2, 1), \; (1, 3), (2, 2), (3, 1), \dots
$$
Even more simply, we can write down an explicit injection $g: \mathbb{N}^+ \times \mathbb{N}^+ \hookrightarrow \mathbb{N}^+$ using unique prime factorization:
$$
g(m, n) := 2^m 3^n
$$
If $2^m 3^n = 2^{m'} 3^{n'}$, unique factorization (or dividing out powers of $2$) forces $m = m'$ and $n = n'$. Since $g$ maps $\mathbb{N}^+ \times \mathbb{N}^+$ injectively onto an infinite subset of $\mathbb{N}^+$, our previous theorem guarantees $\mathbb{N}^+ \times \mathbb{N}^+$ is countably infinite!

**3. Countable Union of Countable Sets:**
For each $n \in \mathbb{N}^+$, since $A_n$ is countable, we can list its elements as $a_{n, 1}, a_{n, 2}, a_{n, 3}, \dots$ (repeating if $A_n$ is finite and non-empty). Then the map $(n, k) \mapsto a_{n, k}$ is a surjection from the countable set $\mathbb{N}^+ \times \mathbb{N}^+$ onto $\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n$. Mapping each $x \in \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n$ to the first pair $(n, k)$ that hits $x$ injects $\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n$ into $\mathbb{N}^+ \times \mathbb{N}^+ \sim \mathbb{N}^+$, proving $\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n$ is countable.

**4. Countability of $\mathbb{Q}$:**
For each positive denominator $q \in \mathbb{N}^+$, let $A_q := \left\{\frac{p}{q} : p \in \mathbb{Z}\right\}$ be the set of fractions with denominator $q$. Since $p \mapsto \frac{p}{q}$ maps $\mathbb{Z}$ onto $A_q$ and $\mathbb{Z}$ is countable, each $A_q$ is countable. Because $\mathbb{Q}$ is the countable union
$$
\mathbb{Q} = \bigcup_{q=1}^\infty A_q
$$
Part (3) immediately implies that **$\mathbb{Q}$ is countably infinite**! $\blacksquare$

## Uncountability of $\mathbb{R}$ and Existence of Irrationals

Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is densely packed on the number line and is still countable, it was natural to wonder whether $\mathbb{R}$ is also countable. In 1874 (and with his famous diagonal proof in 1891), Georg Cantor proved that **$\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable**.

We can prove the uncountability of $\mathbb{R}$ in two ways: either using decimal/binary expansions (**Cantor's Diagonal Argument**), or directly from the **Least Upper Bound Property** using nested closed intervals (which avoids having to develop decimal expansions first!). Let's give the completely self-contained Nested Intervals proof first, and then show the famous Diagonal Argument.

> [!theorem] Uncountability of $\mathbb{R}$ and the Irrational Numbers $\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$
> 1. The set of real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ (and indeed any open interval $(a, b)$ with $a < b$) is **uncountable**.
> 2. Consequently, the set of **irrational numbers** $\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$ is **uncountable**.

**Proof:**
Suppose for the sake of contradiction that $\mathbb{R}$ is countable, so we can list all real numbers in a sequence:
$$
\mathbb{R} = \{x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4, \dots\}
$$
We will construct a nested sequence of closed intervals $I_1 \supseteq I_2 \supseteq I_3 \supseteq \dots$, where $I_n = [a_n, b_n]$ with $a_n < b_n$, such that the $n$-th real number $x_n$ is **excluded** from $I_n$ ($x_n \notin I_n$):
- **Step 1:** Choose $a_1 < b_1$ such that $x_1 \notin [a_1, b_1]$ (for instance, let $a_1 = x_1 + 1$ and $b_1 = x_1 + 2$).
- **Recursive Step ($n \to n + 1$):** Given $I_n = [a_n, b_n]$ with $a_n < b_n$, trisect $I_n$ into three equal subintervals of length $\frac{b_n - a_n}{3}$:
  $$
  \left[a_n, \; a_n + \frac{b_n - a_n}{3}\right], \qquad \left[a_n + \frac{b_n - a_n}{3}, \; a_n + \frac{2(b_n - a_n)}{3}\right], \qquad \left[a_n + \frac{2(b_n - a_n)}{3}, \; b_n\right]
  $$
  The single point $x_{n+1}$ can belong to at most two of these three subintervals (if it happens to be a shared endpoint), so at least one of these three closed subintervals does **not** contain $x_{n+1}$! Define $I_{n+1} = [a_{n+1}, b_{n+1}]$ to be that subinterval, so $I_{n+1} \subseteq I_n$ and $x_{n+1} \notin I_{n+1}$.

Now examine the set of left endpoints $A := \{a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots\}$. Because our intervals are nested ($a_1 \le a_2 \le \dots \le a_n < b_n \le \dots \le b_1$), every right endpoint $b_m$ is an upper bound for $A$. By the **Least Upper Bound Property** of $\mathbb{R}$, the supremum
$$
x^* := \sup A \in \mathbb{R}
$$
exists!
- On one hand, since $a_n \in A$ for every $n$, we have $a_n \le x^*$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}^+$.
- On the other hand, since every $b_n$ is an upper bound for $A$ and $x^*$ is the *least* upper bound of $A$, we have $x^* \le b_n$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}^+$.

Thus $a_n \le x^* \le b_n$, which means **$x^* \in I_n$ for every $n \in \mathbb{N}^+$**! However, by our construction, $x_n \notin I_n$, which implies **$x^* \neq x_n$ for every $n \in \mathbb{N}^+$**—contradicting the assumption that $\{x_1, x_2, x_3, \dots\}$ listed all of $\mathbb{R}$! Therefore $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable.

Finally, for Part (2), notice that $\mathbb{R} = \mathbb{Q} \cup (\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q})$. Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable, if the irrationals $\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$ were also countable, then $\mathbb{R}$ would be the union of two countable sets and hence countable—contradicting Part (1)! Thus $\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$ is uncountable. $\blacksquare$

> [!note] Cantor's Diagonal Argument (Decimal Picture)
> Once decimal expansions are established, the exact same idea can be visualized as a 2D table of digits! Suppose all reals in $(0, 1)$ were listed as
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> x_1 &= 0.\mathbf{d_{11}} d_{12} d_{13} d_{14} \dots \\
> x_2 &= 0.d_{21} \mathbf{d_{22}} d_{23} d_{24} \dots \\
> x_3 &= 0.d_{31} d_{32} \mathbf{d_{33}} d_{34} \dots \\
> &\;\;\vdots
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> Construct a new number $x^* = 0.c_1 c_2 c_3 \dots \in (0, 1)$ by looking down the **main diagonal** $d_{nn}$ and choosing the $n$-th digit $c_n$ to be $1$ if $d_{nn} \neq 1$, and $2$ if $d_{nn} = 1$ (avoiding $0$ and $9$ so there is no $0.1999\dots = 0.2000\dots$ ambiguity). Then $x^*$ differs from $x_n$ in the $n$-th decimal place for every $n \in \mathbb{N}^+$, so $x^*$ cannot be anywhere on the list!

- State and prove that $\mathbb{R}$ satisfies the Least Upper Bound Property by constructing $\sup A$ explicitly for any non-empty bounded-above subset $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ and verifying all cut and supremum conditions.

### Addition of Dedekind Cuts

**[Addition, Zero, and Negation of Cuts]**
- Define the sum $\alpha + \beta$, the zero cut $0^*$, and the additive inverse $-\alpha$ for Dedekind cuts $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$, and explain why the definition of $-\alpha$ requires the strict margin $-p - r \notin \alpha$ ($r > 0$).

**[Additive Group Structure of $\mathbb{R}$ and Order Compatibility]**
- Prove that $\alpha + \beta$ and $-\alpha$ are valid Dedekind cuts, verify that $(\mathbb{R}, +, 0^*)$ forms an abelian group satisfying $\alpha + (-\alpha) = 0^*$, and prove that $\alpha < \beta \implies \alpha + \gamma < \beta + \gamma$.

### Multiplication of Dedekind Cuts and the Complete Ordered Field

**[Multiplication of Dedekind Cuts]**
- Explain why naive pairwise multiplication of all elements in two cuts fails, define $\alpha \cdot \beta$ and $\alpha^{-1}$ for positive cuts $\alpha, \beta > 0^*$, and extend multiplication to all cuts in $\mathbb{R}$ by sign cases.

**[$\mathbb{R}$ Is a Complete Ordered Field Extending $\mathbb{Q}$]**
- Prove that $\mathbb{R}$ is a complete ordered field and that the map $r \mapsto r^*$ is an order-preserving field embedding of $\mathbb{Q}$ into $\mathbb{R}$.

### Rewards of Completeness: The Archimedean Property, Density of $\mathbb{Q}$, and $n$-th Roots

**[The Archimedean Property of $\mathbb{R}$]**
- State and prove the Archimedean Property of $\mathbb{R}$ using the Least Upper Bound Property.

**[Density of $\mathbb{Q}$ in $\mathbb{R}$]**
- State and prove that between any two real numbers $x < y$, there exists a rational number $r \in \mathbb{Q}$ with $x < r < y$.

**[Existence and Uniqueness of Positive $n$-th Roots in $\mathbb{R}$]**
- State and prove using the Least Upper Bound Property that for every real $y > 0$ and every integer $n \in \mathbb{N}^+$, there exists a unique real $x > 0$ such that $x^n = y$.

## 0.6 Absolute Value, Distance, and the Extended Reals

### Absolute Value and the Triangle Inequality

**[Absolute Value and Distance on $\mathbb{R}$]**
- Define the absolute value $|x|$, the distance $d(x, y) = |x - y|$, and the $\varepsilon$-neighborhood $V_\varepsilon(a)$ on $\mathbb{R}$.

**[Fundamental Properties of Absolute Value and the Triangle Inequality]**
- State and prove the sandwich equivalence ($|x| \le c \iff -c \le x \le c$), the Triangle Inequality, the Reverse Triangle Inequality, and the metric triangle inequality for distance.

### The $\varepsilon$-Principle and Supremum Characterization

**[The $\varepsilon$-Principle for Inequalities and Equality]**
- State and prove that if $a \le b + \varepsilon$ for all $\varepsilon > 0$ then $a \le b$, and if $|a - b| < \varepsilon$ for all $\varepsilon > 0$ then $a = b$.

**[The $\varepsilon$-Characterization of the Supremum]**
- State and prove the necessary and sufficient $\varepsilon$-condition for an upper bound $\alpha$ of $E$ to equal $\sup E$.

### Infimums and the Extended Real Number System

**[Existence of Infimums (Greatest Lower Bound Property of $\mathbb{R}$)]**
- State and prove that every non-empty subset $E \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ bounded below has an infimum $\inf E \in \mathbb{R}$ by relating $\inf E$ to $\sup(-E)$.

**[The Extended Real Number System $\overline{\mathbb{R}}$]**
- Define the extended real number system $\overline{\mathbb{R}} = \mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty, +\infty\}$, define $\sup E$ and $\inf E$ for unbounded sets, and explain why $\overline{\mathbb{R}}$ is not a field.
