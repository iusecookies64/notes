# 1. Introduction: Bridging from Electrons to Logic

### 1.1 Demystifying the Computer Stack
In Lecture 1, we established that computers exist to **solve problems and gain insight** (Hamming's dictum). To bridge the semantic gap between human problem formulations and physical electron flows, computing systems rely on the **transformation hierarchy**:

```
  [ Problems ] ──► [ Algorithms ] ──► [ Programs ] ──► [ System Software ]
                                                              │
  [ Microarchitecture ] ◄── [ Instruction Set Architecture (ISA) ]
         │
  [ Digital Logic ] ──► [ Devices (Transistors) ] ──► [ Electrons ]
```

The fundamental goal of this course is to **eliminate the "magic"** across every boundary of this hierarchy. High-level programming languages, operating systems, and artificial intelligence models are not mystical entities; they are abstractions realized by orchestrating billions of electrical switches configured in precise logical patterns.

In this lecture, we begin our upward climb from the bottom of the stack:
1. Examine the **MOS Transistor** as a physical switching device.
2. Combine transistors into **CMOS Logic Gates** implementing Boolean primitives.
3. Interconnect logic gates to construct modular **Combinational Logic Circuits**.
4. Formalize circuit behavior using **Boolean Algebra** and canonical representations.

### 1.2 The Scale of Modern Silicon
Modern microprocessors and memory chips integrate mind-boggling numbers of microscopic transistors:
- **Intel 4004 (1971)**: The first commercial microprocessor integrated **2,300 MOS transistors**.
- **Intel Pentium 4 (2000)**: Scaled to **42 million MOS transistors**.
- **Apple M2 Max (2022)**: Integrates over **67 billion MOS transistors** on a single die.
- **Micron 3D V-NAND Flash (2022)**: 3D-stacked non-volatile memory chips contain over **5 trillion MOS transistors**.

Despite this astronomical density, every single one of these components operates on the exact same elementary building block: the **Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor (MOS) Transistor**.

# 2. The MOS Transistor as a Switch

### 2.1 Physical Composition & Abstraction Boundary
A **MOS Transistor** is fabricated by combining three classes of physical materials:
1. **Metal** (Conductor): Used for interconnect wires and the control gate electrode.
2. **Oxide** (Insulator, typically Silicon Dioxide $\text{SiO}_2$ or High-$\kappa$ dielectrics): Electrically isolates the gate terminal from the semiconductor channel.
3. **Semiconductor** (Silicon substrate doped with impurities): Provides the conducting channel between source and drain.
![](Pasted%20image%2020260924134139.png)
*Figure 2.1: Scanning electron microscope (SEM) cross-section of a physical MOS transistor showing the Gate, Source, and Drain (Source: Slide 10).*

#### The Three Terminals
- **Gate ($G$)**: The control terminal. Applying a voltage creates an electric field across the oxide insulator that either permits or blocks conduction.
- **Source ($S$)**: The terminal where charge carriers enter the conducting channel.
- **Drain ($D$)**: The terminal where charge carriers exit the conducting channel.

> [!NOTE]
> **Abstraction Boundary**:  
> In solid-state physics and microelectronics (H&H Sections 1.6–1.7), transistors exhibit continuous, complex analog behavior governed by semiconductor physics (drift currents, threshold voltages, capacitance, subthreshold conduction). In digital computer design, we abstract away the analog physics and model the transistor strictly as an **electrically controlled binary switch**.

### 2.2 The Wall Switch Analogy
Consider a standard household wall switch connected to a lightbulb and a power supply:

```
  [ Power Supply (+) ] ─── [ Switch ] ─── [ Lamp ] ─── [ Power Supply (-) ]
```

- For the lamp to glow, electrons must flow continuously through a **closed circuit** from the power supply, through the lamp, and back to the power supply.
- Manipulating the switch mechanically makes (closes) or breaks (opens) the conduction path.
- In digital hardware, we replace the mechanical wall switch with an **electronically controlled MOS transistor**.

### 2.3 The Two Types of MOS Transistors: nMOS and pMOS

Modern digital circuits utilize two complementary types of transistors: **n-type (nMOS)** and **p-type (pMOS)**. They operate in exact opposition to one another.
![](Pasted%20image%2020260924134341.png)
*Figure 2.2: Electrical switch abstraction for n-type and p-type MOS transistors (Source: Slide 14).*

```
        nMOS Transistor                   pMOS Transistor
             Drain                             Drain
               │                                 │
               ├──┐                              ├──┐
      Gate ────┤  │                     Gate ──o─┤  │  (Inversion Bubble)
               ├──┘                              ├──┘
               │                                 │
             Source                            Source
```

#### 1. n-type MOS (nMOS)
- **Control Behavior**:
  - Gate supplied with **High Voltage ($V_{DD} = 3\text{V}$, Logical 1)** $\to$ **Switch is CLOSED (ON)**. The channel conducts; source and drain act as a connected wire.
  - Gate supplied with **Low Voltage ($0\text{V}$, Logical 0)** $\to$ **Switch is OPEN (OFF)**. The connection is broken; no current flows.
- **Carrier Type**: Conduction is carried by **electrons** (negative charges).
- **Electrical Property**: Electrons have high mobility. nMOS transistors are exceptionally effective at conducting low voltages to ground. Therefore, nMOS transistors are **strong conductors of 0s, but poor conductors of 1s** (a high voltage passed through an nMOS drops by a threshold voltage $V_{th}$).

#### 2. p-type MOS (pMOS)
- **Control Behavior**:
  - The schematic symbol includes an **inversion bubble** on the gate terminal.
  - Gate supplied with **Low Voltage ($0\text{V}$, Logical 0)** $\to$ **Switch is CLOSED (ON)**. Conduction path is established.
  - Gate supplied with **High Voltage ($V_{DD} = 3\text{V}$, Logical 1)** $\to$ **Switch is OPEN (OFF)**. Broken circuit.
- **Carrier Type**: Conduction is carried by **holes** (positive charge vacancies).
- **Electrical Property**: Holes have lower mobility than electrons. pMOS transistors are exceptionally effective at conducting high voltages from the power rail. Therefore, pMOS transistors are **strong conductors of 1s, but poor conductors of 0s** (a low voltage passed through a pMOS fails to reach true $0\text{V}$).

| Transistor Type | Gate Voltage = High ($V_{DD}$ / Logical 1) | Gate Voltage = Low ($0\text{V}$ / Logical 0) | Conduction Strength | Preferred Circuit Role |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **nMOS** | **ON (Closed)** | **OFF (Open)** | Passes **0 strongly**, 1 weakly | **Pull-Down Network** (to $GND$) |
| **pMOS** | **OFF (Open)** | **ON (Closed)** | Passes **1 strongly**, 0 weakly | **Pull-Up Network** (to $V_{DD}$) |

# 3. Complementary MOS (CMOS) Technology & Gate Design

### 3.1 Why CMOS? The Need for Complementary Pairs
If an engineer attempts to construct logic using only nMOS transistors (as was common in early 1970s nMOS-only logic), pulling the output to $V_{DD}$ results in a severely degraded voltage level (typically $V_{DD} - V_{th} \approx 1.5\text{V}$ instead of $3\text{V}$), ruining noise margins and signal integrity. Conversely, a pMOS-only design cannot pull down to $0\text{V}$ cleanly.

To achieve robust, high-speed, rail-to-rail digital signals ($0\text{V}$ to $V_{DD}$), modern digital electronics universally adopt **Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor (CMOS)** technology, combining both nMOS and pMOS devices on the same silicon die.

### 3.2 General CMOS Gate Structure

Every static CMOS inverting logic gate adheres to a standardized dual-network architecture:
![](Pasted%20image%2020260924134440.png)
*Figure 3.1: The generalized structure of a static CMOS logic gate (Source: Slide 27).*

#### Topological Rules
1. **Pull-Up Network (PUN)**:
   - Built **strictly with pMOS transistors**.
   - Connected between the power supply ($V_{DD}$) and the output node ($Y$).
   - Responsible for pulling the output up to logic high ($V_{DD}$).
1. **Pull-Down Network (PDN)**:
   - Built **strictly with nMOS transistors**.
   - Connected between the output node ($Y$) and ground ($GND$).
   - Responsible for pulling the output down to logic low ($0\text{V}$).
3. **Series vs. Parallel Conduction**:
   - **Parallel Connection**: The network conducts if **at least one** transistor is ON.
   - **Series Connection**: The network conducts **if and only if all** transistors in the series chain are ON.

#### The Rule of Mutual Exclusion (Complementary Operation)
In correct CMOS operation, **exactly one network must conduct at any given time**:
- **PUN is ON and PDN is OFF** $\implies$ Output is connected to $V_{DD}$ ($Y = 1$).
- **PUN is OFF and PDN is ON** $\implies$ Output is connected to $GND$ ($Y = 0$).

> [!CAUTION]
> **Illegal CMOS States**:
> 1. **Both Networks ON (Short Circuit)**: A direct low-resistance path connects $V_{DD}$ directly to $GND$. This causes massive current surge, destroys voltage levels, and can physically overheat or permanently burn the silicon die.
> 2. **Both Networks OFF (Floating Output / High-Z)**: The output node is completely disconnected from both power and ground. The voltage floats at an undefined state, easily corrupted by electromagnetic noise and capacitive coupling. (Note: Intentionally floating states are utilized in *tri-state buffers* for shared buses, but are forbidden in standard logic gates).

### 3.3 The CMOS Inverter (NOT Gate)
The simplest logic structure in CMOS technology is the inverter, requiring exactly **2 transistors** (1 pMOS, 1 nMOS).
![](Pasted%20image%2020260924134539.png)
*Figure 3.2: Transistor schematic, logic symbol, and truth table of a CMOS Inverter / NOT Gate (Source: Slide 21).*

#### Operation Walkthrough
- **Input $A = 0\text{V}$ (Logical 0)**:
  - Gate of pMOS receives $0\text{V} \implies$ pMOS is **ON** (closed).
  - Gate of nMOS receives $0\text{V} \implies$ nMOS is **OFF** (open).
  - Output $Y$ is connected through the pMOS to $V_{DD}$ ($3\text{V}$) and isolated from ground.
  - Result: $Y = 3\text{V}$ (**Logical 1**).
- **Input $A = 3\text{V}$ (Logical 1)**:
  - Gate of pMOS receives $3\text{V} \implies$ pMOS is **OFF** (open).
  - Gate of nMOS receives $3\text{V} \implies$ nMOS is **ON** (closed).
  - Output $Y$ is connected through the nMOS to $GND$ ($0\text{V}$) and isolated from $V_{DD}$.
  - Result: $Y = 0\text{V}$ (**Logical 0**).

$$\text{Boolean Equation: } Y = \overline{A}$$

#### Truth Table: NOT Gate
| Input $A$ | pMOS State | nMOS State | Output Voltage | Output $Y$ |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **0** ($0\text{V}$) | ON | OFF | $3\text{V}$ | **1** |
| **1** ($3\text{V}$) | OFF | ON | $0\text{V}$ | **0** |

> [!NOTE]
> **Inversion Bubble Notation**:  
> In digital schematic diagrams, an **inversion bubble** (a small circle) represents logical NOT. A triangle without a bubble represents a non-inverting **Buffer** ($Y = A$). A triangle with an inversion bubble represents an **Inverter** ($Y = \overline{A}$).

### 3.4 The CMOS NAND Gate
To perform multi-input operations, we combine transistors in series and parallel. A 2-input NAND gate requires **4 transistors** (2 pMOS in parallel, 2 nMOS in series).

![CMOS NAND Gate](page-24.png)
*Figure 3.3: Schematic, logic symbol, and truth table of a 2-input CMOS NAND Gate (Source: Slide 24).*

#### Circuit Topology
- **Pull-Up Network (PUN)**: Transistors $P_1$ and $P_2$ connected in **parallel** between $V_{DD}$ and output $Y$. If *either* $A=0$ or $B=0$, the corresponding pMOS turns ON, pulling $Y$ to $3\text{V}$ (1).
- **Pull-Down Network (PDN)**: Transistors $N_1$ and $N_2$ connected in **series** between output $Y$ and $GND$. *Both* $A=1$ and $B=1$ must be asserted simultaneously to complete the conduction path to $0\text{V}$ (0).

#### Truth Table & Operation: NAND Gate
| $A$ | $B$ | $P_1$ (pMOS) | $P_2$ (pMOS) | $N_1$ (nMOS) | $N_2$ (nMOS) | Output Voltage | Output $Y$ |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **0** | **0** | ON | ON | OFF | OFF | $3\text{V}$ | **1** |
| **0** | **1** | ON | OFF | OFF | ON | $3\text{V}$ | **1** |
| **1** | **0** | OFF | ON | ON | OFF | $3\text{V}$ | **1** |
| **1** | **1** | OFF | OFF | ON | ON | $0\text{V}$ | **0** |

$$\text{Boolean Equation: } Y = \overline{A \cdot B} = \overline{AB}$$

The output evaluates to 0 **if and only if** both inputs $A$ and $B$ are 1.

### 3.5 The CMOS AND Gate: Why Inverting Logic Dominates
How do we construct an AND gate in CMOS? In digital logic, an AND gate is simply an inverted NAND:

$$Y = A \cdot B = \overline{\overline{A \cdot B}}$$

Therefore, a CMOS AND gate is implemented by cascading a **2-input NAND gate** with a **CMOS Inverter**.
![](Pasted%20image%2020260924134617.png)
*Figure 3.4: CMOS AND gate constructed from a NAND gate followed by an inverter (Source: Slide 25).*

- **Transistor Count**: 4 transistors (NAND) + 2 transistors (Inverter) = **6 transistors**.

#### Food for Thought: Can We Build AND with Only 4 Transistors?
A student might intuitively ask: *Why not place 2 nMOS transistors in series in the PUN and 2 pMOS transistors in parallel in the PDN to directly produce AND with only 4 transistors?*

Prof. Mutlu emphasizes why this fails:
1. **Weak-1 Problem**: nMOS transistors conduct electrons, not holes. If nMOS devices are placed in the pull-up network, the output voltage cannot reach $V_{DD}$; it caps at $V_{DD} - V_{th}$ (e.g., $\approx 1.5\text{V}$ instead of $3\text{V}$).
2. **Weak-0 Problem**: pMOS devices in the pull-down network cannot pull down to true ground; the output node stalls at $|V_{tp}|$.
3. **Signal Degradation**: Connecting such degraded outputs to downstream gates causes immense static leakage current, collapses noise margins, and induces circuit failure.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Core Axiom of CMOS Design**:  
> **CMOS is inherently an inverting logic family.**  
> Naturally occurring single-stage CMOS gates are always inverting: **NOT, NAND, NOR, XNOR, AOI (AND-OR-Invert)**. Non-inverting functions (**Buffer, AND, OR, XOR**) inherently require an additional inverter stage, increasing transistor count and propagation latency.

### 3.6 The CMOS NOR Gate
By symmetry, swapping the series and parallel topologies yields a **NOR gate**:
- **Pull-Up Network (PUN)**: 2 pMOS transistors connected in **series** between $V_{DD}$ and output $Y$.
- **Pull-Down Network (PDN)**: 2 nMOS transistors connected in **parallel** between output $Y$ and $GND$.
- **Operation**:
  - If either $A=1$ or $B=1$, a parallel nMOS conducts, pulling $Y$ down to $0\text{V}$ (0).
  - Only when *both* $A=0$ and $B=0$ do both series pMOS devices conduct, pulling $Y$ up to $V_{DD}$ (1).
$$\text{Boolean Equation: } Y = \overline{A + B}$$

# 4. Physical Circuit Constraints: Latency, Power & Energy

Digital abstraction treats gate switching as instantaneous and zero-cost. In physical silicon, every transition is governed by electronic constraints.

### 4.1 Latency: Series vs. Parallel Transistors
Which connection is slower: transistors in series or transistors in parallel?

```
      Series Connection (Slower)          Parallel Connection (Faster)
                 │                                    │
               ┌─┴─┐                                ┌─┴─┐
               │ R │                                │   │
               └─┬─┘                              ┌─┴─┐ ┌─┴─┐
                 │                                │ R │ │ R │
               ┌─┴─┐                              └─┬─┘ └─┬─┘
               │ R │                                │   │
               └─┬─┘                                └──┬──┘
                 │                                     │
           R_total = 2R                           R_total = R
```

- **Transistors in Series**: Slower. For a series chain of $N$ transistors to conduct, current must flow through $N$ individual channels in sequence. Channel resistances sum linearly ($R_{\text{total}} = \sum R_i$). The resulting $RC$ time constant to charge or discharge parasitic wire and gate capacitances is significantly larger, increasing propagation delay.
- **Transistors in Parallel**: Faster. If one transistor turns ON, current flows through a single channel resistance $R$.
- **Architectural Consequence**: Designers avoid high fan-in gates (e.g., 8-input NANDs) because stringing 8 nMOS transistors in series creates unacceptable propagation delay. Instead, wide operations are decomposed into trees of smaller 2-input or 3-input gates.

### 4.2 Power and Energy Consumption
Power dissipation dictates chip packaging, heat dissipation, battery life, and data center operating costs.

#### 1. Dynamic Power Consumption
Dynamic power is dissipated whenever signals switch logic states ($0 \to 1$ or $1 \to 0$), charging and discharging capacitive loads:

$$P_{\text{dynamic}} = C \cdot V_{DD}^2 \cdot f$$

- $C$: Total load capacitance of the switching node (transistor gates + interconnect wires).
- $V_{DD}$: Power supply voltage.
- $f$: Switching frequency (clock rate).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The Voltage Penalty**:  
> Notice that dynamic power scales **quadratically** ($V_{DD}^2$) with supply voltage. Furthermore, because maximum achievable transistor switching frequency $f$ is itself roughly proportional to voltage, power exhibits an effective **cubic relationship** ($P \propto V^3$).  
> Reducing $V_{DD}$ (e.g., from $3\text{V}$ in early technologies down to $\approx 0.8\text{V}$ today) provides dramatic power savings, but severely degrades switching speed and noise margins below certain thresholds.

#### 2. Static Power Consumption (Leakage)
Static power is dissipated continuously, even when the circuit is completely idle and no signals are switching:
$$
P_{\text{static}} = V_{DD} \cdot I_{\text{leakage}}
$$
- Even when turned OFF, sub-micron transistors are imperfect switches; minute **subthreshold leakage currents** flow between source and drain, and quantum tunneling leaks electrons across thin gate oxides.
- With aggressive dimensional scaling, static leakage power has escalated from an afterthought to over **$30\text{--}40\%$ of total chip power**.
- **Mitigation — Power Gating**: Inserting large "sleep" header or footer transistors to physically disconnect idle execution units (e.g., turning off an unused vector multiplier or floating-point unit) from the power rail.

#### 3. Energy vs. Peak Power (TDP)
It is critical to distinguish between **Energy** and **Power**:

```
  Power (Watts)
       ▲
       │        Peak Power / TDP Constraint
  ─────┼──────────────────────────────────────── (Thermal / Delivery Limit)
       │           ┌──────────┐
       │   Dynamic │ Activity │
       │   Power   │  Spikes  │   ┌────────┐
       │           │          └───┘        │
  ─────┼───────────┴───────────────────────┴──── Static Leakage Baseline
       │
       └────────────────────────────────────────► Time (t)
       ◄────────────── Energy = Area Under Curve ──────────────►
```

- **Energy ($E = \int P(t) \, dt \approx P \times \Delta t$)**: Total electrical work done (measured in Joules). Determines **battery life** in mobile devices and the total kilowatt-hour utility bill of a data center.
- **Peak Power / Thermal Design Power (TDP)**: The maximum rate of heat dissipation (measured in Watts) that the cooling cooling assembly (heatsink, fan, vapor chamber) and power delivery grid can sustain. Exceeding TDP triggers thermal throttling or emergency shutdown.
- **Dark Silicon**: Because modern chips can fabricate vastly more transistors than can be simultaneously powered within the TDP budget, a substantial fraction of the chip must remain unpowered ("dark") at any given instant.

# 5. Moore's Law: Enabler of Exponential Integration

The extraordinary capability of modern digital computing was catalyzed by the empirical observation known as **Moore's Law**.
![](Pasted%20image%2020260924134847.png)
*Figure 5.1: Gordon Moore's original 1965 plots: component counts doubling annually/biennially (left), and the economic minimum of manufacturing cost per component shifting downward and to the right (right) (Source: Slide 35).*

### 5.1 Gordon Moore's 1965 Observation
In his seminal 3-page paper (*"Cramming more components onto integrated circuits"*, Electronics Magazine, 1965), Gordon Moore (co-founder of Intel) made two profound observations:
1. **Exponential Component Scaling**: The number of components (transistors and resistors) integrated onto a single silicon chip doubles approximately every two years.
2. **Economic Scaling**: As component density increases, the manufacturing cost per individual component drops exponentially. The "sweet spot" of minimum cost per component shifts toward increasingly dense integrated circuits over time.

#### Remarkable Historical Foresight
Moore presciently wrote in 1965:
> *"With unit cost falling as the number of components per circuit rises, by 1975 economics may dictate squeezing as many as 65,000 components on a single silicon chip."*

While 65,000 components seemed unimaginable in 1965, the industry vastly surpassed it—reaching over **67 billion components** on the Apple M2 Max in 2022.

Moore also anticipated the thermal wall:
> *"Will it be possible to remove the heat generated by tens of thousands of components in a single silicon chip?"*

### 5.2 Innovations Sustaining Scaling at the Bottom
Continuing Moore's Law has required unrelenting materials and manufacturing breakthroughs:
1. **Precision Manufacturing**: Transitioning from optical lithography to **Extreme Ultraviolet (EUV) Lithography** ($\lambda = 13.5\text{nm}$) to print features measuring only a few nanometers across (a few silicon atoms wide).
2. **Advanced Materials**:
   - Replacing aluminum interconnects with **copper** (lower electrical resistance).
   - Introducing **Hafnium Oxide ($\text{HfO}_2$)** high-$\kappa$ gate dielectrics to arrest gate leakage while maintaining capacitive coupling.
3. **Novel 3D Transistor Architectures**:
   - *Planar MOSFET*: Used from 1960 to 2011; abandoned when short-channel leakage became uncontrollable.
   - *FinFET (3D Tri-Gate)*: Raised channel "fin" wrapped on three sides by the gate electrode, providing superior electrostatic channel control.
   - *Gate-All-Around (GAA) / Nanosheet*: Gate material surrounds horizontal silicon nanosheets on all four sides, enabling continued scaling below 3nm.

# 6. Combinational Logic Circuits & Functional Specification

With our understanding of gates established, we now group them into larger logical systems.

### 6.1 Circuit Classification: Combinational vs. Sequential Logic
Digital circuits are divided into two fundamental classes:

1. **Combinational Logic Circuits**:
   - **Memoryless**: The circuit has no internal storage or memory elements.
   - **Strict Dependence**: Outputs at any instant are strictly a mathematical function of the current combination of input values applied to the circuit right now:
	$$
	\text{Outputs} = F(\text{Current Inputs})
	$$
1. **Sequential Logic Circuits**:
   - **Has Memory / State**: Contains feedback loops or internal storage elements (latches, flip-flops, registers).
   - **Historical Dependence**: Outputs depend on both current inputs and the historical sequence of past inputs (the stored state):
	 $$
	 \text{Outputs} = G(\text{Current Inputs}, \text{Stored State})
	 $$

### 6.2 Circuit Specifications
A complete hardware specification requires two descriptions:
1. **Functional Specification**: Describes the input-output transformation mapping without regard to physical time. Every unique combination of inputs yields an identical output value every time.
2. **Timing Specification**: Describes physical signal propagation delays—the time elapsed between an input voltage transition and the corresponding output stabilization (propagation delay $t_{pd}$, contamination delay $t_{cd}$).

### 6.3 Functional Specification Example: 1-Bit Full Adder
![](Pasted%20image%2020260924135040.png)
*Figure 6.1: Functional specification and block diagram of a 1-bit Full Adder (Source: Slide 53).*

A 1-bit full adder accepts two operand bits $A$ and $B$, plus a carry-in bit $C_{\text{in}}$, and produces a sum bit $S$ and carry-out bit $C_{\text{out}}$:

- **Sum Bit ($S$)**: 3-input XOR (odd parity function):
	$$
	S = A \oplus B \oplus C_{\text{in}}
	$$
- **Carry-Out Bit ($C_{\text{out}}$)**: 3-input **Majority Function** (evaluates to 1 if at least two inputs are 1):
	$$
	C_{\text{out}} = A \cdot B + A \cdot C_{\text{in}} + B \cdot C_{\text{in}}
	$$

# 7. Boolean Algebra: Axioms, Theorems & Dualities

To manipulate, optimize, and synthesize combinational logic, computer engineers rely on **Boolean Algebra**, formulated by George Boole in 1847 (*The Mathematical Analysis of Logic*).

### 7.1 Formal Axioms
Boolean algebra operates over the two-element set $B = \{0, 1\}$ using three primitive operations: **AND** ($\cdot$), **OR** ($+$), and **NOT** ($\overline{A}$ or $\sim A$).

The system is defined by six fundamental axioms:

| Axiom Number | Axiom Name | Statement (Form $i$) | Statement (Form $ii$) | Meaning |
| :---: | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **A1** | **Two Elements** | $B$ contains at least two elements, $0$ and $1$, such that $0 \neq 1$ | — | Binary domain |
| **A2** | **Closure** | For $a, b \in B$: $a + b \in B$ | For $a, b \in B$: $a \cdot b \in B$ | Operations remain within $\{0,1\}$ |
| **A3** | **Commutative Laws** | $a + b = b + a$ | $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$ | Input ordering does not matter |
| **A4** | **Identities** | $a + 0 = a$ | $a \cdot 1 = a$ | Identity elements ($0$ for OR, $1$ for AND) |
| **A5** | **Distributive Laws** | $a + (b \cdot c) = (a + b) \cdot (a + c)$ | $a \cdot (b + c) = (a \cdot b) + (a \cdot c)$ | $+$ distributes over $\cdot$; $\cdot$ distributes over $+$ |
| **A6** | **Complement** | $a + \overline{a} = 1$ | $a \cdot \overline{a} = 0$ | Operating with complement yields identities |

> [!NOTE]
> **Distributivity in Boolean vs. Real Algebra**:  
> In conventional real algebra, multiplication distributes over addition ($a \cdot (b+c) = ab + ac$), but addition **does not** distribute over multiplication ($a + bc \neq (a+b)(a+c)$). In Boolean algebra, **both operations distribute over each other** (Axiom 5).

### 7.2 The Principle of Duality
Every axiom and theorem in Boolean algebra occurs in **dual pairs**.

> **The Duality Principle**:  
> If any Boolean expression or equation is algebraically true, its **dual form** is also guaranteed to be true.  
> The dual is formed by:
> 1. Replacing every **AND** ($\cdot$) operation with an **OR** ($+$).
> 2. Replacing every **OR** ($+$) operation with an **AND** ($\cdot$).
> 3. Replacing every constant **$1$** with a constant **$0$**.
> 4. Replacing every constant **$0$** with a constant **$1$**.
> 5. **Leaving all variable literals and complements completely unchanged.**

**Example**:
$$\text{Original: } a + (b \cdot c) = (a + b) \cdot (a + c) \implies \text{Dual: } a \cdot (b + c) = (a \cdot b) + (a \cdot c)$$

### 7.3 Essential Laws & Simplification Theorems
From the axioms, we derive practical transformation theorems:

| Law | Standard Form | Dual Form | Engineering Intuition |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Operations with 0 and 1** | $X + 0 = X$ <br> $X + 1 = 1$ | $X \cdot 1 = X$ <br> $X \cdot 0 = 0$ | $0$ is identity for OR, annihilator for AND; <br> $1$ is identity for AND, annihilator for OR |
| **Idempotent Law** | $X + X = X$ | $X \cdot X = X$ | Redundant inputs have no effect |
| **Involution Law** | $\overline{\overline{X}} = X$ | — | Double negation cancels |
| **Complementarity** | $X + \overline{X} = 1$ | $X \cdot \overline{X} = 0$ | A variable ORed/ANDed with its opposite |
| **Associative Laws** | $(X + Y) + Z = X + (Y + Z)$ | $(X \cdot Y) \cdot Z = X \cdot (Y \cdot Z)$ | Grouping order / parenthesization does not matter |
| **Combining (Adjacency)** | $X \cdot Y + X \cdot \overline{Y} = X$ | $(X + Y) \cdot (X + \overline{Y}) = X$ | **The Uniting Theorem**: if $Y$ toggles while $X$ stays constant, $Y$ is irrelevant |
| **Absorption Law** | $X + X \cdot Y = X$ | $X \cdot (X + Y) = X$ | The wider product term is completely absorbed |
| **Elimination Law** | $(X + \overline{Y}) \cdot Y = X \cdot Y$ | $(X \cdot \overline{Y}) + Y = X + Y$ | Simplifies mixed terms |

### 7.4 Algebraic Proof Walkthroughs

#### Proof 1: The Combining (Uniting) Theorem
$$
\text{Prove that: } X \cdot Y + X \cdot \overline{Y} = X
$$
1. Factor out $X$ using Distributivity (A5):
$$
X \cdot (Y + \overline{Y})
$$
2. Apply Complementarity (A6): since $Y + \overline{Y} = 1$:
$$
X \cdot 1
$$
3. Apply Identity (A4):
$$
X \quad \blacksquare
$$

#### Proof 2: The Absorption Law
$$
\text{Prove that: } X + X \cdot Y = X
$$
1. Apply Identity (A4) to express $X$ as $X \cdot 1$:
$$
X \cdot 1 + X \cdot Y
$$
2. Factor out $X$ using Distributivity (A5):
$$
X \cdot (1 + Y)
$$
3. Commutativity and Operations with 1: since $1 + Y = 1$:
$$
X \cdot 1
$$
4. Apply Identity (A4):
$$
X \quad \blacksquare
$$

> [!TIP]
> **Hardware Implication of Absorption**:  
> If an architect writes $F = X + XY$, synthesizing this naively requires an AND gate and an OR gate. Recognizing the absorption law allows the compiler or designer to replace both gates with a **bare wire directly connected to $X$**, saving silicon area, latency, and dynamic power.

### 7.5 De Morgan's Laws & Gate Transformations

De Morgan's Laws provide the mathematical mechanism to invert complex expressions and transform gate types:

$$
1. \quad \overline{X_1 + X_2 + \dots + X_N} = \overline{X_1} \cdot \overline{X_2} \cdot \dots \cdot \overline{X_N}
$$
$$
2. \quad \overline{X_1 \cdot X_2 \cdot \dots \cdot X_N} = \overline{X_1} + \overline{X_2} + \dots + \overline{X_N}
$$

#### Natural Language Intuition
- *"If at least one of $A, B, C$ is TRUE, then it is NOT the case that $A, B, C$ are all FALSE."*
![](Pasted%20image%2020260924135432.png)
*Figure 7.1: De Morgan's Laws and graphical "bubble pushing" gate equivalences (Source: Slide 62).*

#### Graphical "Bubble Pushing" Equivalences
1. **NOR Gate Equivalence**:
	$$
	\overline{X + Y} = \overline{X} \cdot \overline{Y}
	$$
   A NOR gate is functionally identical to an **AND gate with inverted inputs**.
2. **NAND Gate Equivalence**:
	$$
	\overline{X \cdot Y} = \overline{X} + \overline{Y}
	$$
   A NAND gate is functionally identical to an **OR gate with inverted inputs**.

#### Why This Matters in Hardware
In a semiconductor standard-cell library, NAND gates are smaller and significantly faster than NOR gates (due to parallel pMOS and series nMOS). Using De Morgan's laws, Electronic Design Automation (EDA) synthesis tools can restructure an entire circuit to use only the fastest and most power-efficient gate types without changing the logical specification.

# 8. Canonical Representations: SOP and POS Forms

A given Boolean function can be expressed in an infinite number of algebraically equivalent equations. To create unique mathematical signatures and facilitate automated CAD synthesis, we use **canonical (standardized) forms**.

### 8.1 Key Definitions
For an $N$-input function:
- **Literal**: A variable in its uncomplemented form ($A$) or complemented form ($\overline{A}$).
- **Product Term (Implicant)**: An AND combination of one or more literals (e.g., $A\overline{B}$, $ABC$).
- **Sum Term**: An OR combination of one or more literals (e.g., $A + \overline{B}$, $A + B + C$).
- **Minterm ($m_i$)**: A product term containing **every single one of the $N$ input variables** exactly once (complemented or uncomplemented). A minterm evaluates to 1 for **exactly one** row of the truth table.
- **Maxterm ($M_i$)**: A sum term containing **every single one of the $N$ input variables** exactly once. A maxterm evaluates to 0 for **exactly one** row of the truth table.

### 8.2 Canonical Sum of Products (SOP) Form
The **Sum of Products (SOP)** form (also known as *Disjunctive Normal Form* or *Minterm Expansion*) represents a function as an **OR (Sum)** of individual **AND (Products / Minterms)**.

#### Systematic Construction Procedure
1. Construct the truth table for the function.
2. Identify all rows where the output evaluates to **1** (the *ON-set*).
3. For each such row, write its corresponding **minterm**:
   - If an input variable is $0$, write it as a complemented literal ($\overline{X}$).
   - If an input variable is $1$, write it as an uncomplemented literal ($X$).
1. **OR ($+$)** all generated minterms together.

#### Worked Example: 3-Variable Truth Table
![](Pasted%20image%2020260924135555.png)
*Figure 8.1: Constructing the Canonical Sum of Products (SOP) form from truth table minterms (Source: Slide 70).*

Consider the 3-input function $F(A, B, C)$:

| Row | Binary ($ABC$) | $A$ | $B$ | $C$ | Output $F$ | Minterm Symbol | Minterm Algebraic Expression |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :--- |
| **0** | $000_2$ | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | $m_0$ | $\overline{A}\,\overline{B}\,\overline{C}$ |
| **1** | $001_2$ | 0 | 0 | 1 | 0 | $m_1$ | $\overline{A}\,\overline{B}\,C$ |
| **2** | $010_2$ | 0 | 1 | 0 | 0 | $m_2$ | $\overline{A}\,B\,\overline{C}$ |
| **3** | $011_2$ | 0 | 1 | 1 | **1** | $m_3$ | $\overline{A}\,B\,C$ |
| **4** | $100_2$ | 1 | 0 | 0 | **1** | $m_4$ | $A\,\overline{B}\,\overline{C}$ |
| **5** | $101_2$ | 1 | 0 | 1 | **1** | $m_5$ | $A\,\overline{B}\,C$ |
| **6** | $110_2$ | 1 | 1 | 0 | **1** | $m_6$ | $A\,B\,\overline{C}$ |
| **7** | $111_2$ | 1 | 1 | 1 | **1** | $m_7$ | $A\,B\,C$ |

The function is 1 at rows 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7:

$$
\text{Canonical SOP Form: } F(A, B, C) = \overline{A}BC + A\overline{B}\overline{C} + A\overline{B}C + AB\overline{C} + ABC
$$
$$
\text{Compact Minterm Notation: } F(A, B, C) = \sum m(3, 4, 5, 6, 7)
$$

#### Hardware Realization: Two-Level Logic
Every canonical SOP expression maps directly into a regular **two-level AND-OR circuit**:
- **Level 1**: A bank of AND gates, each decoding one specific minterm.
- **Level 2**: A single wide OR gate collecting all minterm outputs.
![](Pasted%20image%2020260924135639.png)
*Figure 8.2: Mapping a Sum of Products (SOP) expression into two-level AND-OR logic (Source: Slide 74).*

#### Canonical Form vs. Minimal Form
Canonical form is universally standard, but **it is almost never minimal**:

$$\begin{aligned}
F &= A\overline{B}\overline{C} + A\overline{B}C + AB\overline{C} + ABC + \overline{A}BC \\
  &= A\overline{B}(C + \overline{C}) + AB(C + \overline{C}) + \overline{A}BC \\
  &= A\overline{B}(1) + AB(1) + \overline{A}BC \\
  &= A(\overline{B} + B) + \overline{A}BC \\
  &= A(1) + \overline{A}BC \\
  &= A + \overline{A}BC \\
  &= A + BC \quad (\text{by Elimination Law})
\end{aligned}$$

- **Canonical SOP Implementation**: 5 three-input AND gates + 1 five-input OR gate.
- **Minimized Implementation ($A + BC$)**: 1 two-input AND gate + 1 two-input OR gate.
Both perform the identical function, but the minimized circuit consumes a fraction of the silicon area, latency, and power.

### 8.3 Canonical Product of Sums (POS) Form
The **Product of Sums (POS)** form (also known as *Conjunctive Normal Form* or *Maxterm Expansion*) represents a function as an **AND (Product)** of individual **OR (Sums / Maxterms)**.
![](Pasted%20image%2020260924135709.png)
*Figure 8.3: Constructing the Canonical Product of Sums (POS) form from truth table maxterms (Source: Slide 79).*

#### Systematic Construction Procedure
1. Identify all rows in the truth table where the output evaluates to **0** (the *OFF-set*).
2. For each such row, write its corresponding **maxterm**:
   - If an input variable is $0$, write it as an uncomplemented literal ($X$).
   - If an input variable is $1$, write it as a complemented literal ($\overline{X}$).
3. **AND ($\cdot$)** all generated maxterms together.

*(Notice the inversion of rule polarity: maxterms trigger on $0$ inputs and multiply together so that if any maxterm evaluates to $0$, the entire product is forced to $0$).*

#### Worked Example (Same Function)
In our earlier truth table, $F = 0$ at rows 0, 1, and 2:
- Row 0 ($000_2$): Maxterm $M_0 = (A + B + C)$
- Row 1 ($001_2$): Maxterm $M_1 = (A + B + \overline{C})$
- Row 2 ($010_2$): Maxterm $M_2 = (A + \overline{B} + C)$

$$\text{Canonical POS Form: } F(A, B, C) = (A + B + C)(A + B + \overline{C})(A + \overline{B} + C)$$

$$\text{Compact Maxterm Notation: } F(A, B, C) = \prod M(0, 1, 2)$$

> [!NOTE]
> **POS as De Morgan of $\overline{F}$**:  
> Writing the POS form of $F$ is mathematically identical to taking the Sum of Products of the inverted function $\overline{F}$ and applying De Morgan's Law:
> $$\overline{F} = m_0 + m_1 + m_2 \implies F = \overline{m_0 + m_1 + m_2} = \overline{m_0} \cdot \overline{m_1} \cdot \overline{m_2} = M_0 \cdot M_1 \cdot M_2$$

### 8.4 Minterm and Maxterm Conversions
Because the minterm indices where $F=1$ and the maxterm indices where $F=0$ partition the truth table, conversions are trivial:
1. **Minterm to Maxterm**: Replace the summation with product notation and list all indices not present in the minterm expansion:
   $$F(A,B,C) = \sum m(3, 4, 5, 6, 7) = \prod M(0, 1, 2)$$
2. **Complement of a Function**:
   $$\overline{F}(A,B,C) = \sum m(0, 1, 2) = \prod M(3, 4, 5, 6, 7)$$

# 9. Modular Combinational Building Blocks

Rather than designing large processors out of millions of discrete NAND and NOT gates, architects work with **modular building blocks**. These blocks hide lower-level circuit intricacies beneath clean functional abstractions.

### 9.1 The Decoder

#### Definition & Operation
A **Decoder** is an *input pattern detector*. An $N$-to-$2^N$ decoder has:
- $N$ binary inputs.
- $2^N$ binary outputs.
- **One-Hot Property**: For any given input bit combination, **exactly one output line is asserted HIGH (1)**, while all other $2^N - 1$ outputs remain LOW (0). The asserted output indicates the decimal equivalent of the applied binary input.
![](Pasted%20image%2020260924135740.png)
*Figure 9.1: Gate schematic and operation of a 2-to-4 Decoder (Source: Slide 91).*

#### 2-to-4 Decoder Implementation
- Inputs: $A, B$
- Outputs: $Y_0, Y_1, Y_2, Y_3$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Y_0 &= \overline{A}\,\overline{B} \quad (\text{asserts when } AB = 00_2) \\
Y_1 &= \overline{A}\,B \quad (\text{asserts when } AB = 01_2) \\
Y_2 &= A\,\overline{B} \quad (\text{asserts when } AB = 10_2) \\
Y_3 &= A\,B \quad (\text{asserts when } AB = 11_2)
\end{aligned}
$$

| $A$ | $B$ | $Y_0$ | $Y_1$ | $Y_2$ | $Y_3$ | Active Minterm |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| 0 | 0 | **1** | 0 | 0 | 0 | $m_0$ |
| 0 | 1 | 0 | **1** | 0 | 0 | $m_1$ |
| 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 | **1** | 0 | $m_2$ |
| 1 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 | **1** | $m_3$ |

#### Essential Computer Architecture Applications
1. **Memory Address Decoding**: In a memory chip, the CPU supplies a binary address (e.g., 32 bits). A decoder decodes the address to assert exactly one physical wordline, enabling reading or writing to a single row out of millions of DRAM cells.
2. **Instruction Opcode Decoding**: In a processor's control unit, a decoder inspects the opcode bits of an instruction (e.g., `000000` for ADD, `000100` for BEQ) and asserts a dedicated control wire to activate the appropriate hardware datapath unit.

#### Implementing Arbitrary Logic with Decoders + OR Gates
Since an $N$-to-$2^N$ decoder inherently generates every single minterm $m_0, \dots, m_{2^N-1}$ of its inputs, **any arbitrary Boolean function in SOP form can be implemented by connecting an OR gate to the appropriate decoder outputs**!
![](Pasted%20image%2020260924135836.png)
*Figure 9.2: Implementing an XNOR gate ($Y = \overline{A \oplus B} = m_0 + m_3$) using a 2:4 decoder and a single OR gate (Source: Slide 103).*

- For example, to implement an XNOR function ($Y = \overline{A \oplus B} = \overline{A}\,\overline{B} + AB = m_0 + m_3$), simply route outputs $Y_0$ and $Y_3$ of a 2-to-4 decoder into a 2-input OR gate!

### 9.2 The Multiplexer (MUX / Selector)

#### Definition & Operation
A **Multiplexer (MUX)** acts as a controllable multi-way data switch. It directs one of $N$ data inputs to a single output based on the binary numerical value applied to $k = \log_2 N$ control **select lines ($S$)**.

#### 1. The 2-to-1 Multiplexer
- **Inputs**: Data inputs $D_0, D_1$ (or $A, B$), Select input $S$.
- **Output**: $Y$ (or $C$).
![](Pasted%20image%2020260924135911.png)
*Figure 9.3: Gate-level realization and operational behavior of a 2-to-1 Multiplexer (Source: Slide 95).*
$$
\text{Boolean Equation: } Y = \overline{S} \cdot D_0 + S \cdot D_1
$$
- When $S = 0$: The upper AND gate passes $D_0$, while the lower AND gate outputs $0$. $Y = D_0 + 0 = D_0$.
- When $S = 1$: The upper AND gate outputs $0$, while the lower AND gate passes $D_1$. $Y = 0 + D_1 = D_1$.

| Select $S$ | Output $Y$ |
| :---: | :---: |
| **0** | $D_0$ |
| **1** | $D_1$ |

#### 2. The 4-to-1 Multiplexer
A 4-to-1 MUX selects among 4 data inputs ($D_0, D_1, D_2, D_3$) using 2 select lines ($S_1, S_0$).
![](Pasted%20image%2020260924140016.png)
*Figure 9.4: Two implementations of a 4-to-1 MUX: hierarchical tree of three 2:1 MUXes (left) and two-level gate-level design (right) (Source: Slide 97).*

- **Gate-Level Implementation**:
	$$
	Y = \overline{S_1}\,\overline{S_0}\,D_0 + \overline{S_1}\,S_0\,D_1 + S_1\,\overline{S_0}\,D_2 + S_1\,S_0\,D_3
	$$
  Implemented using 4 three-input AND gates and 1 four-input OR gate.
- **Hierarchical Modular Implementation**:
  Constructed as a **tree of 2-to-1 MUXes**:
  - Stage 1: One 2:1 MUX selects between $D_0$ and $D_1$ using $S_0$. A second 2:1 MUX selects between $D_2$ and $D_3$ using $S_0$.
  - Stage 2: A third 2:1 MUX selects between the Stage 1 outputs using $S_1$.

### 9.3 Multiplexers as Programmable Lookup Tables (LUTs)

Prof. Mutlu highlights an elegant, profound insight:  
**A multiplexer can be used as a universal hardware Lookup Table (LUT) to implement ANY arbitrary logic function without adding extra logic gates.**

#### The General LUT Principle
To implement an $N$-variable Boolean function:
1. Connect the function's $N$ input variables directly to the **Select lines** of an $2^N$-to-1 multiplexer.
2. Connect the **Data inputs** ($D_0, \dots, D_{2^N-1}$) to constant voltages ($V_{DD}$ for 1, $GND$ for 0) matching the desired truth table outputs for each row!

#### Examples of MUX Logic:
- **AND Gate using 4:1 MUX**: Route inputs $A, B$ to select lines $S_1, S_0$. Tie data inputs $D_0, D_1, D_2$ to $0$ ($GND$), and $D_3$ to $1$ ($V_{DD}$).
- **AND Gate using 2:1 MUX**: Route $A$ to select $S$. When $A=0$, $Y=0$ (tie $D_0$ to $GND$). When $A=1$, $Y=B$ (connect $D_1$ to $B$).
- **XOR Gate using 2:1 MUX**: Route $A$ to select $S$. When $A=0$, $Y=B$ (connect $D_0$ to $B$). When $A=1$, $Y=\overline{B}$ (connect $D_1$ to $\overline{B}$).

```
   Implementing AND with 2:1 MUX          Implementing XOR with 2:1 MUX
              A (Select)                             A (Select)
                  │                                      │
               ┌──┴──┐                                ┌──┴──┐
         0 ───►│ 0   │                          B ───►│ 0   │
               │   Y ├──► Y = AB                      │   Y ├──► Y = A ⊕ B
         B ───►│ 1   │                         ~B ───►│ 1   │
               └─────┘                                └─────┘
```

### 9.4 How FPGAs Work: The Lookup Table (LUT) Architecture
This lookup table concept is precisely how modern **Field-Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGAs)** achieve software-defined hardware reconfigurability!
![](Pasted%20image%2020260924140341.png)
*Figure 9.5: Organization of an 8-input Lookup Table (3-LUT) with 8 configuration memory bits and a 3-bit select multiplexer (Source: Slide 101).*
![](Pasted%20image%2020260924140408.png)
*Figure 9.6: Programming a 3-LUT to implement a 3-input majority function: C code vs. FPGA configuration memory contents (Source: Slide 102).*

- **FPGA Logic Elements**: An FPGA does not contain hardwired AND or OR gates for user logic. Instead, it contains millions of **$K$-input LUTs** (typically 3-LUT to 6-LUT).
- **Configuration Memory (SRAM)**: The data inputs of the multiplexer are driven by small static memory cells (SRAM).
- **Bitstream Loading**: When you "program" an FPGA, the synthesis tool generates a bitstream that writes 0s and 1s into these configuration memory cells.
- **Instantaneous Reprogrammability**: Changing the values stored in the SRAM cells instantly changes the hardware functionality of the chip without fabricating a single piece of silicon!

#### Case Study: 3-Input Majority Function in Software vs. Hardware
Consider a function that outputs 1 if at least two out of three inputs are 1:

**Software Implementation (C Language)**:
```c
// Iterative bit counting:
int count = 0;
for (int i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
    count += input & 1;
    input = input >> 1;
}
if (count > 1) return 1;
return 0;

// Or Switch-Case:
switch (input) {
    case 0: case 1: case 2: case 4:
        return 0;
    default:
        return 1;
}
```

**Hardware Implementation (FPGA 3-LUT)**:
The truth table outputs are stored directly in the 8-bit configuration memory vector:
$$
\text{Configuration Memory Bits: } [0, 0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1, 1]
$$
Whenever the 3-bit input pattern arrives at the multiplexer select lines, the MUX immediately routes the corresponding configuration bit to the output—executing the function in a fraction of a nanosecond.

## 10. Summary & Key Takeaways

1. **The Transistor as a Switch**:
   - Modern computing is built on MOS transistors abstractly modeled as binary on/off switches.
   - **nMOS** conducts when Gate = 1 ($V_{DD}$); strong pull-down to $0\text{V}$, weak pull-up.
   - **pMOS** conducts when Gate = 0 ($GND$); strong pull-up to $V_{DD}$, weak pull-down.
2. **Complementary CMOS Architecture**:
   - To achieve reliable rail-to-rail signaling, CMOS pairs a **pMOS Pull-Up Network** with an **nMOS Pull-Down Network**.
   - Exactly one network must conduct at steady state. Both ON causes destructive short circuits; both OFF causes high-impedance floating states ($Z$).
   - CMOS is an **inverting logic family**: NOT, NAND, and NOR are elementary; non-inverting gates (AND, OR) require an extra inverter stage.
3. **Physical Design Constraints**:
   - Series transistors introduce additive channel resistance, increasing propagation delay.
   - Dynamic power scales quadratically with voltage ($P \propto V^2 f \approx V^3$).
   - Static leakage power ($P_{\text{static}} = V_{DD} I_{\text{leakage}}$) requires active mitigation via power gating.
   - Energy ($E = P \times t$) dictates battery runtime; Peak Power (TDP) dictates cooling limits and leads to "dark silicon."
4. **Boolean Foundations & Canonical Forms**:
   - Boolean algebra axioms and duality enable systematic algebraic circuit simplification.
   - De Morgan's laws enable technology mapping between NAND-centric and NOR-centric circuits.
   - Any truth table can be expressed canonically in **Sum of Products (SOP)** form (AND-OR two-level logic for ON-set minterms) or **Product of Sums (POS)** form (OR-AND two-level logic for OFF-set maxterms).
5. **Modular Combinational Blocks**:
   - **Decoders** ($N \to 2^N$) detect specific input patterns; indispensable for memory address decoding and processor opcode decoding.
   - **Multiplexers** ($N \to 1$) act as data selectors and universal Lookup Tables (LUTs).
   - Modern **FPGAs** implement arbitrary digital logic by configuring SRAM memory cells feeding multiplexer trees.
