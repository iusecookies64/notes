*A Detailed Guide to Sampling, Estimation, and the Central Limit Theorem*

## 1. The Why and How of Sampling

Statistical inference is the art and science of drawing conclusions about a **population** using information obtained from a **sample**. Since studying an entire population is often impractical or impossible, sampling becomes the foundation upon which all inference is built. The quality of our conclusions depends critically on how the sample is collected.

---

## 1.1 Populations, Samples, Parameters, and Statistics

A **population** is the complete set of individuals or objects of interest. Examples include all students at BITS Pilani, all smartphones produced by a factory in a year, or all eligible voters in India.

A **parameter** is a numerical characteristic of a population. Parameters are fixed but usually unknown quantities. They are commonly denoted using Greek letters, such as:

* population mean: $\mu$
* population standard deviation: $\sigma$
* population proportion: $p$

A **sample** is a subset drawn from the population and is the data we actually observe and analyze.

A **statistic** is a numerical characteristic calculated from sample data. Statistics vary from sample to sample and are denoted using Roman letters, such as:

* sample mean: $\bar{x}$
* sample standard deviation: $s$
* sample proportion: $\hat{p}$

The central objective of statistical inference is to use a **known statistic** to estimate or infer the value of an **unknown parameter**. For this inference to be meaningful, the sampling method must be carefully designed.

---

## 1.2 Types of Sampling Methods

A good sample should be **representative** of the population. Probability-based sampling methods achieve this by giving each population member a known chance of selection.

**Simple Random Sampling (SRS):**
Every individual and every possible sample of size $n$ has an equal probability of being selected. This is the theoretical gold standard of sampling.

**Stratified Sampling:**
The population is divided into non-overlapping groups called strata based on a relevant characteristic (such as department or year of study). A simple random sample is then drawn from each stratum. This ensures that all important subgroups are represented.

**Cluster Sampling:**
The population is divided into clusters, often based on geography. A random sample of clusters is selected, and all individuals within those clusters are included. This method is often more practical and cost-effective than SRS.

**Systematic Sampling:**
After randomly selecting a starting point, every $k$-th individual in an ordered list is selected.

**Convenience Sampling:**
Individuals are selected based on ease of access. This method is highly susceptible to bias and is **not suitable** for valid statistical inference.

## 2. Point Estimation: The Best Single Guess

A **point estimate** is a single numerical value used as the best guess for an unknown population parameter, based on sample data. For example, the sample mean $\bar{x}$ is used as a point estimate for the population mean $\mu$.

---

## 2.1 Properties of Good Estimators

A good estimator should possess certain desirable properties.

**Unbiasedness:**
An estimator is unbiased if the mean of its sampling distribution equals the true parameter value. The sample mean $\bar{x}$ is an unbiased estimator of $\mu$.

**Efficiency:**
An estimator is efficient if it has a small variance in its sampling distribution. Greater efficiency implies that repeated samples tend to produce estimates closer to the true parameter.

---

## 2.2 The Inherent Limitation: Sampling Error

The major limitation of a point estimate is that it provides **no information about precision**. A single estimate is almost guaranteed to be slightly incorrect due to random sampling variability. To understand and quantify this uncertainty, we must study how the estimate behaves across many hypothetical samples.

---

## 3. The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

![](Pasted%20image%2020251218190743.png)

*Target-shooting analogy for estimators: the bullseye represents the true parameter, and each shot represents a point estimate from a different sample.*

The **Central Limit Theorem** is one of the most important results in statistics. It describes the behavior of the **sampling distribution of the sample mean**, regardless of the shape of the original population distribution.

### The Central Limit Theorem States:

Let $\bar{x}$ be the sample mean of a random sample of size $n$.

* **Center:**
  $$\mu_{\bar{x}} = \mu$$

* **Spread (Standard Error):**
  $$\sigma_{\bar{x}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

* **Shape:**
  The sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ is approximately normal for sufficiently large $n$.

A common rule of thumb is that $n \ge 30$ is large enough for the normal approximation to be valid in most practical situations.

## 4. Interval Estimation: A Range of Confidence

Point estimates alone are incomplete. Interval estimation addresses this limitation by providing a **range of plausible values** for an unknown parameter.

---

## 4.1 Structure of a Confidence Interval

A confidence interval for a population mean has the general form:

$$
\text{CI} = \bar{x} \pm \text{Margin of Error}
$$

or equivalently,

$$
\text{CI} = \bar{x} \pm (\text{Critical Value} \times \text{Standard Error})
$$

---

## 4.2 Case 1: When $\sigma$ Is Known (The Z-Interval)

When the population standard deviation $\sigma$ is known, the standard error is:

$$
\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

Critical values are obtained from the standard normal ($Z$) distribution. The confidence interval is:

$$
\bar{x} \pm Z_{\alpha/2}\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
$$

---

## 4.3 Case 2: When $\sigma$ Is Unknown (The T-Interval)

When $\sigma$ is unknown, it is replaced by the sample standard deviation $s$. The standard error becomes:

$$
\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

Critical values are obtained from the **t-distribution** with degrees of freedom:

$$
\text{df} = n - 1
$$

The t-distribution has heavier tails than the normal distribution, leading to wider confidence intervals, especially for small sample sizes.

---

## 4.4 The Correct Interpretation: Net Fishing

Imagine the true population mean $\mu$ as a stationary fish in a pond. A confidence interval is like a net cast into the pond. A 95% confidence level means that **in the long run, 95% of such nets will capture the fish**.

It does **not** mean there is a 95% probability that your specific interval contains $\mu$. The confidence lies in the **procedure**, not in any single realized interval.
