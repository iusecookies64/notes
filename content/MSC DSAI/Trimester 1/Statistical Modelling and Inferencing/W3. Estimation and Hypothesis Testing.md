## 1. From Point Estimates to Interval Estimates

In statistics, a common objective is to estimate an unknown population parameter, such as the population mean $\mu$. The most natural approach is to draw a random sample from the population and compute the sample mean $\bar{x}$. This value serves as a **point estimate** of $\mu$.

While intuitive, a point estimate suffers from a major limitation: it provides **no indication of its accuracy**. Because of **sampling error**—the natural variability that arises from using different samples—it is almost guaranteed that $\bar{x}$ will not be exactly equal to the true population mean $\mu$. If a different sample were drawn, a different value of $\bar{x}$ would likely be obtained.

To address this unavoidable uncertainty, statisticians use **interval estimates**, commonly known as **confidence intervals**. Rather than offering a single number, a confidence interval provides a **range of plausible values** that is likely to contain the true population parameter.

---

### 1.1 The Core Idea

An interval estimate is more realistic and informative because it explicitly acknowledges uncertainty. For example, stating that the average score lies between 72 and 78 is far more meaningful than claiming it is exactly 75. The interval communicates both an estimate and its precision.

---

## 2. Anatomy of a Confidence Interval

Every two-sided confidence interval for a population mean follows the same basic structure:

$$
\text{Confidence Interval} = \text{Point Estimate} \pm \text{Margin of Error}
$$

Each component plays a distinct role.

The **point estimate** is our best single guess of the parameter. For estimating a population mean, this is the sample mean $\bar{x}$, which forms the center of the interval.

The **margin of error**, denoted by $E$, determines how wide the interval is. It quantifies the uncertainty in the estimate at a chosen confidence level. A smaller margin of error indicates a more precise estimate.

The margin of error itself is the product of two quantities:

$$
\text{Margin of Error} = (\text{Critical Value}) \times (\text{Standard Error})
$$

The **critical value** depends on the chosen confidence level (commonly 90%, 95%, or 99%). Higher confidence requires a larger critical value.

The **standard error** is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of the estimator. For the sample mean, it measures the typical distance between $\bar{x}$ and $\mu$ across repeated samples.

---

## 3. The Ideal Case: Population Standard Deviation ($\sigma$) Is Known

In rare situations, the true population standard deviation $\sigma$ is known. When this happens, the sampling distribution of the sample mean is normal (or approximately normal for large samples), and we use the standard normal (Z) distribution to construct a confidence interval. This is called a **Z-interval**.

The standard error of the sample mean is:

$$
\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

Let $\alpha$ denote the significance level, where:

$$
\alpha = 1 - \text{confidence level}
$$

For a 95% confidence interval, $\alpha = 0.05$, and the critical value $Z_{\alpha/2}$ is chosen so that an area of $\alpha/2 = 0.025$ lies in each tail of the standard normal distribution. This gives:

$$
Z_{0.025} = 1.96
$$

The formula for a Z-interval for the population mean is therefore:

$$
\text{CI} = \bar{x} \pm Z_{\alpha/2}\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
$$

---

## 4. The Realistic Case: Population Standard Deviation ($\sigma$) Is Unknown

In almost all practical situations, the population standard deviation $\sigma$ is unknown. We must estimate it using the sample standard deviation $s$.

Replacing $\sigma$ with $s$ introduces additional uncertainty. To account for this, we no longer use the Z-distribution. Instead, we use the **Student's t-distribution**, and the resulting confidence interval is called a **T-interval**.

---

### 4.1 The t-Distribution

The t-distribution is a family of symmetric, bell-shaped curves centered at zero, similar to the normal distribution but with **fatter tails**. These heavier tails reflect the extra uncertainty that arises when $\sigma$ is estimated from the data.

The shape of the t-distribution depends on its **degrees of freedom**, denoted by $\text{df}$. For a one-sample mean, the degrees of freedom are:

$$
\text{df} = n - 1
$$

As the sample size $n$ increases, the degrees of freedom increase, and the t-distribution gradually approaches the standard normal distribution.

The formula for a T-interval for the population mean is:

$$
\text{CI} = \bar{x} \pm t_{\alpha/2,\text{df}}\left(\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
$$

Here, $t_{\alpha/2,\text{df}}$ is the critical value from the t-distribution with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom that leaves an area of $\alpha/2$ in the upper tail.

---

## 5. The Correct Interpretation of a Confidence Interval

The interpretation of confidence intervals is one of the most commonly misunderstood topics in statistics.

**Incorrect interpretation:**
“There is a 95% probability that the true population mean $\mu$ lies in the interval $[72, 78]$.”

This statement is wrong because $\mu$ is a fixed but unknown constant. It is either inside the interval or it is not; the probability is therefore 0 or 1.

**Correct interpretation:**
“I am 95% confident in the procedure used to construct this interval. If I were to repeat the sampling process many times, approximately 95% of the confidence intervals constructed in this way would contain the true population mean $\mu$.”

Confidence refers to the **long-run success rate of the method**, not to the probability associated with any single computed interval.

## Reading Material: Determining Sample Size

---

## 1. The Importance of Sample Size

Before conducting any survey, experiment, or data analysis, one of the most important design questions is:

**“How much data do we need?”**

The choice of sample size, denoted by $n$, is a central issue in statistical inference. It represents a fundamental trade-off between **precision** and **resources** such as time, cost, and effort.

If the sample size is **too small**, the resulting estimates will have a large margin of error. This lack of precision increases the risk of committing a **Type II error**, where a real effect or difference exists but goes undetected. Conclusions drawn from such a study are often unreliable.

If the sample size is **too large**, the estimates may be very precise, but the additional data collection may be unnecessary and wasteful. Beyond a certain point, increasing $n$ yields only marginal improvements in precision while significantly increasing cost.

The objective of sample size determination is therefore to find the **minimum sample size** that achieves a desired level of confidence and precision.

---

## 2. The Key Ingredients of Sample Size Calculation

To determine an appropriate sample size, three inputs must be specified in advance.

### Margin of Error ($E$)

The **margin of error** represents the maximum amount of estimation error we are willing to tolerate.

* For a population mean, it appears as a “plus or minus” quantity, such as $\pm 3$ kg.
* For a population proportion, it appears as a percentage range, such as $\pm 4$%.

A smaller margin of error implies greater precision, which in turn requires a larger sample size.

---

### Confidence Level ($1 - \alpha$)

The **confidence level** reflects how certain we want to be that our interval estimate captures the true population parameter. Common confidence levels include 90%, 95%, and 99%.

The confidence level determines the **critical value** used in calculations, such as $Z_{\alpha/2}$. Higher confidence levels require larger critical values and therefore larger sample sizes.

---

### Population Variability ($\sigma$ or $p$)

Population variability measures how spread out the data is.

* For estimating a mean, variability is represented by the population standard deviation $\sigma$.
* For estimating a proportion, variability is captured by the term $p(1 - p)$.

More variable populations require larger samples to accurately reflect their characteristics. This is often the most difficult quantity to estimate before data collection begins.

---

## 3. Sample Size for Estimating a Population Mean ($\mu$)

The formula for determining sample size when estimating a population mean comes directly from the margin of error of a Z-interval.

The margin of error is:

$$
E = Z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

Solving this equation algebraically for $n$ gives:

$$
n = \left(\frac{Z_{\alpha/2}\sigma}{E}\right)^2
$$

Since sample size must be a whole number, the computed value of $n$ is **always rounded up** to the next integer to ensure sufficient precision.

---

### 3.1 The Challenge: Estimating $\sigma$

A practical difficulty is that the formula requires $\sigma$ to be known *before* data is collected. Several strategies are commonly used to estimate it:

* **Pilot Study:** Conduct a small preliminary sample and use the sample standard deviation $s$ as an estimate of $\sigma$.
* **Previous Research:** Use standard deviation values reported in similar studies or historical datasets.
* **Range Rule of Thumb:** Approximate $\sigma$ as one-fourth of the expected data range:
  $$
  \sigma \approx \frac{\text{Maximum} - \text{Minimum}}{4}
  $$

While rough, this method can be useful when no better information is available.

---

## 4. Sample Size for Estimating a Population Proportion ($p$)

When estimating a population proportion, the required sample size is derived from the margin of error formula for proportions.

The sample size formula is:

$$
n = p(1 - p)\left(\frac{Z_{\alpha/2}}{E}\right)^2
$$

This formula shows that required sample size depends strongly on the variability term $p(1 - p)$.

---

### 4.1 The Challenge: Estimating $p$

Just like $\sigma$, the population proportion $p$ must be estimated in advance.

* If prior studies or pilot data are available, their estimate of $p$ can be used.
* If no prior information exists, we use the **most conservative choice**.

The expression $p(1 - p)$ reaches its maximum value when $p = 0.5$. Using this value guarantees the **largest possible sample size** for a given confidence level and margin of error.

Thus, when $p$ is completely unknown, the safe formula is:

$$
n = 0.25\left(\frac{Z_{\alpha/2}}{E}\right)^2
$$

This ensures that the sample will be sufficiently large regardless of the true proportion.

---

## Closing Insight

Determining sample size is not merely a mathematical exercise; it is a critical design decision that balances statistical reliability with practical constraints. By explicitly specifying precision, confidence, and variability, researchers can design studies that are both efficient and scientifically sound.
