### Lesson 1: Core Probability Concepts

**Reading 1: Revision of Basic Probability**

---

## 1. Introduction to Probability

Probability is a branch of mathematics that provides a numerical measure of uncertainty. It quantifies how likely an event is to occur or how strongly we believe that a particular statement is true, based on available information.

The probability of any event is always a number between 0 and 1:

* A probability of 0 indicates that the event is impossible.
* A probability of 1 indicates that the event is certain.
* Values between 0 and 1 represent varying degrees of likelihood.

In real-world applications—such as data science, machine learning, risk analysis, and statistics—probability forms the mathematical foundation for reasoning under uncertainty.

---

## 2. Sample Space and Events

### 2.1 Sample Space

A **random experiment** is a process whose outcome cannot be predicted with certainty in advance.
The **sample space**, denoted by $S$ or $\Omega$, is the set of *all possible outcomes* of a random experiment.

**Examples:**

* **Coin toss:**
  When a fair coin is tossed, the possible outcomes are Head (H) and Tail (T).
  $$ S = {H, T} $$

* **Rolling a six-sided die:**
  The possible outcomes are the numbers from 1 to 6.
  $$ S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} $$

The sample space must include every outcome that can possibly occur and exclude anything that cannot occur.

---

### 2.2 Events

An **event** is any subset of the sample space. In other words, an event consists of one or more outcomes of a random experiment.

If $S$ is the sample space, then an event $A$ satisfies:
$$ A \subseteq S $$

**Example (Rolling a Die):**

Let the sample space be:
$$ S = {1,2,3,4,5,6} $$

* Let $A = {2,4,6}$ represent the event of rolling an even number.
* Let $B = {5,6}$ represent the event of rolling a number greater than 4.

Both $A$ and $B$ are valid events because they are subsets of the sample space.

---

## 3. Axioms of Probability

The modern mathematical framework of probability is based on **Kolmogorov’s Axioms**. These axioms define probability in a rigorous and consistent way.

Let $S$ be a sample space and $A$ be any event in $S$.

### Axiom 1: Non-negativity

The probability of any event is always non-negative:
$$ P(A) \ge 0 $$

### Axiom 2: Unitarity (Normalization)

The probability that *some* outcome in the sample space occurs is 1:
$$ P(S) = 1 $$

### Axiom 3: Additivity

For a sequence of **mutually exclusive (disjoint)** events $A_1, A_2, \ldots$, where:
$$ A_i \cap A_j = \varnothing \quad \text{for } i \ne j $$

the probability of their union is the sum of their probabilities:
$$
P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i)
$$

For two mutually exclusive events $A$ and $B$, this simplifies to:
$$ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) $$

---

## 4. Basic Probability Rules

### 4.1 Complement of an Event

The **complement** of an event $A$, denoted by $A^c$ or $A'$, consists of all outcomes in the sample space that are *not* in $A$.

If $A$ occurs, $A^c$ does not occur, and vice versa.

The probability of the complement is:
$$ P(A^c) = 1 - P(A) $$

---

### 4.2 Union of Events (Addition Rule)

For any two events $A$ and $B$, the probability that *at least one* of them occurs is given by:
$$
P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)
$$

The subtraction term avoids double-counting outcomes that belong to both $A$ and $B$.

If $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive, then $P(A \cap B) = 0$, and the formula reduces to:
$$ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) $$

---

## 5. Conditional Probability and Independence

### 5.1 Conditional Probability

**Conditional probability** measures the probability of an event occurring, given that another event has already occurred.

The conditional probability of event $A$ given event $B$ is denoted by $P(A \mid B)$ and defined as:
$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \quad \text{provided } P(B) > 0
$$

This definition restricts the sample space to outcomes where $B$ has occurred.

**Example (Rolling a Die):**

* Let $A = {6}$ be the event of rolling a 6.
  $$ P(A) = \frac{1}{6} $$

* Let $B = {2,4,6}$ be the event of rolling an even number.
  $$ P(B) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2} $$

* The intersection is:
  $$ A \cap B = {6} \Rightarrow P(A \cap B) = \frac{1}{6} $$

Thus,
$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{1/6}{1/2} = \frac{1}{3}
$$

---

### 5.2 Independent Events

Two events $A$ and $B$ are said to be **independent** if the occurrence of one event does not affect the probability of the other.

Mathematically, $A$ and $B$ are independent if and only if:
$$
P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)
$$

For independent events, conditioning does not change probabilities:
$$
P(A \mid B) = P(A), \quad P(B \mid A) = P(B)
$$

---

## 6. Bayes’ Theorem

**Bayes’ Theorem** is a fundamental result that allows us to update probabilities when new information becomes available. It relates conditional probabilities in reverse order.

The theorem is stated as:
$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A) P(A)}{P(B)}
$$

Here:

* $P(A)$ is the **prior probability** of $A$,
* $P(B \mid A)$ is the **likelihood**,
* $P(A \mid B)$ is the **posterior probability** after observing $B$.

Bayes’ theorem is widely used in machine learning, statistics, medical diagnosis, and decision-making systems to revise beliefs based on evidence.

### Reading 2: Random Variables & Distributions

---

## 1. Bridging the Gap: Introducing Random Variables

In the previous reading, probability was discussed in terms of *outcomes* and *events*, such as “rolling a 4” or “drawing a King from a deck of cards.” While this description is suitable for defining probability theoretically, it is often not sufficient for data analysis and mathematical modeling. In practice, we are usually interested in **numerical quantities** derived from these outcomes rather than the outcomes themselves.

This is where **random variables** become essential.

A **random variable** acts as a bridge between the real-world outcomes of a random experiment and numerical values that can be analyzed mathematically. It assigns a number to each possible outcome of the experiment, allowing us to apply algebra, calculus, and statistics.

**Formal Definition:**
A random variable is a variable whose value is a numerical outcome of a random phenomenon. Random variables are typically denoted by capital letters such as $X$, $Y$, or $Z$.

---

### 1.1 An Analogy: The Vending Machine

Consider a vending machine that dispenses a random snack when you press a button.

* **Experiment:** Pressing the button.
* **Outcomes:** ${\text{Chips, Chocolate Bar, Pretzels}}$

The outcome itself is not numerical. However, suppose we define a random variable $X$ as the **number of calories** in the snack received.

* If the snack is Chips, then $X = 150$
* If the snack is a Chocolate Bar, then $X = 220$
* If the snack is Pretzels, then $X = 110$

Here, the random variable $X$ translates each possible outcome into a numerical value. This numerical representation allows us to compute averages, probabilities, and other statistical measures. Importantly, the randomness lies in the *experiment*, not in the variable itself—the variable merely records the numerical consequence of that randomness.

---

## 2. The Two Flavors of Random Variables

Random variables are broadly classified into two types based on the nature of the values they can take.

---

### 2.1 Discrete Random Variables

A **discrete random variable** takes values from a **countable** set. This means the possible values can be listed explicitly, even if the list is very large.

Typical examples include:

* The number obtained when rolling a die:
  $$ X \in {1,2,3,4,5,6} $$
* The number of heads in three coin tosses:
  $$ X \in {0,1,2,3} $$
* The number of defective bulbs in a box of 100:
  $$ X \in {0,1,2,\dots,100} $$

The defining feature of discrete random variables is that there are **gaps** between possible values. For instance, it is impossible to roll a value like 2.5 on a standard die.

---

### 2.2 Continuous Random Variables

A **continuous random variable** can take **any value within a continuous range**. These variables arise naturally from measurement rather than counting.

Examples include:

* The exact height of a student (e.g., 175.132… cm)
* The temperature in a room (e.g., 21.59… °C)
* The time taken by a computer to boot up (e.g., 32.7… seconds)

For continuous variables, there are no gaps between values. Between any two possible values, no matter how close, there exists another possible value. This infinite divisibility leads to important differences in how probabilities are defined and calculated.

---

## 3. Mapping It Out: Probability Distributions

Defining a random variable is only the first step. To fully describe its behavior, we must specify how likely each possible value is. This information is captured by a **probability distribution**.

A probability distribution provides a complete mapping between the values of a random variable and their associated probabilities. The form of this mapping depends on whether the random variable is discrete or continuous.

---

### 3.1 Discrete Random Variables: Probability Mass Function (PMF)

For a discrete random variable, the probability distribution is described using a **Probability Mass Function (PMF)**.

The PMF specifies the probability that the random variable $X$ takes exactly the value $x$:
$$
P(X = x)
$$

**Example: Fair Six-Sided Die**

If $X$ represents the number obtained when rolling a fair die, then:
$$
P(X = x) = \frac{1}{6}, \quad x \in {1,2,3,4,5,6}
$$

Every PMF must satisfy two fundamental conditions:

1. **Non-negativity:**
   $$
   0 \le P(X = x) \le 1
   $$

2. **Total probability equals 1:**
   $$
   \sum_x P(X = x) = 1
   $$

These conditions ensure that the PMF represents a valid probability model.

---

### 3.2 Continuous Random Variables: Probability Density Function (PDF)

For continuous random variables, probabilities are defined differently. Instead of a PMF, we use a **Probability Density Function (PDF)**, typically denoted by $f(x)$.

A crucial conceptual difference arises here:

**For a continuous random variable, the probability of taking any exact single value is zero.**

Mathematically,
$$
P(X = x) = 0
$$

This occurs because the set of possible values is infinite and uncountable. For example, the probability that a person’s height is *exactly* 175.0000… cm is effectively zero.

Instead, probabilities are assigned to **intervals**. The probability that $X$ lies between $a$ and $b$ is given by the area under the PDF curve over that interval:
$$
P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x),dx
$$

Every valid PDF must satisfy the following conditions:

1. **Non-negativity:**
   $$
   f(x) \ge 0 \quad \text{for all } x
   $$

2. **Total area equals 1:**
   $$
   \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x),dx = 1
   $$

The PDF itself does not give probabilities directly; instead, probabilities are obtained by integrating the PDF over a range of values.

---

## Closing Remark

Random variables and their probability distributions provide a powerful mathematical framework for modeling uncertainty in real-world systems. By converting random outcomes into numerical form and describing their likelihood through PMFs and PDFs, we gain the ability to analyze, predict, and make informed decisions—forming the backbone of statistics, data science, and machine learning.

### Reading 3: Common Probability Distributions

---

## 1. Why Use Standard Distributions?

So far, we have learned that a probability distribution describes how probabilities are assigned to the values of a random variable. While it is theoretically possible to define a new distribution for every situation, doing so is neither efficient nor necessary.

In practice, many real-world random phenomena follow **common, repeatable patterns**. Over time, statisticians have identified these patterns and given them standard names. These standard probability distributions act as reusable mathematical models for uncertainty.

Instead of reinventing the wheel, we simply identify which known distribution best fits the situation and determine its parameters.

---

### 1.1 An Analogy: The Baker’s Recipes

Think of probability distributions as a baker’s standard recipes. A baker does not write out the full instructions every time; they simply say, “Let’s make a chocolate cake.” The recipe is already known. Only the parameters—such as pan size or amount of chocolate—are adjusted.

In the same way, standard probability distributions are “recipes” for modeling randomness. Once we recognize the correct distribution, we only need to specify its parameters to fully describe the uncertainty.

---

## 2. Famous Discrete Distributions (The Counters)

Discrete distributions model random variables that arise from **counting** outcomes.

---

### 2.1 The **Bernoulli Distribution**: The Coin Flip

The Bernoulli distribution is the simplest probability distribution. It models a **single trial** with exactly two possible outcomes, typically called *success* and *failure*.

Examples include:

* A single coin flip (Heads vs. Tails)
* A product being defective or not defective
* A student passing or failing an exam

The distribution has one parameter:

* $p$: the probability of success

The random variable $X$ takes values:
$$
X \in {0, 1}
$$

with probabilities:
$$
P(X = 1) = p
$$
$$
P(X = 0) = 1 - p
$$

---

### 2.2 The **Binomial Distribution**: Many Coin Flips

The Binomial distribution extends the Bernoulli distribution to **multiple independent trials**. It models the number of successes in a fixed number of identical Bernoulli trials.

Examples include:

* The number of heads in 10 coin flips
* The number of defective items in a batch of 50
* The number of students who pass out of 100 students

The Binomial distribution has two parameters:

* $n$: the number of trials
* $p$: the probability of success in each trial

The probability that exactly $k$ successes occur is given by:
$$
P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n-k}
$$

This distribution is appropriate when:

* The number of trials is fixed
* Each trial is independent
* The probability of success remains constant

---

### 2.3 The **Poisson Distribution**: Rare Events in Time or Space

The Poisson distribution models the **number of events occurring in a fixed interval** of time, space, or volume, when events happen independently and at a constant average rate.

Typical examples include:

* The number of emails received in an hour
* The number of customer arrivals at a store per minute
* The number of printing errors on a page
* The number of earthquakes in a region per year

The distribution has a single parameter:

* $\lambda$ (lambda): the average number of events in the interval

The probability of observing exactly $k$ events is:
$$
P(X = k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}, \quad k = 0,1,2,\dots
$$

Key characteristics:

* It models **counts**, not measurements
* It is especially useful for **rare events**
* The mean and variance are both equal to $\lambda$

The Poisson distribution can also be seen as an approximation to the Binomial distribution when $n$ is large and $p$ is small.

---

## 3. Famous Continuous Distributions (The Measurers)

Continuous distributions model random variables that arise from **measurement**, where values lie on a continuous scale.

---

### 3.1 The **Uniform Distribution**: The Perfect Guess

The Uniform distribution models situations where **every value within a given range is equally likely**.

Examples include:

* A random number chosen between 0 and 1
* Waiting time for a bus that arrives exactly every 15 minutes, assuming random arrival

If $X$ is uniformly distributed between $a$ and $b$, its PDF is:
$$
f(x) =
\begin{cases}
\frac{1}{b-a}, & a \le x \le b \
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$

The graph of the PDF is a flat rectangle, indicating constant probability density across the interval.

---

### 3.2 The **Normal Distribution**: The Bell Curve

![Image](https://www.simplypsychology.org/wp-content/uploads/normal-distribution-1024x640.jpeg?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The Normal distribution, also known as the Gaussian distribution, is the most important and widely used continuous distribution in statistics.

Many natural and human-made phenomena approximately follow this distribution, including:

* Heights and weights of people
* Blood pressure levels
* Measurement errors
* Noise in sensors and instruments

The Normal distribution is symmetric and bell-shaped, centered around its mean. It is defined by two parameters:

* $\mu$ (mu): the mean, which determines the center of the distribution
* $\sigma$ (sigma): the standard deviation, which controls the spread

Its probability density function is:
$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
$$

A smaller $\sigma$ produces a narrow, tall curve, while a larger $\sigma$ produces a wider, flatter curve.

---

### The Empirical Rule (68–95–99.7 Rule)

One of the most useful properties of the Normal distribution is the Empirical Rule:

* Approximately **68%** of observations lie within $\mu \pm \sigma$
* Approximately **95%** lie within $\mu \pm 2\sigma$
* Approximately **99.7%** lie within $\mu \pm 3\sigma$

This rule provides a quick way to estimate probabilities without performing calculations.

---

## Closing Remark

Choosing the correct probability distribution is a fundamental skill in data analysis. It involves understanding the story behind the data—whether you are counting events or measuring quantities—and matching that story to the appropriate mathematical “recipe.” Once the right distribution is chosen, uncertainty becomes something that can be quantified, analyzed, and reasoned about systematically.

## Lesson 2: Data Analysis & Decision Theory

**Reading 1: An Introduction to Decision Theory**

---

### 1. The Art and Science of Choosing

Human life is filled with decisions. Some are routine and inconsequential, such as choosing what to eat for breakfast, while others are complex and life-altering, such as choosing a career, an investment strategy, or a medical treatment. What makes many decisions difficult is **uncertainty**—we rarely know for sure how the future will unfold.

**Decision Theory** is the formal study of how rational decisions should be made when outcomes depend on uncertain future events. It provides a structured, logical, and often mathematical framework for evaluating choices and selecting the best possible action. Rather than relying on intuition alone, decision theory emphasizes reasoning, data, and clearly stated assumptions.

---

## 1.1 An Analogy: The Picnic Dilemma

Suppose you are planning a picnic for tomorrow. You must choose between having it outdoors in a park or indoors at home. Your enjoyment of the picnic depends on the weather, which you cannot control.

This simple situation already captures the essence of a decision problem: a choice must be made, the outcome depends on uncertain future conditions, and different outcomes lead to different levels of satisfaction.

---

## 2. The Three Pillars of a Decision Problem

Every decision problem can be decomposed into three essential components.

---

### Decision Alternatives (Actions)

Decision alternatives are the actions that are **under your control**. They represent the set of all possible choices available to you.

In the picnic example, the set of decision alternatives is:

$$
{\text{Picnic Outdoors}, \text{Picnic Indoors}}
$$

A decision problem must clearly specify all available actions.

---

### States of Nature

States of nature are future events or conditions that affect the outcome of your decision but are **outside your control**. These states must satisfy two conditions:

* They are **mutually exclusive** (only one can occur).
* They are **collectively exhaustive** (one of them must occur).

For the picnic scenario, the states of nature are:

$$
{\text{Sunny}, \text{Rain}}
$$

You cannot choose the weather, but it strongly influences the outcome of your decision.

---

### Payoffs

A **payoff** represents the outcome that results from choosing a particular action when a specific state of nature occurs. Payoffs may be measured in profit, cost, time, or subjective quantities such as satisfaction or enjoyment.

For example:

* If you choose *Picnic Outdoors* and it is sunny, the payoff might be high (say, 10).
* If you choose *Picnic Outdoors* and it rains, the payoff might be low (say, 2).

Payoffs convert outcomes into numerical values, allowing comparison and analysis.

---

## 3. Organizing the Problem: The Payoff Table

A decision problem is commonly summarized using a **payoff table**, where rows represent decision alternatives and columns represent states of nature.

| Decision Alternative | Sunny | Rain |
| -------------------- | ----- | ---- |
| Picnic Outdoors      | 10    | 2    |
| Picnic Indoors       | 6     | 7    |

Each cell contains the payoff corresponding to a specific action–state combination. This table provides a complete representation of the decision problem.

---

## 4. Decision Environments: How Much Do You Know?

The appropriate decision strategy depends on how much information is available about the states of nature.

---

### Decision Making under Certainty

In this case, the decision-maker knows with certainty which state of nature will occur.

If it is known for sure that the weather will be sunny, the decision is straightforward: compare the payoffs in the *Sunny* column and choose the action with the highest payoff. Here, *Picnic Outdoors* is optimal with a payoff of 10.

---

### Decision Making under Risk

Under risk, the decision-maker does not know which state will occur but **can assign probabilities** to each state.

For example, if the probability of rain is $0.3$ and the probability of sunshine is $0.7$, these probabilities can be combined with the payoff table to compute the **expected value** of each action. The action with the highest expected payoff is then chosen.

This setting directly connects decision theory with probability theory developed in Lesson 1.

---

### Decision Making under Uncertainty

Under uncertainty, the decision-maker neither knows which state will occur nor can assign reliable probabilities to the states of nature.

In such cases, decisions are made using alternative criteria that reflect the decision-maker’s attitude toward uncertainty, such as pessimism, optimism, or compromise strategies.

---

## Closing Perspective

Decision theory provides a systematic way to analyze choices in uncertain environments. By clearly identifying decision alternatives, states of nature, and payoffs—and by understanding how much information is available about the future—we can move from intuition-based choices to structured, rational decision-making. This framework lays the foundation for advanced topics such as expected value, utility theory, and Bayesian decision analysis.

### Lesson 2: Data Analysis & Decision Theory

**Reading 2: Parametric vs. Non-Parametric Methods**

---

## 1. The Analyst’s Fork in the Road

When analyzing data, the ultimate objective is usually **inference**—drawing conclusions about a population based on a sample. Statistical tests help us do this, but before choosing a specific test, we must make a fundamental methodological decision:

**Do we assume a particular form for the population distribution, or do we avoid such assumptions altogether?**

This question divides statistical methods into two broad categories:

* **Parametric methods**
* **Non-parametric methods**

The difference between them lies not in what they analyze, but in **what they assume** about the data-generating process.

---

### 1.1 An Analogy: The Auto Mechanic

Imagine taking your car to two different mechanics.

The **parametric mechanic** assumes your car is a very common model, say a Toyota Camry. Based on experience, they say, “For this model, 90% of the time the problem is the spark plugs,” and immediately test them. If the assumption is correct, the diagnosis is fast and efficient. However, if the car turns out to be a rare vintage model, this assumption can lead to a completely wrong conclusion.

The **non-parametric mechanic**, by contrast, makes no such assumptions. They systematically inspect the engine, electrical system, and fuel line. This approach works for any type of car, but it may be slower and less direct when the problem is something common and obvious.

This analogy captures the trade-off between parametric and non-parametric methods: **efficiency versus robustness**.

---

## 2. Parametric Methods: The Power of Assumption

Parametric statistical methods are based on the assumption that the data comes from a population following a specific probability distribution, most commonly the **Normal Distribution**.

### Core Idea

The underlying idea is that the population distribution has a known *shape*, and the analysis focuses on estimating a small number of **parameters** that define that shape. For example, a normal distribution is fully characterized by:

* the mean $,\mu,$
* the standard deviation $,\sigma,$

Once these parameters are estimated from the sample, statistical inference becomes mathematically precise and powerful.

---

### Common Examples

Some widely used parametric tests include:

* **t-test**: compares the means of two groups
* **ANOVA** (Analysis of Variance): compares the means of more than two groups

These tests rely heavily on assumptions such as normality, equal variances, and independence of observations.

---

### Advantages of Parametric Methods

When their assumptions are satisfied, parametric methods offer:

* **Higher statistical power**, meaning they are more likely to detect a true effect when one exists
* **Simpler interpretation**, since results are usually expressed in terms of means, differences of means, and standard deviations

They are the “sharp tools” of statistical analysis.

---

### Limitations of Parametric Methods

The main weakness of parametric methods is their reliance on assumptions. If the data significantly deviates from the assumed distribution—due to skewness, outliers, or small sample size—the results can become misleading. In such cases, elegant mathematics cannot compensate for poor model fit.

---

## 3. Non-Parametric Methods: The Flexible Approach

Non-parametric methods are often described as **distribution-free**. They do not require the data to follow any specific probability distribution.

### Core Idea

Rather than working directly with the numerical values, non-parametric methods frequently operate on **ranks**. The smallest observation is assigned rank 1, the next smallest rank 2, and so on. By doing this, the analysis becomes largely independent of the underlying distribution’s shape.

This transformation sacrifices some numerical information but gains robustness.

---

### Common Examples

Well-known non-parametric tests include:

* **Wilcoxon rank-sum test**: a non-parametric alternative to the t-test
* **Kruskal-Wallis test**: a non-parametric alternative to ANOVA

These tests compare distributions using ranks rather than raw data values.

---

### Advantages of Non-Parametric Methods

Non-parametric methods are:

* **Robust to outliers** and skewed data
* **Versatile**, working well with ordinal data such as survey ratings (“Strongly Disagree” to “Strongly Agree”)
* Reliable when assumptions required by parametric tests are violated

---

### Limitations of Non-Parametric Methods

The main drawback is that non-parametric methods can be **less powerful** when the data actually follows a normal distribution. By converting data to ranks, some information is lost, making it harder to detect subtle effects.

---

## 4. Which One Should You Choose?

There is no universally “better” approach; the choice depends on the data and context.

### Choose Parametric Methods When:

* The sample size is large
* Assumptions appear reasonable (checked using plots or diagnostics)
* The **Central Limit Theorem** helps justify approximate normality of sample means

In such cases, parametric methods offer efficiency and power.

---

### Choose Non-Parametric Methods When:

* The sample size is small
* The data is heavily skewed or contains significant outliers
* The data is ordinal rather than truly numerical
* You are unsure whether parametric assumptions hold

In uncertain situations, non-parametric methods provide a safer, more conservative choice.

---

## Closing Insight

The decision between parametric and non-parametric methods mirrors a broader theme in data analysis: balancing **assumptions** against **robustness**. Strong assumptions can yield powerful conclusions when justified, but flexibility becomes essential when data refuses to behave ideally. A skilled analyst understands both approaches and knows when to apply each.
