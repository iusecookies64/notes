### **Why do we need to visualize data?**

- We need to visualize the data as almost 70% humans are visual learners i.e. they learn by seeing things rather than reading or listening to them.

- We retain almost 80% of the information that we learn through visualization as compared to only 20% (**fact check needed here**) when we are reading it.

- Our brain processes visual information much faster than other senses like hearing.

For look at the image below comparing voter turn out ratio of Lok Sabha Election 2024 vs 2019.

![[Pasted image 20250920132708.png]]

This information by itself is flat, un-interesting and the audience will most likely forget much of this. But if we add a chart to accompany the text, this levels up the report quite a bit and the audience is more likely to retain the key information from this report through the chart.

![[Pasted image 20250920133057.png]]

As visual information is easily perceptible to humans it helps us to quickly find hidden patterns and trends in the data which otherwise would be hard to find just by looking raw text data.

It helps improve our understanding of the data which aids in better decision making.

Lastly it helps us in presenting arguments or telling a story.

>[!Quote]
>There are two goals when presenting data: convey our story and establish credibility.
> \- ***Edward Tufte (Father of data visualization)***

### **Gestalt Principles**

These principles were developed in Germany by studying human psychology and how human brain perceives visual information. The core idea in these principles is that 

>***the whole is more than the sum of it's parts***

When humans see something (which more often then not is a collection of parts) we tend to see it as a whole and give little importance to individual parts. For example when we look as a persons face we often see it as a whole i.e. a combination of individual features like eyes, nose etc.

The **Gestalt Psychologists** use a set of principles to describe how our brain tend to group and interpret visual information. These principles guide us how to present visual images for effective information delivery to the human mind.

##### 1. Principle of Figure-Ground

We tend to separate the main object (the figure) from it's background (the ground). We can use colors, depth and contrast to guide the brain to automatically focus of something as main figure and leave every thing else as background.

![[Gemini_Generated_Image_7ueg2s7ueg2s7ueg.png]]

##### 2. Principle of Similarity

We tend to group similar items together, for example in the image below we can see that there are circles of two distinct colors. Our brain automatically considers circles with similar color as a group. We can also see this principle in action in the voter turnout chart in first section where states with voter turn out higher than national average were colored differently than states with lower voter turn out.

<img src="Pasted image 20250920135026.png" style="display:block; margin: 0 auto;" />

##### 3. Principle of Continuity

It states that the human mind detects patterns in continuous forms, regardless of their shape or color. For example look at the chart below, it is hard to get much information from the left one, but from the right graph which is sorted by voter turn out ratio it is easy to understand and interpret. Hence, whenever there is natural ordering in our data, we must use to better visualize the data.

![[Pasted image 20250920141118.png]]

##### 4. Principle of Focal Point

Whenever there is similar background and we highlight a particular point, then our mind will automatically focus on the highlighted point. For example in the image below our focus directly goes to the highlighted point. We use this principle to highlight the most important data.

<img src="Pasted image 20250920135520.png" style="display:block; margin: 0 auto;" />

>[!Caution]
>We must only use this when there are 1 or 2 important data points that we want to highlight, otherwise the audience might get confused.

##### 5. Principle of Closure

The **principle of closure** states that people will fill in blanks to perceive a complete object whenever an external stimulus partially matches that object. Even if a shape or image isn't fully drawn, our brain will fill in the missing parts to see a complete, familiar whole. It prefers to see a simple, closed shape rather than a series of broken lines or incomplete figures.

Humans groups things that are closer to each other in space without we needing to explicitly mention this. For example in the chart below the states are grouped by regions and this grouping is clearly perceptible just by looking the chart.

### The 7 Stages of Data Visualization

Before we start a data visualization exercise, we need to do some preliminary ground work to make sure that the visualization that we make effectively communicates the message to the audience.

The established principles of visualization have identified  7 steps in this process.

![[Pasted image 20250921133904.png#center]]

##### 1. Acquire: Getting the Raw Materials

The very first step is to **acquire** the data. This means collecting all the necessary information from a reliable source. Think of this as gathering your ingredients before you start cooking. The quality of your visualization depends heavily on the quality of the data you start with.

- **Example:** A health researcher wants to visualize the link between exercise and sleep. They _acquire_ data by downloading public health surveys and collecting sleep tracker information from a study group.

##### 2. Parse: Understanding Your Data's Structure

Once you have the data, you need to **parse** it. This involves giving it a first look to understand its structure and format. You'll check what kind of information each column contains (e.g., numbers, text, dates) and get a general feel for what you're working with. This step is about making sure the data is organized and ready for analysis.

- **Example:** You open a spreadsheet of customer sales. You _parse_ it by noticing that one column is for the customer's name (text), another for the purchase date (date format), and a third for the amount spent (a number).

##### 3. Filter: Removing the Noise

Not all data you collect will be relevant to your goal. **Filtering** is the process of cleaning up your dataset by removing anything you don't need. This helps you focus only on the information that is important for the story you want to tell.

- **Example:** Your sales data includes information from the last ten years, but you only want to show this year's trends. You _filter_ out all the records from previous years to narrow your focus.

##### 4. Mine: Digging for Insights

This is where the real analysis happens. To **mine** data means to explore it deeply to uncover hidden patterns, trends, or important relationships that aren't obvious on the surface. You're essentially digging for "gold" in the form of valuable insights.

- **Example:** By _mining_ customer data, a coffee shop owner discovers that sales of iced lattes spike on days when the temperature rises above 25°C. This is a valuable pattern they can act on.

##### 5. Represent: Choosing the Right Visual

In this step, you **represent** your findings in a visual form. The goal is to choose a basic chart or graph that accurately shows the patterns you discovered during the mining stage. This is your first draft of the visualization.

- **Example:** To show the sharp increase in iced latte sales on hot days, the coffee shop owner creates a simple line graph that plots sales numbers against the daily temperature. This _representation_ clearly shows the trend.

##### 6. Refine: Making it Clear and Beautiful

A first draft is rarely perfect. The **refine** stage is all about improving your initial visualization to make it clearer and more impactful. This can involve changing colors, adding labels, adjusting the layout, or simplifying the design so the main message stands out.

- **Example:** The initial line graph is functional but boring. The owner _refines_ it by giving it a clear title like "Hot Weather Drives Iced Latte Sales," highlighting the trend line in a bold color, and labeling the axes clearly.

##### 7. Interact: Sharing and Gathering Feedback

Finally, you **interact** with your audience by presenting your visualization. This stage is about communicating your findings and allowing others to engage with the data. Their questions and feedback are crucial, as they might lead you to go back and make further refinements to tell an even better story.

- **Example:** The owner shows the refined graph to their staff. A barista asks if this trend is also true for other cold drinks. This _interaction_ prompts the owner to go back and create a new visualization comparing sales of all cold drinks, leading to a richer understanding.

Hence the steps are not always sequential, sometimes they are sequential, sometimes they run parallelly and most of the time it is iterative.

### Various Types of Charts

The choice of chart we use to showcase our findings in a data mining step is very important to make sure the audience can easily understand the message. The choice of chart depends on the story we want to tell and the type of variables involved in the story. Below we discuss a few of the most common types of charts and the situations they are best suited for.

##### 1. Bar Chart

A bar chart is perfect for **comparing categorical data**. Each bar represents a distinct category, and the length of the bar corresponds to a numerical value. This makes it easy to see which category is biggest or smallest at a glance. They can be oriented vertically or horizontally.

- **Example**: A chart showing the total sales for different product categories (e.g., Electronics, Apparel, Groceries) in a quarter. The categories are on the x-axis, and the sales figures are on the y-axis.

![[Pasted image 20250921181821.png]]

There are several useful variations of the bar chart:

- **Grouped (or Clustered) Bar Chart**: This variant is used to compare sub-categories within the main categories. Bars for the sub-categories are placed side-by-side.
    
    - **Use Case**: Comparing the sales of two different years (e.g., 2024 vs. 2025) for each product category.
        
    - **Example**: For the 'Electronics' category, you would have two bars next to each other: one for 2024 sales and one for 2025 sales. This is repeated for 'Apparel' and 'Groceries'.

![[grouped_bar_chart.png]]

- **Stacked Bar Chart**: This variant is used to show how the main categories are divided into different parts. Instead of being side-by-side, the sub-categories are stacked on top of each other to form a single bar.
    
    - **Use Case**: Showing the total sales for each product category while also displaying the contribution of different regions (e.g., North, South, West) to that total.
        
    - **Example**: The bar for 'Electronics' would be a single bar with its total height representing total sales. This bar would be segmented into different colors, with each colored segment showing the sales from the North, South, and West regions.

![[stacked_bar_chart.png]]

##### 2. Line Chart

A line chart is primarily used to show **trends over a continuous period of time**. It connects a series of data points with a line, making it excellent for visualizing how a value changes.

- **Example**: Tracking a company's monthly revenue over a year. The x-axis would represent the months (January to December), and the y-axis would represent the revenue amount. The connecting line would clearly show if revenue is trending up, down, or staying constant.

![[line_chart.png]]

##### 3. Scatter Plot

A scatter plot is used to observe and show the **relationship between two numerical variables**. Each dot on the plot represents an observation with coordinates corresponding to the values of the two variables. It's great for spotting correlations or patterns.

- **Example**: Plotting students' "hours of study" on the x-axis against their "exam scores" on the y-axis. If the dots trend upwards from left to right, it suggests a positive correlation (more study hours lead to higher scores).

![[scatter_chart.png]]

##### 4. Histogram

A histogram is used to show the **frequency distribution of a single numerical variable**. It looks similar to a bar chart, but there's a key difference: a histogram groups numbers into ranges (called bins), and the height of the bar shows the frequency of data points falling into that range. There are no gaps between the bars, indicating a continuous range of data.

- **Example**: Visualizing the distribution of heights of students in a school. The x-axis would have height ranges (e.g., 150-155 cm, 155-160 cm, 160-165 cm), and the y-axis would show the number of students whose height falls within each range.

![[histogram_chart.png]]

##### 5. Bubble Chart

A bubble chart is a variation of a scatter plot that adds a **third dimension** to the data. While the x and y axes represent two different variables (just like a scatter plot), the size of the bubble (the dot) represents a third numerical variable.

- **Example**: Extending the scatter plot example, you could plot "hours of study" (x-axis) vs. "exam scores" (y-axis), and the size of each bubble could represent the "number of classes attended" by each student. This allows you to visualize three variables at once.

![[bubble_chart.png]]

##### 6. Choropleth Map

A choropleth map is a geographical map where different areas (like states, countries, or districts) are shaded or colored to represent the value of a particular variable. It's excellent for visualizing **geographical data**.

- **Example**: A map of India where each state is colored based on its population density. States with higher population density might be shaded with a darker color, while those with lower density would have a lighter shade, making regional differences immediately obvious.

![[newplot.png]]

##### 7. Composition Charts

Composition charts are used to show how a whole is divided into different parts. Their primary goal is to display the **part-to-whole relationship**, illustrating the relative proportion or percentage that each category contributes to the total.

- **Use Case**: These charts are perfect when you want to answer questions like, "What percentage of our total sales comes from each product category?" or "What is the market share distribution among competitors?"

There are several common types of composition charts:

- **Pie Chart / Donut Chart**: These are the most classic composition charts. A circle (or "donut") is divided into slices, where the size of each slice is proportional to its percentage of the whole. They are best used with a small number of categories (ideally less than 6) to avoid clutter.
    
    - **Example**: A pie chart showing the percentage breakdown of a company's total Q1 revenue by product category: Electronics (40%), Apparel (35%), and Groceries (25%).

![[pie_chart.png]]

Very similar to pie chart is a **Donut Chart**.

- **100% Stacked Bar Chart**: This is a variation of the stacked bar chart where each bar adds up to 100%. Instead of showing absolute values, it shows the relative percentage of each sub-category within its main category. This is excellent for comparing the composition across multiple groups.
    
    - **Example**: Two bars, one for 2024 and one for 2025, both showing the regional sales breakdown. The chart would clearly show if the North region's _share_ of total sales grew from 30% in 2024 to 40% in 2025, even if total sales changed.

>[!Note]
>Few points we need to keep in mind while making charts.
>- Choose appropriate scale for numerical axes so that the data could be represented in reasonable space.
>- Add appropriate labels to axes, legends and a chart title.
>- Don't add too much information on a single chart.
